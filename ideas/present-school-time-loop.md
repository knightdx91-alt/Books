# PRESENT
### *A horror novel — the school that never reaches tomorrow*

**Working title:** *Present* — the answer to roll call, and the tense nobody in the building
can get out of.
**Alternates:** *Attendance* · *Nobody Is Absent* · *The Long Thursday* · *Substitute*

**Status:** premise development. Seeded by the author's dream, 2026-09-13. Not yet scaffolded
as a book folder. To start one: `bash tools/new-book.sh present "Present"`.

---

## 1. The dream (author's words, preserved)

> It is a school that does the same thing like in a time loop everyday. A teacher tries to
> replace a teacher that had a child, but there is a twist. It is like an unbreakable spell
> on everyone and nothing changes in the school or the people in it every day. They go home
> and go back, but it is like the same day every day.
>
> The twist is everyone is dead. The adults are vampires but never feed or do anything but
> the same day. The kids in the school are dead but don't know it — they still act normal.
>
> The human woman who tries to get in the school by replacing a teacher later has to leave,
> as her reason for being there is to replace a teacher who is out for the day — but she
> shows up like normal, and it becomes a big thing as people start trying to figure out what
> is going on in the school and with the people who seem to have stopped living normal lives
> outside.
>
> She was shocked when the woman appeared, when she had sent the woman she replaced away to
> find her son that was alive outside the place.

*Author notes: woke before the protagonist found much out; no ending. Felt structurally
different from most dreams — it arrived with a twist already in it.*

---

## 2. One-line premise

A substitute teacher takes a one-day assignment at a school where every child answers roll,
every morning, forever — and discovers that the day is a ritual held open by the dead, and
that the roll sheet in her hand is the only thing in the building capable of ending it.

---

## 3. The engine

The spine is the dream's own last image: **she sends the replaced teacher away to find her
living son, and the next morning that teacher is back at her desk with her roll sheet, no
memory of having left.**

From that, the core rule:

> **The loop does not repeat time. It repeats people.**

Going home is part of the performance. The loop extends exactly as far as it must to stay
convincing, and not one inch further. The road out of town bends back.

---

## 4. Why a substitute is the one who gets in

A substitute is the only living person the day can admit. A permanent hire would require a
tomorrow. **A substitute exists for one day only — she is precisely the shape of the hole.**

The loop lets her in, and then cannot heal over her.

---

## 5. The twist under the twist

Vampires who never feed. Children who don't know they're dead. Asking *why* produces the real
story:

> **The loop is an act of love, and that is what makes it unforgivable.**

The faculty were already what they are. Something killed the children — fire, gas, a bus, a
flood; choose late and keep it mundane. The adults could not save them, so they built a single
day and refused to let it end. Feeding requires a tomorrow, so the abstinence is not virtue;
it is the price of the ritual. Nobody grows, nobody graduates, nobody is buried.

They are not jailers. They are parents who would not sign the paperwork.

Which reframes the protagonist entirely: she is not escaping monsters. She is the woman who
came to switch off the machine keeping someone's child "alive."

---

## 6. What is the loop FOR?

The premise's hardest joint. What does eighty years of the same day accomplish?

### First answer: nothing, and that is the point

Nobody decided to do this for eighty years. They decided *not today*, and then again. A loop
is not a plan — it is a refusal, repeated until it sets. There is no architect to confront and
no bargain to strike. In horror this is usually stronger than any invented purpose.

### Second answer, and the recommended spine: the goal is the roll call

Every morning every name is read and every child answers *present*. That is the ritual.
Everything else — lessons, the lunch nobody eats, going home, coming back — is scaffolding
built to make the roll call happen again tomorrow.

Dead children do not answer roll. So as long as every name gets an answer, nobody is dead.
Grief in the most school-shaped form available: **the paperwork says they are here, therefore
they are here.**

The goal can never be completed, only re-achieved — daily, forever, like a prayer. A goal that
went somewhere would end. This one cannot. *That is what a loop is.*

It retro-explains every element already in the dream:

| Element from the dream | What the roll call explains |
|---|---|
| Why a school | Nowhere else does an adult read your name aloud daily and require you to prove you exist |
| Nobody is ever absent | Not a spooky detail — the load-bearing rule |
| The empty desk | A name with no answer is the crack in the dam |
| Vampires who never feed | Appetite means wanting something *next*; feeding admits tomorrow exists |
| A substitute breaks it | She is the one person who does not know the answers |

### Alternatives, if the loop should have a true purpose

- **Containment.** The children are not merely dead, they are dead *wrong*. End the day and
  they do not rest — they wake as something. The faculty are holding the lid on, and their
  abstinence is the example they are setting. Turns the protagonist's mercy into the
  catastrophe.
- **Waiting.** The day is held open like a door for someone who is not in it — which is why a
  substitute could get in at all. Gives the dream's own detail a job: they are waiting for the
  son. Gut-punch: they have waited so long they have forgotten who they are waiting for, and
  only realise it when he walks in at seventy.
- **Payment.** The vampires caused it. The loop is not a memorial but a sentence being served,
  with the children as collateral. Coldest version, fewest moving parts.

> **Recommended: the roll call as the stated goal, waiting underneath it.** The faculty would
> tell you the point is that every child is accounted for. One of them, somewhere in the
> building, still knows that is not it.

## 7. The weapon is the roll sheet

A consequence of §6 that decides what kind of book this is.

If the roll call is the ritual, the protagonist's weapon is not a stake, a fire, or a broken
bell. **It is her voice reading a list.** And she is a substitute — she is handed the roll
sheet on the first morning, legitimately, as part of the job.

The loop puts the only thing that can kill it directly into her hands on day one, because it
cannot imagine a list being read any way but correctly.

Which makes the climax a woman calling names in an empty room. Cheap to stage, impossible to
forget.

---

## 8. Rules of the day

Horror lives on rules. The working set:

1. **Nobody is ever absent.** Every child answers roll, every day, forever — except one desk.
   That desk is the thread.
2. **Blood ends it.** Living blood on the floor is the one thing the day cannot process. A
   paper cut, a nosebleed — the building *notices*.
3. **Her changes persist, and they should not.** Day one she moves a chair; day three it is
   still where she left it. The loop is degrading around her, and the faculty register it
   before she does. This is the escalation clock — the story gets a ticking element, not just
   dread.
4. **Seams, not gore.** A boy laughs a beat before the joke. A girl writes the same sentence
   all period. The lockers are empty. Lunch is served and nobody eats. Follow a child home:
   the house is right, the dinner is right, the parents are right — and at 9pm every house on
   the street goes dark at once, like a switch.

---

## 9. Protagonist

Her stated reason for being there is the substitute job. Her real reason should be a
photograph, a record, a name — something proving there is a school the town has a soft blank
about.

**Give her a loss of her own** — a child, a marriage, a year she would live again if offered.
Two reasons:

- It makes the temptation to stay a live wire rather than a late-act gimmick, which is what
  Ending B needs to work.
- It sharpens the confrontation. The faculty are not arguing with a skeptic. They are arguing
  with someone who understands exactly why they did it.

## 10. Candidate endings

**A — The mercy massacre.** She finds the anchor (roll book / bell / a body still in the
building) and breaks it. The children take the news *well* — they are relieved. The adults do
not: released into time, they are hungry for the first time in eighty years, and there is one
living woman in the room. She survives because the children stand between her and the faculty.
*Most commercial.*

**B — She takes its place.** The loop did not admit her; it recruited her. It is running down
and needs a living heart. She volunteers. Last image: a new substitute arriving, handed the
same roll sheet. *Most quotable, most bleak. Requires her own loss planted early.*

**C — She simply leaves.** She gets out. Nothing happens. No one believes her, the school is a
field, the town is fine — and every year on that date she wakes at 6:40 with the taste of
chalk. The horror is the indifference. *Most literary, hardest sell.*

**D — She empties the school.** She cannot break the loop, but she can do what the faculty
would not: tell the children the truth, one at a time. Every child who believes stops. The
adults watch eighty years of work drain out. The last is the child whose body is still in the
building — she has to go find it. They let her walk out, because there is nothing left to
guard.

**E — The son.** *(Straight from the dream's own detail.)* The replaced teacher's boy is alive
outside — and seventy years old. The loop has run that long. She brings him back through the
front doors, and a mother sees her son with an old man's face. Time enters the building
through a face, and the day comes apart.

> **Recommended: E as the climax mechanism, D as the resolution.** The old man is the crowbar;
> emptying the school is the ending. A is the commercial alternative; B is the one that gets
> quoted.

---

## 11. Open questions

- **What killed the children?** Keep it mundane. Decide late — the reveal is emotional, not
  clever.
- **Whose desk is the empty one?** Likely the child whose body is still in the building, the
  ritual's anchor. Finding the desk is act one; finding the body is act three.
- **How long has it run?** Eighty years is proposed. It must be long enough that the son is
  elderly.
- **Does the town outside know, refuse to know, or have a hole where the knowing goes?**
- **Does she have a child, or did she?** See §9 — this one changes the book.
