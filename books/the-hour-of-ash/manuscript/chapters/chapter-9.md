*In the archonship of Kleodamos, in the month Poitropios, Xenon son of Euangelos, a Delphian, sold to Apollo the Pythian a female body. Her name is Aphrodisia, she is home-born, and the price is three minae of silver, and he has the whole price. Aphrodisia has entrusted the sale to the god, on these terms: that she be free, and not to be laid hold of by anyone all her life, doing what she wishes and going to whom she wishes. But let Aphrodisia remain with Xenon as long as Xenon lives, doing what she is told, so far as she is able, without complaint. If she does not remain, or does not do what she is told, let Xenon have power to punish her as he wishes, by beating and by selling, and let the sale to the god be void. Guarantor according to the law: Damon son of Aristion. Witnesses: the priests Euthydikos and Nikandros; of private persons, Babylos, Eudoros, Philon son of Philon.*

The letters had been cut that winter. All round them the wall was written over from the paving to as high as a man could reach off a ladder, freedom after freedom, each one fitted to the face of whatever block it happened to fall on. A line would run to the edge of one stone, jump the joint and carry on in the next. The old ones had weathered soft, and the round letters in them had filled with green like wells. Aphrodisia's stood out pale and sharp among them. The cutter had scored himself guidelines with a point before he began, and they still showed above and below each line, fine as the scratches on a long-playing record. In the third line he had cut a wrong letter and made it into the right one with two more strokes; the mended letter stood a little fatter than its neighbors.

I had climbed the Sacred Way that morning to find a priest, and I was standing under the temple terrace with my head back, reading a woman's freedom, when a chip of limestone dropped into my hair.

"Mind yourself down there," a man called. "That's a soft block. It flies."

He sat a few stones along, on a plank laid across two trestles. His legs hung down, and he was floured all over with stone dust, with a mallet in one fist. He was cutting the next one, and he had reached the archon.

"Did you cut this?" I said.

"All that end. Everything you can read without stooping. You read, then?"

"Yes."

"Read me the end of the second line, there. He made me leave his grandfather off. Saved himself the letters, and now he reads like something that grew in a field."

"Xenon son of Euangelos."

"And son of Xenon, it ought to say, which is a whole word I've not been paid for." He blew into the cut he was making, and the dust went past my face. "They all do it. A man frees a girl and haggles with me over his own grandfather."

"Who is she?"

"Xenon's girl. Born in his kitchen. She's been putting it by since she was small, an obol here and an obol there out of what the lodgers gave her. Last month she brought it up here in a pot and paid it to the god, and the god paid it to Xenon."

"So that nobody can say she bought herself."

"And the god doesn't want a girl to cook for him, so she's free."

"And then she went home with Xenon."

"Where else would she go? It says. He's forty, and eats for two. She'll be free the day they carry him out past here to the tombs, if she's not under a stone of her own by then, and after that she can go where she likes."

"And if he sells her before then? To some man in Amphissa who has never—"

"She brings him up here and stands him in front of it, and he reads it, and his money's gone." He struck, and a flake sprang past my ear and ticked away down the steps behind me. "The wall's for that. A tablet gets lost, or a man says it did. I've never had to cut one of these twice. She paid me an obol over the price, from what was left in her pot, to cut hers deep. She can't read a letter of it. I read it out to her the day I finished, twice through, and she listened to the whole of it and went home." He tilted the mallet at the Way. "That's her."

A young woman was passing along the Way with a basket of washing on her head, in a tunic dyed a cheap blue that had faded in streaks. She went by under the wall, near enough to touch, with her gaze on the steps.

"Which of the priests would tell a woman what the god costs?" I said.

"Any of them would tell you, and add a bit. Euthydikos. His name's at the foot of half this wall; he likes it in stone. He lives over the gymnasium lane, the house with a bay tree in a pot at the door. You'll hear him before you find it. He's got the smith in this morning over a lamp-stand."

I took the gymnasium lane, putting my question together. The god was supposed to answer in verse, and I had no verse to give him back. What I had was a mountain with a crack in it that had let me in and would not let me out, and no Greek for the middle of that story. *Where is the way back?* was too short; any god could answer it by pointing down the road to Kirrha. *Where is the door I came in by, now it is shut?* was nearer. I tried it over in a murmur, and it sounded like somebody patting her pockets for a key. Whether I believed in Apollo was beside the point. He kept the one window in Phocis that took questions about doors, and the cave had been shut to me twice.

* * *

The bay tree stood in its pot, and a slave on a stool beside it, shelling beans into his lap, looked at my feet first, as everyone in Delphi did, and then worked his way up. Beyond the door a man's voice was raised in complaint, and another man's answered it, both at once, and under the two of them the wheeze of a bellows.

"The temple's up the hill," the slave said.

"I have a question for the priest Euthydikos."

"Everybody has a question." He tipped the beans off his lap into the bowl and went in. He left the door open. I came after him as far as the threshold and stood on it.

The court was paved, with a colonnade down two sides and a well-head in the middle under a wooden lid, and it smelled of charcoal. A boy knelt by a pot of coals working a goatskin bellows with both knees. The smith was short and wore a leather apron, and his eyebrows had long since gone into somebody's fire. He was down on the paving beside a sack, and on the sack lay a bronze stand of the kind that held a dish of coals for the incense: three lion's feet joined at the heels, a shaft fluted like a column, and at its top a shallow bowl under a pierced lid on a chain. Standing, it would have come to my hip; it lay on its side. The shaft had snapped across just above the feet, and the smith was pressing wet clay round the stump in a fat collar.

On the step of the colonnade stood the priest, in a white himation, with his hands up its sleeves. His beard was long and gray, combed out and cut square at its end, so neat that it looked put on over his own. He was telling the smith that his grandfather had given that stand to the god.

"Your grandfather bought it cast too thin," the smith said, "and the clay's nearly on."

From the threshold the bowl was in plain view. Where it sat on the head of the shaft a band of duller metal ringed the join, gray where the rest was green and brown, smooth where the rest was pitted. It was lead. Somebody had broken that bowl off once and run solder in to fix it on again; it had been neatly done, dressed down level afterward, and a little of it had crept away into the flutes.

"If you heat it," I said, "the bowl will come off."

The slave swung round in the doorway and stared at me as though I had ridden in on a mule.

"Who's that?" the smith asked.

"Kallia's Syracusan," said the slave. "She wants the god."

"Ask her what she means by it," the priest said to the slave.

I answered over the slave's shoulder. "The bowl is held on with lead. There, where it is gray. When the shaft is hot enough for new bronze, the heat will climb to the top, the lead will let go, and the bowl will drop into his coals."

"I know it's lead," the smith said. "I put it there."

The priest's hands emerged from his sleeves. They were red and swollen across every knuckle with chilblains, tight and glossy as cherries, and he inspected them with distaste and withdrew them again.

"Yours, is it?"

"Nine years back, for your father. It's held, hasn't it?"

"And if the bowl had dropped into your fire this morning?"

"I'd have put it on again. It's no trouble to me." The smith turned round on his knees to have me in front of him. "And you know lead, do you?"

"That it runs long before bronze does," I said. "And that the bronze at the break is full of holes. Look at it."

Everyone had looked at it but the priest. The broken face of the shaft was gray and granular, pocked all over with little round cavities where gas had been caught in the metal as it cooled. The casting had been bad since the day it was poured, and new metal run against it would have had to fight its way into every hole, and it would have lost.

"What would she do instead?" said the priest.

"No forge," I said. "I would put a rod inside the shaft, across the break, so the two halves sit on it. And I would fill the crack over with wax until you cannot find where it was."

"Wax." The smith spat beside the sack. "On the god's bronze. It'll stand till the first warm day and then lie down in the incense."

"The rod takes the weight. The wax is only for the eye."

"And when some slave knocks it over again?"

"It will come apart along the same line," I said, "and in no new place, and the next mender will have nothing to undo but wax."

Up to then Euthydikos had looked at the slave, or at the smith, or at the air a little behind my shoulder, where a husband might have stood. This question he asked of me.

"Where does a woman learn bronze?"

"In Syracuse," I said, which was where I was from, now.

"And for this rod and this wax, what does she want?"

"Tell me first what a question to the god costs."

He sought the answer from the slave, and the slave had none for him, having no beans left to examine.

"For a Syracusan? First the pelanos, into the god's treasury, before he'll hear you at all. What a Syracusan pays I'd have to send to the archive to learn, to find whether your city ever made terms with the god, and if it did, whether anybody in it remembers. Call it a stater."

"Call it two," the smith told his clay.

"Then the goat. Cold water goes on its head at the altar, and it must tremble from the hooves up, every leg of it, or the god isn't receiving that day; it goes on the fire whatever it did, and home you go, and back you come next month with another goat." He considered the next item with his eyes half shut. "And a Delphian to stand for you, since you aren't one, and he'll expect a present for his trouble. And a man to carry the thing in, since you aren't that either."

"I know about the man."

"The rest is only money."

"Mend it for a stater," I said.

"A stater. For wax."

"For a stand that stands when the god comes home."

He spoke to the smith over my head. "Your price?"

"Three drachmas, and the clay found."

"And three more for the bowl, when it fell in your fire." He brought his gaze down to me, or near me. "A smith's price is for a smith's fire. I have wax from my own bees."

"It is not a smith's work. That is why it will last."

"Half."

The boy gaped at me over the bellows. Above us somebody had stepped out onto the gallery to listen. She showed me nothing but a hem at the balustrade, and the hem stayed perfectly still.

"When I have finished," I said, "look for the break. If you find it, pay me nothing. If you cannot, pay me the smith's price."

"In what light?"

"In any light you like."

He kept silent while the smith began scraping his clay off the stump. It came away in one piece, with the print of the flutes on its inside.

"Pay the smith for his clay," he told the slave, and then, from the house door, "In Syracuse, what do they do for chilblains?"

"Wool on the hands," I said. "With a place for each finger."

"Like a slave at the olive picking." He went in. "Daylight," he said, from inside.

The smith slapped the collar into his bucket and got his bellows under his arm. On his way out past me he stopped.

"When it lies down," he said, "send to me. I'll bring the lead."

* * *

The rod came from the smith in the end, by way of the slave at the door and two of the priest's obols.

They gave me the corner of the colonnade nearest the rear gate, where the light was good until the middle of the afternoon, and an old slave to watch me, who kept the priest's bees in a row of clay hives along the back wall and cared for little besides. He sat on an overturned basket by the hives and talked to them in an undertone. Now and again he rose and laid his ear to the end of one.

"There's a woman at the master's bronze," he told them, the first morning. "From Syracuse. Don't mind her."

The shaft had been cast round a core of clay, and the core was still in it, burnt black and gone to grit. I raked it out of both halves with a bent nail the bee-man found me, a pinch at a go. With a twist of dry grass I swept both faces until the cavities were empty and the gray crystal of the metal caught the sun. Then the rod. The smith had sent his second-best file with it, I think in the hope that I would ruin it. I filed along the rod, gave it a quarter turn and filed again, and now and then I tried it in the mouth of the shaft, and the rod refused. It was too fat, and I filed it, and it was still too fat.

There had been a hardware store off Fortieth Street that sold screws singly out of little wooden drawers, by weight, with a cat asleep on the counter scale, and its name had gone, and I filed through most of an afternoon hunting for it.

Late in the day the rod went in. It slid into the foot end of the shaft to half its length and stopped, snug, and when I lowered the upper half of the shaft over the part that stood out, it went down with a faint push of air against my fingers and the two broken faces met. No fingernail would go into the crack. Where they met there was a line a hair wide, running round the shaft and through every flute, and from a pace off the line was all I saw.

"Have you any wax?" I asked the bee-man. "Raw wax, not off a tablet."

"From them?" He considered his hives. "They'll want to know what for."

"Tell them it is for the god."

In the morning he brought me a yellow cake of it wrapped in a fig leaf, and a sherd with lamp soot in it scraped off the kitchen wall.

I broke off a piece of the wax and worked it between my palms until it gave, and it smelled of a red crayon left on a schoolroom radiator until it slumped and shone, and I kneaded the soot into it until it was dark enough to pass for bronze in the shade. I warmed the blade of the bee-man's knife over the lamp, pressed the wax into the line round the whole shaft and polished it down with a pad of wool.

It was a good fill; in the colonnade's shade it disappeared. I carried the stand into the open to see it as the priest would, and with the low sun slanting over it the line came back as a thin gold thread, because the wax shone and the old bronze had stopped shining in the priest's grandfather's day.

On the second try I put in more soot and a pinch of earth from the bay tree's pot, and dulled the fill afterward with my thumb instead of the wool. In the sun the thread had gone. But the wax was soft, and by noon the sun on the paving was warm enough to make it softer, and close to, my own thumbprint showed in it, the whorls raised in the fill.

"Out it comes," the bee-man said. "He'll not know the difference. He couldn't find his own feet in that beard."

Early in the afternoon a woman descended the gallery stair into the court, and the bee-man woke and was suddenly very busy at his hives. She was much younger than the priest, and she kept her himation up over her head in her own house; under it her hair was crimped in rows as regular as the flutes on the stand. She came and stood over it.

"Does it hold?"

"It holds. It is the seam I am taking out."

"He wants it in its place for the god's return. He's told half the town his grandfather's stand will be back by the altar." She had something wrapped in a napkin against her breast, and she opened the napkin far enough to show me: the handle of a bronze mirror, a little woman standing on a tortoise with her arms raised to hold up a disc, and no disc. "The rest is upstairs. It came off on the stair at midwinter. It was my mother's."

"I can join it again."

"Not now." She folded the napkin over the little woman's face. "Not in his court."

She climbed back to the gallery, and the bee-man watched her to the top of the stair.

I scraped the second fill out. Wax by itself was never going to be hard enough; it wanted resin. The priest's lamps burned oil, and his kitchen burned oak.

At the back gate Valens was unloading split wood off a line of mules into the priest's woodshed.

The gray stood tied to the gatepost apart from the others with her load off. A little pot hung from the post by its handle, and he was working what was in it into a sore place on her withers where the saddle had galled her. Her ears were flat. When she swung her head round to bite him he put her nose away with his elbow and went on working.

"Is that pitch?" I said, at the gate.

"For her back. The new saddle's rubbed her."

"Will you sell me some? A walnut's worth."

"What for?"

"To harden wax. Mine is too soft. It takes a thumbprint."

His eyes left me for the court: to the stand on its sack in the sun, and to the old man on his basket by the hives, who had gone to sleep sitting up.

"That's the god's," he said.

"The priest's. He is paying me to mend it."

He wiped his fingers on the gray's mane, unhooked the pot and broke the cake of pitch in it with the haft of his knife, and gave me a piece. It was almost black, cold and brittle, with a mule hair in it.

"Will it stand?" he said.

"With this in it? Years. If nobody knocks it over."

"Somebody will."

"What do I owe you?"

"An obol."

"I have nothing until he pays me."

"He'll pay." He bent to her withers again.

I melted the pitch into a lump of the wax in a sherd over the lamp. When it ran I drew the shaft off the rod, poured a little of the hot mixture into the mouth of each half and seated them again while it was soft. That held. Once it had cooled I could have lifted the stand by its bowl and swung it round my head.

The third fill went in dark and hard and took the soot well, and when I rubbed it down it went as dull as the old metal beside it. In the shade it was gone and in the sun it was gone. I knelt and brought my eye down level with the shaft and sighted along it toward the sun, and the line was there. The bronze had a skin like an orange's, pitted with the small pits the years had put in it, and my fill was smooth.

I dug it out a third time, too fast, and the knife skidded off the hard fill and laid my thumb open at the base. I sucked it until it stopped.

"She's cut herself." The bee-man was smoking a hive. "On his bronze. Don't tell him."

The sun had left the court by the fourth fill. I pressed it in, and before it could harden I pricked the whole face of it with a bundle of bristles from the bee-man's brush, rubbed soot into the pricks with a fingertip and took the rest off with the wool. I had lost it myself. If I sighted along the shaft I thought there was something, and it might have been only the light going.

"He'll look at it in the morning," the bee-man said from his basket. "When the sun's on the court. He said the sun."

I wanted one more try at dawn, before Euthydikos came out. I set the stand on its three feet on the paving. The bowl sat level, and the lid hung still on its chain.

*I bind Glykera the weaver, and her loom, and the warp on her loom, and the weights that hang from the warp, and her two hands and her eyes. Let every thread she throws go slack and every thread she pulls snap, and let what comes off her loom come off crooked, and let nobody buy it. I give her down to Hermes below and to Earth, and I nail her under the lead.*

The boy who found it was fishing in the mud at the drovers' end of the spring basin, where the trough emptied into a culvert, lying on his belly with one arm down it. Kallia wanted the jars filled before she lit her fire, and I was at the lions with the first of them; the stand would have to go to the priest as it stood. It was barely light, and the water bit where it ran over my wrist; the almond trees on the terraces below the road had come into flower in the last week, and the water was still in January.

He scrambled up with something gray and folded in his fist and ran with it to the women at the lions.

"Put that where you found it," one of the women said. "It's been given to the ones below, and they don't want it handed round."

None of them would look at it, so in the end he brought it to me.

"Is there money in it?"

It was a sheet of lead about the size of my palm, folded twice over and pierced through every layer with a bronze nail. It had lain in the mud till the creases went white. I worked the nail out and opened the folds slowly, because lead that had lain in wet ground tore like old cloth, and the writing was scratched in capitals beneath the white, crowded, every line running uphill at its end.

"What's it say?"

"It is a curse. Somebody wants a weaver's cloth to come off the loom crooked."

"Which weaver?"

"Glykera."

"There's no Glykera at the spring." He studied the lead, deeply cheated. "Is it worth anything?"

"It is lead. The smith will give you something for it."

I closed it up and handed it back to him, with the nail, and he ran off up the steps toward the town, past the women and their jars, holding it out in front of him.

* * *

Once the sun was full on the court, the priest came out with his beard combed and the bee-man behind him, and he circled the stand before he bent to it.

He knew roughly where it had snapped; he had stood on his step and watched the smith pack clay round the stump. He went to the foot of the shaft first. He studied it close, and then from farther off. Then he sent the bee-man to the kitchen for a lamp, though the court was full of sun, and held the flame beside the shaft himself and moved it slowly up and down.

"Here?" He had a swollen finger on a flute a little above the line.

"Lower."

The finger slid down the flute, past the place, and up again.

"Show me."

I put my fingertip on the line at the back, where the fill crossed the deepest flute. He put his face so close to it that his beard brushed the bowl, and looked at my finger and at the bronze around it. His lips were moving. When he straightened at last he gave the lamp back to the bee-man.

"The smith's price," he said.

He kept his purse on a cord round his neck, under the white wool. He brought it out and loosened it and took the coins out coin by coin with his red fingers. I held out my hand, and he let them fall into it from a little above, so that his fingers stayed clear of my palm. The last one struck the edge of my hand, went off onto the paving and rolled under the stand.

I knelt for it. It had stopped against a lion's foot, and I hooked it out with a finger. When I stood again the bee-man was holding out a square of linen to his master, and the priest was wiping his fingers on it, each one, the thumb last.

Above us the hem was at the balustrade again.

My palm was black in every crease with soot and wax, and the coins lay on the black. He had not touched it. Perhaps it was the soot.

"Thank you," I said, and bent my head to him as well.

"There's that smith's lead in the top that wants cutting out," he said, already on his way indoors. "When I send, come back and do the bowl."

The bee-man walked me to the rear gate.

"The mistress says," he told me there, "when you come for the bowl, come round to the kitchen door."

He went off to his hives.

Kallia was in the court at home, scouring the big pot with sand. I opened my hand over the hearthstone and tipped the coins out on the stone.

"Into your hand?"

"Onto it. From above."

To that she gave no word at all. She filled the basin at the jar and stood it on the hearthstone beside the coins. A lump of the gray washing clay went down next to it.

I washed. The water in the basin went black, and the black worked into the clay, and the cut in my thumb came open in the water and put a thread of red into the black. I cleaned round it. It went on coloring the basin for a while, fainter each time my hands went in, and then the water stayed black.

"He says a stater for the god," I said, with my hands in the water, "and a goat that shakes, and more after that."

The sand hissed round the belly of the pot.

"Who takes the goat up?" said Kallia.

"The man, I suppose."

"Then you'll want a man a goat will walk for. They don't, for most."

When my hands were as clean as they were going to get, I knotted the priest's coins into a rag with the two brass ones from 1998, the poet and the other, and the whole of it lay in my palm and weighed very little. Kallia's sand had gone quiet while I tied it.

*Runaway from the household of Quintus Arrius Celer at Corinth: a Thracian, answering to the name Lupus, the property of that household. About thirty years old, tall, broad in the shoulder, with a scar on the back of the right hand from a wound badly stitched. He speaks Latin, and Greek like a foreigner. He has been in the arena; whoever takes him should take him with others. To whoever brings him back to the household at Corinth, five hundred drachmas. To whoever shows where he is kept, in a sanctuary or in the house of a man able to answer for him, three hundred. Copied from the notice posted at Corinth in the month Dadaphorios.*

It was painted in red on a board whitened with gypsum, pegged to a pillar of the colonnade in the square, beside the door the herald used. The brush had dragged in the gypsum, and the red had run a little at the foot of every upright.

My errand in the square was to price a goat.

The goat man had his animals penned in hurdles at the lower end of the square beside the fountain, a dozen of them, and he was eating a heel of bread.

"For the god?" he said, with his mouth full. "It's a shaker you're after." He reached across the hurdle and took a young she-goat by the horn, dipped his cupped hand in the fountain trough and threw the water down her spine, and she shuddered from her ears to her tail and stamped. "There. Like a bride. Mine all do it; I keep them warm, so the water surprises them. The fellow out beyond the stadium keeps his on the hill all winter, and you can pour a river on them and they'll only look at you."

"What does she cost?"

He looked at the rag in my fist, which was the shape of what was in it. "More than that."

"How much more?"

"For that you can have the shake and not the goat." He shook the rest of the water off his hand. "Send whoever's leading her up, with the money. I'll keep her warm till then."

The men at the board were why I went to read it. There were five or six of them, and they had the look of men who had been standing there since it went up and had settled among themselves what it said. They talked across one another, and what reached me came in pieces.

"Five hundred drachmas."

"At Corinth. You'd have to walk him to Corinth for it, every step."

"Walk a Thracian to Corinth? He'd walk you into the gulf."

"Three hundred, then, for saying where he is. You don't have to walk anybody for that."

"Who'd give up a man in his own town for three hundred?"

"I would. For three hundred I'd give up my landlord, and hold the rope."

"When did that go up?"

"With the oil from Thebes. First carts since the snow, and the clerk had it on the wall before the oil was off."

"Lupus. What sort of name is that for a man?"

"It's a wolf."

"A Roman will call a slave anything. My cousin's master in Patrae called his cook Victory."

"There's a Thracian sells eels at Kirrha."

"That one's sixty, and he couldn't take a kid off its mother."

They drifted away toward the money-changer, arguing about the eel-seller, and I stepped in among the pillars with my rag of coins and read the board through.

I read it through again. The rag had gone damp in my fist.

Valens came into the square at its upper corner, with the gray behind him on a slack rope. She carried nothing. He came along the row of pillars, past the votive stalls, at his ordinary pace. The men from the board had their backs to him.

He halted at the board.

I had moved to the next column. He stood with the gray's nose at his shoulder while a slow man might read it once, and then as long again.

He turned his head and found me there by the column. I was looking at him. I looked at his face.

He looked back at me while a cart came in at the top of the square and rolled down past us, oil jars roped in straw, the carter swearing at his ox. Then he took the gray down the square at a walk, past the men still at the eel-seller, to the trough below the fountain. I had his obol in the rag. I kept my place.

He let the gray drink first.
