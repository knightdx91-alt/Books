*A girl, holy cow, and the carter swears the kid has a set of lungs on her they can hear at the river. I did her stars the night your note came, with the bad hand propped on a stack of tablets and the shakes quiet for a change. They're on the other sheet in my best Greek for your landlady to hang on a nail. Tell her the child's got Jupiter sitting square on top of her head, which is the kind of thing a landlady likes to hear and happens to be about half true if you squint. Aelia. Good name. Sounds like a girl who'll get things done and not say please first. Tell your mister I said so. And tell him my stair's fixed. I had a man in from the rope walk who charged me like he was putting up the Walt Whitman Bridge.*

*She'll be near four the summer we go, by my book, which is plenty big to sit through nine innings if somebody keeps the peanuts coming, and believe me somebody will. Feed her. Sleep when she sleeps. That's what my mother told every woman on our block with a baby. I never knew what it meant till tonight, and I still don't. A. F.*

The letter was written on the reverse of a stranger's future. On the front, in Fenn's courtly Greek, a wine shipper of Puteoli had been promised a fair passage to Ostia and a son before the vintage, until somebody knocked the ink over the son. Fenn had flipped it and written to me on the clean side of the blot. His English ran small and square right out to the sheet's edges, without a margin anywhere. Each line dipped toward its finish, where the wrist had tired. A drop of milk had already landed on the Walt Whitman Bridge and dried there in a small glazed blister.

The Greek sheet with her stars on it had gone next door at once. Grata had it read out to every customer that day, Jupiter and all. She nailed it above the loom in her back room, next to Lucius's stars, with a better nail.

Aelia was three weeks old. She fed with the whole of herself, her fists working at the air and her feet treading in the blanket and her jaw going so hard that the tops of her ears moved with it. When the milk let down faster than she could take it, she came off choking and roaring at once and latched on again with the roar still in her. For a while there was a noise in the room like somebody gargling in a cistern. She had been born with a thatch of black hair that stuck up from her scalp, and no wet palm in the house could smooth it. She had been born with fingernails too, which nobody had warned me about. She had already scratched a pink line down her own nose with them. When she was fed she went suddenly heavy, a warm sack of flour against my arm, and the top of her head smelled of milk and of the wool she had been lying on and of something else that was only hers, which I put my nose to between paragraphs.

I read her the letter by the little lamp at the bed's head. It was the hour when the bakers opposite fired their oven, and their voices at the oven mouth were the only others in the street. I read it in English. Since my first winter in Phocis I had used no English aloud unless somebody was there to hear it. I had settled the question of whether Aelia was somebody on the night the letter came; nobody else was awake to argue. She had it from me three times over, the rope walk and the bridge and the peanuts, in the voice I would have used to read out a recipe. On the third night, reaching the bottom while she was still drinking, I read her the reverse too, the wine shipper's son, in Greek.

The room was cold at that hour, with the brazier sunk to a red eye in its ash and the shutter barred on the wind off the bay. The lamp flame bowed at every draft under the shutter and straightened after it. I leaned on the wall with the blanket round us both, the letter propped against my shins. I held the sheet to the light with my free hand when I needed to, and turned it with my chin when I didn't.

Valens lay asleep on his front at my side with one arm dangling to the floor, the palm up on the boards. He had got in off the road after dark, wet to the thigh from a ford above Stabiae. Nothing short of the roaring woke him; the English never had. Lucius slept at the bottom of the bed on his own pallet, folded up with his knees under him and his rump in the air.

Two raps sounded through the plaster from Grata's room, with something wooden, and after them her voice, which the wall did very little to stop.

"That one's got your temper and his chest. Not a notion of the hour between the three of you, and I've the market before light and a pot to have on before the market."

Aelia lost the breast at the knocking with her mouth still open, and roared at the wall until the bakers laughed.

* * *

His first letter had come within a month of our getting back from Puteoli, while I still had no idea I was carrying her. A carter who hauled jars of fish sauce down from Puteoli brought it, because he took his wine at Grata's on his way through. It came folded small and tied round with a thread, with my name and Grata's written on the outside in Greek, so that anybody at the gate could tell him whose door it belonged at. Every letter since had come by him. I kept them in the satchel now, in the order they had come, lying on top of the notebook.

In her fourth week Aelia had a night when nothing would serve her but walking. I walked her between the bed and the shutter with the satchel open on the blanket, and read her the first of them from the beginning.

*You weren't gone an hour when it hit me I never asked what they call you down there, Mrs. Big Fellow or what. So this is going care of Grata, and Grata can sort it out. The stars for your boy are in with this, a long life and so forth. Don't let your landlady pay me. I'd like to tell you I got a good night's sleep after you left. The fact is I talked all night. I don't mean to anybody. I sat on the stool and talked American till the gulls started up, about the automat and the El on Market Street and a girl in homeroom who sat behind me and smelled of oranges, and somewhere around the third watch the man downstairs who salts the tunny took a broom handle to his ceiling, which is my floor, and told me in good plain Greek to shut up or he'd come up. First time in thirty-odd years a man's told me to pipe down while I was talking English. Okay, he told me in Greek. You get the idea. The tables are coming soon as there's a clean copy, which is a bigger job than you'd think. A. F.*

She roared through the broom handle and the tables and both initials. I left the letter on the blanket and walked her, reading nothing, until near light, and then she slept, for no reason I could find. One fist was knotted in my neckline so hard that I had to unbend the fingers to lay her down.

I had answered that first letter the week it came, sitting on the roof in the long light with a sheet from the stationer by the forum and a reed I had cut too fine. My English left the reed in a hand I barely knew. Except for the tremor notes in the back of the notebook, which were dates and a few words apiece, I had last written any length of it in Philadelphia. Partway down the sheet I stopped over scissors and wrote it twice in the margin to see which looked right; both looked wrong, and I wrote shears instead and went on. I wrote him about the stair tread Valens had put in, and the potted fig, and Grata, and nothing whatever about the door.

When Aelia was eight days old, Grata hung a crescent of thin bronze round her neck on a red thread, against the evil eye and every other thing that went abroad in the town. She had the thread in her mouth before the prayer was over, and the crescent after it.

"You'll have that Chaldean writing to this one next," Grata told me, knotting the thread, "and she'll be reading him before she's walking, the pair of you at it, in that scratching nobody in this town can make head or tail of, and me at my own accounts. My man reckons it's a curse, what he sends you. He'd call the moon a curse if it rose on a market day."

Aelia put everything in her mouth. She had my hair there whenever my head came in reach, and Valens's beard, and the corner of the blanket. Once she had the end of the bronze strainer I was mending, because I had been fool enough to dangle it over her while I looked for the split.

In the mornings Lucius planted himself at the bedside, chin on the blanket, and gazed at her for long stretches, the way he gazed at the mules being shod. From time to time a finger went out, to try whether her eye would shut at a touch. It was to keep that finger busy that I gave him the knucklebone. It had ridden in the bottom of my bags since the Corycian, under the notebook, next to the tape in its sock, with the other things I could have explained to no one this side of the cave. I had taken it from a cave floor against every rule I was ever taught and every rule I had since taught to other people. Now a boy's mouth tested it and returned it shining, and it rolled, and was fetched, and rolled again. The bone had lain in the dark since somebody's goat died.

* * *

In her fifth week she began to stay awake after feeding, with her eyes wide and roving after the lamp flame. One such night Valens was awake as well. He was sitting at the end of the mattress with a lead rope over his thighs. One of the mules had chewed its end to a brush at a tethering post outside Nuceria. He had unlaid the strands back a palm's length and was splicing an eye into it, with the lamp on the boards at his feet. He tucked each strand under its neighbor with the point of a nail and drew it home.

"From Puteoli?" he asked, his eyes on the rope.

"His summer letter."

I read it to Aelia. I had read it once before, at the vintage, by myself, and not again since.

*You want a thing about the Phillies I didn't tell you in my room, here's one, and after it I'll knock it off. The Sunday after Chico Ruiz they've dropped six straight and my sister's good coat's on already with the scorecard pencil stuck through the buttonhole. She kept score. I never could, I'd lose the inning, but she filled hers in like a bank deposit slip, every little diamond, the pitches down the side in a hand you could read across a room. So we go up to Twenty-first and Lehigh on the trolley and sit up in right, and Johnny Callison hits three home runs. Three. Same afternoon. She writes them in, and after the third she draws a ring around it and shows it to the fellow in the next seat, who doesn't care. And we lose fourteen to eight.*

*She won't take the trolley home, after. She starts walking. So we walk. Germantown Avenue, every block of it, in Sunday shoes, with the trolleys going by us full of losers, and it gets dark around Mount Airy, and under every streetlight she stops and reads me the game off the card, every out, the whole shebang, and before we're home she's read it so we nearly won. She left the card on the hall table when we got in. That's where I left her the note, the morning I went. No idea if the card was still under it. Home by Labor Day, I put. A line about the milk.*

*The bathhouse man says his pool went cloudy Tuesday. Nothing came of it. A. F.*

When I finished, his hands had stopped too, with a strand half under and the nail in his fist.

"Who's dead?"

"Nobody. His side lost a game."

"What side?"

"Men who hit a ball with a stick, in his city, when he was young. He went to watch them with his sister. She made him walk home after, the length of the city."

"What was she called?"

"He doesn't put her name."

"All that way with her, and no name."

Her name was in the front of the notebook, beneath the letters in the satchel, in his own capitals, written the winter before he went. I left the satchel as it was.

"You read it slow."

"It's uphill, that street."

Aelia had fallen asleep somewhere between the third home run and the note. Her mouth hung open on the breast, with a bead of milk standing at the corner of it. Valens had walked her the previous night for the whole of the first watch and half the second, pacing the shop among the customers' pots, talking to her low in Latin. She had roared at him from start to end of it until I came through.

"She sleeps for that," he said.

"She sleeps because she's full."

"She was full last night."

He finished the splice and coiled the rope beside the lamp, the new eye uppermost.

"She knows it," he said. "She had it in her ears before she was born. Grata says they come out knowing the voice they heard inside."

"She never heard a word of it inside. I spoke it to nobody while I carried her."

"She hears it now."

He sat bent forward with his big hands hanging loose, gazing at his daughter, who was asleep in a language he did not have.

"Give me a word of it," he said.

"Which word?"

"One she knows."

She knew none of it, and nor did a soul in the street. But he had asked me for something, and I gave him goodnight. I explained it: the word for a day's end, given to a sleeper. He tried it twice, low, eyes on the flame. At his first try the word broke in two. At the second the pieces ran together. On the third he leaned down over Aelia and gave it to her, very low. She went on sleeping through it, full of milk, with her fingers spread on my breast.

* * *

At daybreak I had her in the shop as Valens passed with the string. There were five mules nose to tail in the half-dark with their breath standing over them, and Felix at the back of the line with a lantern on a pole. Grata had her shutter boards half down and was stacking them against the counter, in a cloak with the hood pulled over her night-cap. Valens halted the lead mule beside her with a word, and said to her, in a clear voice:

"Goodnight."

Grata looked at him over the board in her arms. "And to you, and to all your mules, and to that loud one in there. I hope your day goes kinder than my night did, I heard every hour of it through the plaster, and I've a mouth on me now like a dog's bed, and the bread's late, and the fish man's boy is a thief."

He gave it to the dun as well, at the front of the string, scratching under her headstall where she liked it. After that he took his mules off downhill, walking light. Felix came past at the tail and asked what the word had been, and was it a god's name, and could he have one too. He had rounded the corner by the fullery before I had answered any of it.

I did not call after either of them. Before him, the only English ever spoken aloud in that street had been mine.

The street went by as it did every morning at that hour. The bakers' boys passed with the loaves stacked steaming on boards on their heads, and the fuller's lads bawled across the vats; somebody's bucket clanked at the fountain on the corner. It was Latin, all of it, and the Latin of this street, which shortened its words and swallowed the ends of them and had a curse for every trade. I held Aelia up to the gap in the boards, into the cold and the smell of the bread. I told her the names of the things going by in it, low, the loaves and the boys and the bucket, in the language she heard all day from everybody but me at night.

* * *

The autumn letter had come at the vintage, when I was so big that I sat at the bench sideways. I had owed him an answer since. In her sixth week I unfolded it at the bench in the shop front, with Aelia asleep against me in the sling and the brazier at my feet. The bronze strainer from the inn at the crossroads waited at my elbow for a patch over the split that ran through its pierced holes. It was raining in the street. The gutter in front of the shop ran deep enough that people crossed on the raised stones with their skirts gathered up. Nothing would dry out of doors that week, so her swaddling bands were strung across the shop front, and every customer reached my bench through a wet curtain of them. For once the wet had knocked the fuller's smell down, and the shop had only charcoal in it, and the strainer's old wine, and the milk on my tunic.

*Sure, I said it, with my own mouth in my own room, so sue me. Who won the Series in ninety-seven? Just that. I've been lying up here figuring how much there is I've missed, and that's the piece of it that won't let me sleep. I had a birthday. I keep it by the old calendar, as near as I can work it against theirs, and as of this letter that's one person around here who knows it. I bought myself a melon. A. F.*

The answer needed no thinking about, and I had not watched a pitch of that Series. My father had left it on the voicemail at work on the Monday morning. The little red light on my telephone had been going when I walked in with my coffee. It's me, he had said, I'm in the kitchen in my socks, Florida won it, Florida, in the eleventh, would you believe it, a team that isn't as old as your car, call me when you're home, I'll be around. I sharpened a reed and turned it into his kind of English for him, as near as I was able. The Florida Marlins beat Cleveland in the seventh game, in extra innings, I wrote, and my father left me the news on my voicemail at work, first thing Monday. Beneath that went the girl's weight, in the terms Grata used for it, which were the creases at her wrists; then the rain, and a strainer on my bench with a split through the holes that I did not think I could patch well enough to fool the wine. The melon got no mention; whatever there was to say about it, the sheet had no room for.

While the ink dried I cut a patch for the strainer out of a scrap of thin bronze, the bottom of a lamp somebody had trodden flat in the street. I pierced it to match from the underside with a nail, hole for hole, so that the wine going through would find a strainer and never learn of the patch. Piercing it was dull and slow. Aelia did not wake for the tapping; her cheek was squashed flat on my collarbone and her lips went on sucking at nothing. Marlins. A marlin was a fish men hung from a dock by its tail to be photographed beside, not a fish anybody ate. A boy on the 34 had worn the team's cap all that fall, blue-green, with the gold sticker still on the bill. The patch went on with three rivets, and I tapped them over cold.

Grata came for it herself, in the afternoon, with her cloak hooded for the rain and lentil steam coming in with her.

"Let's have it, he's at my counter now, the fish-sauce man, wanting his cup on credit like a son of mine, and I'll see it into his fist myself or he'll leave it on a bench at Herculaneum like he did in the autumn." She took the letter and stood it in the dry inside her cloak. "And somebody's painted a baker's son right over my Secundus by the baths, in red, in letters as high as a child, when every soul in Pompeii knows that wall's my boy's. That one's grown in a week. Look at her wrists. You'd best stop eating standing up."

* * *

The same carter brought his answer nine days after, and delivered it himself at the shop door, with the rain dripping from the point of his hood onto the sheet.

"Your Chaldean wants to buy me a new hood, the rate he keeps me wet," he said. "Last time he paid me in my wife's stars instead of the copper, and she's had nothing but luck since, and not a crumb of it's come my way."

It was a fresh sheet, nobody's fortune on the back of it, and the hand was larger than I had seen it. The reed had split up its nib before the first line was done. He had not stopped to cut another, and every long stroke in the letter had a hair of a second stroke running beside it.

*Florida. Okay. Florida, a ball club named for a fish, fine, I'll live, I'll get used to it, I got used to Claudius. But what in God's name is a voicemail? You put it right in the middle of a sentence like it's bread, like everybody's got one, like a fellow ought to know, and I sat up here with my lamp and turned it every way I know how. Voice. Mail. Is it a letter you talk into? Who carries it? Where's it go when nobody's home? I've got the Greek for every star there is over this roof, I can tell a Syrian sailor from an Egyptian one by how he swears, and you send me one word from home and I can't read it. Skip it. Don't explain it to me, I mean it. I'll find out back there same as the rest of them, I'll walk in off the street and somebody'll hand me one and I'll know what it's for. All this time I've been keeping the words. I kept them. I said them over at night so they'd keep. Nobody said they'd go on making new ones without me.*

*Tables soon. A. F.*

I read it to Aelia, because she was awake in my lap and I had the habit of reading them to her by then. She took God's name and the fish and the Syrian sailors staring into the brazier, her fist round my thumb. Partway down, my milk let down, both sides at once, though she had fed an hour since and wanted nothing. It soaked my tunic through to the skin, warm and then cold. I sat at the bench in it and read her the rest.

My father was not in it anywhere.

I wrote back that night on the bed, with the board on my lap and the lamp at my elbow. Valens woke as I wrote and lay watching me with his head on his arm, and I felt him do it for some while before he spoke.

"Is he sick?"

"No. He's angry with me."

"With you?"

"I used a word he'd never heard."

He turned onto his back with that and put an arm over his eyes against the lamp. "Then use the ones he knows."

I wrote a line about the rain. He spoke again, his arm still over his eyes.

"Do you tell him about me?"

"Sometimes."

"What?"

"That you walked her till the second watch."

"The ford at Stabiae's up to a mule's belly, and the dun swam the last of it. Put that in."

He was asleep again with half the sheet still to fill. I put the ford in. The strainer had held, I wrote, after a fashion. Lucius had learned to climb the stair on his hands and come down it backward on his belly, and Grata's son had been painted out by the baths. Near the bottom I began a line that started *It's a machine that*, and stopped, and took the sponge from the ink pot and wet it and lifted the words off the sheet before they could set, which was the one mercy of lampblack and gum. The sheet dried with a pale cloud in that place. What a voicemail was, I did not explain.

* * *

The tables came at the tail of the winter, sewn up in sacking, and it was Felix who carried them from the carter at the gate on his shoulder and lowered them onto the bench with a grunt, and kept his hand on them after.

He was seventeen that winter, or said he was, and he had a cut in one eyebrow, closed and crusted, that was new.

"From the Chaldean? It's heavy for letters. It's gold, I'd know that weight anywhere."

"It's figures. And your eye?"

"The dun threw her head."

"The dun."

"She's been jumpy since the rain. Two boats came round from Surrentum this morning, I watched them in from the wall. Is the sea open, then, or is it only them being stupid? Is it open for good?"

"Till autumn."

He was out and away, whistling.

Under the sacking there was a box of plain cedar, the kind a clerk keeps his rolls in, with a lid that slid in a groove, and a letter tied to the top of the lid with a thread.

*Here's the works, copied fair. Not by me. My hand won't do columns anymore. It goes wandering down the page after something. So I had the copying done by a boy I teach on the hill, the one with the olive pits, who's got a hand like a printer and doesn't know what a single one of the numbers is. He thinks they're Egyptian. He thinks I'm teaching him a cipher for spies, and he's never worked so hard for me in his life, and his father's tickled pink. You'll find it all checks. I'll be a monkey's uncle if it doesn't. A. F.*

*P.S. I read your letter again. Your old man. I'm sorry.*

Lucius was at the bench on tiptoe, to see. He had found the lid's groove with his thumbnail before I had finished reading. I took the box from him and carried it through, and slid it under the bedstead, deep, into the dust. Back in the shop, I gave him the bone.

* * *

One day near the winter's end brought hard bright sun and no wind at all, when the paving steamed in the morning and every house threw its shutters back to the air at once.

Valens had been at home for days. The roads had been rivers since the Kalends, and he had stabled the mules and spent two days on harness that wanted nothing. Lucius had the bone by the bed. He had learned to throw it so that it rolled instead of bouncing, by setting it on its flat side and pushing it off his palm. He sent it skittering away, chased it on his feet, which were new to him and mostly reliable, and sent it away again. Once it went too far and under the bed. He went down flat after it and wriggled in on his belly until only his feet were out. The feet went still while he worked at it in there. He backed out a while after with the bone in his fist and dust in his eyebrows and a long gray thread of cobweb hanging off his chin, and showed the bone to his father.

"Where did he get that?"

"From me."

"And you?"

"Off the floor of a cave."

Valens rolled it over on the boy's palm with a fingertip and left it with him. "Is it lucky?"

"It's a bone," I said.

I fetched the Pentax down from its shelf, in its cloth. There was a number in the window by the lever. I read it just enough to know the roll had not run out. I had spent no frames since the one of Valens asleep in the fig's shade.

A slab of sun lay in from the light-well over the blanket, warm, with the dust turning in it. I asked Valens to sit the boy there. He brushed the cobweb off him with the back of a finger and set him in the sun on the bed's end with his back to the wall and both his soles toward me. I fetched Aelia from her basket, where she had woken and was hunting along her own fist for a mouthful. Valens took her from me, and bent, and laid her across Lucius's lap, and fitted one of the boy's arms under her head and the other over her middle.

He was watching the boy. "He'll drop her."

"He won't."

"He's never held anything that kicks."

He drew his own hands back barely clear of her and held them so.

"Don't move," I told Lucius in Latin.

Lucius set about it. He stopped breathing as well, and his face began to go dark with it. He watched me. The rest of him did not stir at all. Aelia was the one moving. She got a hand free of the blanket and swung it about, and it found her brother's lower lip, and took hold.

The camera was at my eye. I turned the focusing ring until the dust in his eyebrows came clear. He had to breathe sometime. He blew it out through his nose in one gust that lifted her hair. Her fist stayed shut on his lip. His eyes ran over. I let the shutter go.

His father rested a hand on his hair. "You can move now."

He sat on in the sun with his lip in her fist, holding her very carefully, until his father lifted her away.
