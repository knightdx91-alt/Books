# Chapter 1: The Reception

I had been at my father's memorial reception for forty minutes when I wrapped a square of lemon cake in a paper napkin, put it in the pocket of my good black jacket, and started for the back stairs. I had not wanted the cake. It was the third lemon cake on the dessert table and the worst of the three, dense and damp, with a glaze that had gone clear as wet tissue under the gallery lights; it had been annoying me since I walked in. Three separate people had baked for my father, and all three had thought of lemon. Somewhere, I assumed, there was a pamphlet.

The upper gallery had been emptied of its cases for the afternoon, all but a long run of Coptic textiles bolted to the floor. The food stood on folding tables down the middle: foil trays of baked ziti, a crockpot of meatballs with its cord taped to the parquet, a sheet cake from the Acme with a trowel piped on it in chocolate icing. Sterno burned blue under the chafing dishes; a woman from the dean's office kept lifting the lids to check on things that did not need checking. At the far end a slide carousel was working its way through forty years of my father's trenches. It clicked, and a wall of mud brick in Cyprus came up, and held, and slid away into black while the projector fan hummed through the gap.

I had spent most of the forty minutes beside the textiles, watching the room the way I watched a cracked pot that had come up from storage, to see where the strain had gone. It had gone into the dean, who jingled the change in his pocket whenever he heard *Emilio*. It had gone into the graduate students, who stood in a clump by the meatballs and laughed too loudly once and then not at all. My mother had sent lilies from Phoenix, with a card that said, "Thinking of you, sweetheart, today and always." That was true. It was also more than she had said about my father since 1981.

Doug Fessler had given the eulogy. He had dug beside my father for eleven seasons in Cyprus and two in Sicily. He had flown in the night before from a winter season in Jordan, his forehead and nose burned the pink of boiled ham. When he leaned into the microphone, a flake of skin lifted on the bridge of his nose and stayed up, trembling. I watched it for most of what he said.

"Anybody who dug with Emilio Reyes knows what I mean when I say the man was generous with his time," he said. "He'd give you a night. He'd give you a whole season if you needed one. You'd come to him with a problem in your section and he'd climb down into it with you, into the mud, in his good shirt, and he'd stay down there until it made sense. I saw him give a whole Sunday to a kid from the village who wanted to know if the bones in our trench were his grandfather's." He looked at his notes, and lost his place, and found it. "Generous with his time," he said again, with his palm on the lectern, and all around the room people nodded at the floor.

Afterward the woman from the dean's office found me by the textiles with a paper plate, a square of lemon cake on it and a white plastic fork laid across the top.

"You haven't eaten," she said. "I've been watching you. You have to eat something."

"Is there coffee yet?"

"Have the lemon. It's Carol's, or else the slide library's, I can never keep them straight." She put the plate into my hands, and my hands, which had been doing nothing, closed on it. "They tell you no food in the galleries. The registrar nearly had a stroke. I said, Frank, it's Emilio, what's he going to do, put us in a case?" She laughed and stopped, alarmed at herself, and tipped her face up toward the ceiling. "Is that the Sterno? If the detectors go off, the whole building empties." She was already moving off toward the food. "Eat that," she said over her shoulder.

I had been holding the plate ever since.

The carousel had reached Sicily when I put the cake in my pocket. I knew those slides. I had seen them at our kitchen table every September of my childhood, projected onto a bedsheet thumbtacked over the china cabinet, my father's shadow crossing a temple when he leaned in to point. If I stayed for the Sicily tray I would stay for all of them, then for the coffee, then for the foil going down over the ziti and the line at the elevators where everyone would want to hold my hands.

I got as far as the meatballs. The dean stepped into my path there and took my hand in both of his.

"Mara. We'll be in touch about the lecture fund. Something in his name, every spring. He'd have hated it." He smiled at that, pleased with himself for knowing it. "Do you need a ride anywhere?"

"No. Thank you."

I got as far as the chafing dishes.

"There you are." Doug had a paper plate with a single stick of celery on it. Up close the sunburn had a rim, a pale line across his forehead where his hat had sat for six weeks. "Tell me I didn't go on."

"You didn't go on."

"I cut a whole page. There was a story about Paphos, eighty-four, I had a sherd I couldn't place, some ugly piece of local ware, and he sat up with me in the dig house until two in the morning going through the type series. He had a flight at seven." "I cut it because I'd have cried. I'm in a borrowed tie. Did you notice? It's my brother-in-law's. He sells insurance in it. Royal Jordanian lost my duffel at Kennedy, every context sheet from the season, and they keep telling me it's in Amman." He put the celery in his mouth and spoke around it. "It isn't in Amman. It's in a cage in Queens, I'd put money on it."

"I'm sorry about your sheets."

"No, God, listen to me. Today." He took the celery out again and frowned at it. "If you need anything. Boxes, his offprints, anything in the office. I can do the office."

"Thank you."

"Are you keeping the house?"

"Your nose is peeling."

He touched it and looked at his fingertip. "The last week of every season he'd start in about that house. The porch. He was going to jack up the porch, he had it drawn out on the back of a context sheet. Every season. He talked about you, too, you know. On site. All the time."

I said thank you again, which was what you said, and he patted my arm twice and went back toward the screen, where Sicily had given way to Turkey.

My coat was on a rolling rack by the elevators, and beside the rack, at the table with the guest book, stood my cousin Gloria in a navy suit. To reach the coat I would have had to pass her and be looked at. I went the other way, through the door marked STAFF and down the back stairs, poured concrete painted battleship gray, the handrail wrapped in electrical tape where the paint had worn through. The stairs let out onto a loading dock on the south side of the building, where a security guard was eating a soft pretzel under the overhang and it was sleeting.

He stopped chewing and took in my jacket.

"You want me to call you a cab?"

"I'll find one."

"You won't, in this."

I went down the ramp into the weather.

* * *

I found one on Spruce Street, after two blocks with my shoulders up around my ears and the sleet working into my collar like cold sand. The cab smelled of wet wool and a cardboard pine tree; the driver had a man on the radio talking about roses. I gave him Pine Street and Tenth and sat back with the cake squashed against my hip.

"Shopping?" the driver said.

"Shopping."

"In this." He turned the radio up a notch.

On the radio the man said burlap, you leave the burlap on them till the forsythia comes out, and I wondered whether burlap and hessian, which the English on my father's digs called it, were the same cloth with two passports, and whether hessian had anything to do with the soldiers, and then we were over the river and the water below the bridge was the color of an old nickel.

"Where on Pine?" the driver said, at Tenth.

"Here's fine."

"Here's a hydrant." He pulled up at it anyway.

Antique Row was three blocks of narrow fronts with their lights on against the afternoon. I walked it slowly and looked into each window long enough to fog it. A tall-case clock with a replaced finial; I could see the new wood from the sidewalk, too red and too sharp. A pair of Staffordshire dogs, one of them restored badly at the ear, the paint gone the wrong white. Then, in a window lettered in flaking gold, I. SZABO · MAPS · PRINTS · SMALL ANTIQUES, a Chinese export platter on a wire stand with a crack across it and four iron staples bridging the crack. Somebody had drilled half through the porcelain from the back, a little way into the body and not a hair farther, then laid the staples in hot and let them shrink. It had been done perhaps a hundred and fifty years ago by a man with a bow drill and a steady hand who would have been out of work in my department in a week, because you could see it from the street. I stood on the wet sidewalk and could have kissed him.

The door was locked; there was a buzzer. I pressed it and a woman in the back of the shop looked up from the counter and studied me through the glass for long enough that I began to feel the sleet in my shoes. A speaker over the door crackled.

"Yes? What is it?"

"To look," I said to the speaker.

The lock buzzed.

The shop was deep and narrow; it smelled of foxed paper and the hot dust on a space heater, whose orange bars glowed by the counter. Framed prints hung frame to frame on both walls, botanical plates, a Hogarth, the Battery in 1840; the back of the room was flat-file cabinets, gray steel, the kind every library sold off in the seventies. On a shelf behind the counter a small television played a college basketball game with the sound off. The woman at the counter was in her seventies, with a white braid pinned flat around her head, a liver-colored cardigan and fingerless gloves. She did not get up.

"You are dripping," she said.

"Sorry."

"Do not be sorry. Stand on the mat."

I stood on the mat. "The platter in the window. The stapled one. Could I see it?"

"Chinese export, about eighteen-twenty. It broke, and then a man put holes in it. Stapled does not sell. Americans want a thing like new or they want it in the garbage. You can see it from there."

"The holes don't go all the way through."

She looked at me properly then, over the top of a magnifier she had pushed up into her hair, and her eyes stopped on the pocket where the napkin was showing. "No," she said. "They do not. You do this for money."

"I work at the museum. In the conservation—"

"Museums do not shop on Pine Street." She turned a page of the ledger in front of her. "And you are not buying a platter in that jacket."

"What's wrong with the jacket?"

"Nothing. It is a good jacket for standing a long time in a room."

"I came from work," I said.

"On a Saturday."

"Conservators work Saturdays."

She made a small sound that was not quite a laugh. I took my wet shoes on a tour of the walls; my feet began, slowly, to hurt.

"Do you have anything Greek?"

I had not known I was going to ask it.

"Greek what?"

"Anything."

She considered that for longer than it deserved.

"Greek coins, fake. Greek pots, worse, because a man in Athens made them last spring and buried them in his garden for a month so they would come up dirty. I have one chart. Real, and ruined."

She walked to the flat files at the speed of a woman who had decided years before not to hurry for customers, ran her gloved finger down the labels, and drew out one of the wide shallow drawers with a steel shriek. What she lifted out lay between sheets of yellowed tissue. She carried it back on her forearms, laid it on the glass of the counter, and turned the tissue back the way you turned back a sheet from a face.

"Hold that corner," she said. "Not with the wet hand."

I held it with the dry one; she held the other end, and between us lay the Gulf of Corinth.

It was a copperplate engraving on a sheet perhaps twenty inches wide, and the plate had been pressed so hard into the paper that the edge of it still showed as a shallow beveled step on all four sides. It had been colored by hand soon after printing, the land in a wash of yellow ochre, the coastlines edged in green. The green was verdigris, copper salts in gum, a cheap brilliant pigment that ate whatever it was laid on; over three hundred years it had eaten. All along the northern shore of the gulf the paper under the green line had gone brittle and brown and then simply gone, so that the coast was perforated in long fine slots like the edge of a stamp. You could have torn Phocis off along it.

"Your green's verdigris," I said.

"I know what my green is. Three hundred years it eats. Another hundred, it finishes."

The lettering was Italian, cut in a clear upright hand. GOLFO DI LEPANTO across the water; *Salona* inland, on a river. Above the water on the north side ran a range of hachured mountains, each one a tufted hump like a sheep seen from behind. Among them was *M. Parnaso*, and below it a cluster of four tiny houses and the word *Castri*.

I lifted my corner a few inches toward the lamp, carefully, and the watermark swam up in the paper: three crescent moons in a row, diminishing.

"*Tre lune*," Mrs. Szabo said. "You know it."

"Venetian mills."

"Venetian mills, or a Dutchman pretending to be one. This one is not pretending."

In blue ballpoint, over the mountains above Castri, someone had written two lines of coordinates, 38° 31′ N and 22° 32′ E, and under them, in square capitals pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through, BIBLIOTHECA? Beside it was a doorway the size of my littlest fingernail, drawn freehand, an arch on two posts with the inside scribbled dark. I turned the sheet over at the corner and on the back the words stood up out of the paper in reverse, embossed, legible with a fingertip.

"Those are coordinates," I said.

"Those are numbers in ballpoint. That is all I know about them." She sniffed. "Morosini's war. Sixteen eighty-seven, sixteen eighty-eight. The Venetians wanted the Morea and they sent their navy and their navy drew the coast, every bay, with a lead line over the side. The same people who blew up the Parthenon. They were very good at coasts." She tapped the gulf with one gloved finger, near Lepanto, not touching the paper.

"You bought it for the coast."

"The coast is right. A faker gives you the coast out of his head and it is a pretty coast and it is wrong. And a faker writes *Delfi* there, big, because Delphi sells. In sixteen eighty-seven it is a village of goats on top of the ruins. *Castri*. They write what is there."

"Kastri," I said. "They moved the village off the site in the nineties. The eighteen-nineties. The French paid."

She let the French go by. "You know Greek."

"I read it."

"You know what a man with a ballpoint pen does to a thing like this."

"He takes it from four thousand dollars to nothing."

"Not to nothing," she said. "To what I paid. Estate sale, Chestnut Hill, last October. Margaret Fenn. Old lady, no children, a house full of boxes."

"What was she like?"

"She was dead. The lawyer was not nice. He wanted the house empty by Sunday, and he stood in the kitchen with his car keys while we carried." She nodded at the ballpoint. "That is the brother. Treasure hunter." She said it in the voice another woman might have kept for *bookie*. "He went to Greece in sixty-eight and he did not come back. The sister kept his things. I buy boxes. I do not buy stories about the boxes."

"What was he looking for?"

"You read Latin also."

"Yes."

"You have read it." She kept her eyes off the ink. "A library. There is one library people dig for. Once a month a man parks a car with a metal detector in the back seat outside my door and asks me for a map to Alexandria, or else to Captain Kidd. Sometimes it is the same man."

"The library at Alexandria burned."

"Everything burned. Still they come with a pen and say, not everything, not this. It is under a mountain. Or it is down a well behind a grandmother's house, and the grandmother wants money. The one with the metal detector reads better than you would think. He is just very sure."

The question mark after BIBLIOTHECA did not look sure.

She reached under the counter and set a cigar box down beside the chart, a Phillies box, the paper label worn soft at the corners. "This comes with it. I do not split a lot."

I put my thumb under the lid. Inside, on a bed of loose tobacco crumbs that still faintly smelled of something, lay a notebook with a black oilcloth cover, the kind with an elastic band, though the elastic had perished and lay across it in two dry pieces. The cover said A. J. FENN in the same square capitals. It was warped from damp and dried again; I closed the lid.

"I do not read notebooks," she said. "A notebook belongs to the family."

"You said there wasn't any family."

"Then it is for whoever pays." She slid the box an inch toward me. "The rest was nothing. Navy uniforms. Forty years of *National Geographic*, which I could not give away. Also a diving helmet with no glass in it. That I sold to a seafood restaurant in Wildwood, and they put a lobster in it. The box is free. The chart is fourteen hundred."

"It's been defaced."

"It has been defaced. That is why it is fourteen hundred and not four thousand."

"The verdigris has gone through the coast in six places. One more winter in that drawer and you'll have two maps."

"Seven places," she said. "Fourteen hundred."

"Five hundred."

"No. A dealer says five hundred and goes to the door. You have not moved."

"Who else is going to buy it? A collector won't touch it with the pen on it."

"A collector will not. A decorator will. They put it in a gold frame over a sofa in Bryn Mawr. Who reads ink over a sofa?"

The coast that had cost a navy lay between us under the lamp.

"Eight," I said.

"Eight is what I paid. I am not a charity for museums." She glanced up at the screen. "I have five dollars on this game with the barber next door," she told it. "The barber says the ones in red. His nephew played for them." The man in white shot and missed. "Twelve hundred. Because you will not roll it. You held the corner like a person who has been shouted at. Twelve."

"Eleven."

"Eleven fifty. I give you a board for it, and you go home."

I said yes. I had said it before I had checked anything at all, my account or my rent or the fact that my father's estate was sitting in a lawyer's office on Walnut Street waiting for a form.

"I do not take checks from people I do not know," she said.

"I don't have eleven hundred and fifty dollars in my pocket."

"And in your pocket?"

"Cake. Lemon."

"I did not ask what kind." She looked at the napkin a moment longer and then pulled a receipt pad toward her. "License number across the top. Telephone underneath. Name."

"Mara Reyes."

She wrote it on the pad and underlined the surname. "There was a Reyes in the *Inquirer*. In January. A professor who dug things."

"It's a common name."

"It is a common name," she agreed. Under it she wrote *One chart, Venetian, defaced, with box*, and pushed a Bic at me across the glass.

The date went in first, then her name, which she spelled for me without being asked, then the amount in figures and in words, *Eleven hundred fifty and 00/100*. I signed it and copied my license number off the card. Then I wrote the phone number. I wrote it fast, in an order my hand had known since before I could spell, and it was the number of my father's house.

I looked at it. The pen had gone in hard on the last digit and dented the paper.

Mrs. Szabo had gone back to the flat files to shut the drawer. It went in with the same shriek. I could have voided the check and written another. My eyes had gone hot and blurred, so that the blue numbers swam in their little boxes, and I held the pen very still above the check and waited for them to stop swimming, and they did not stop. "It's this heater," I said, to nobody. The heater ticked. After a while I tore the check out along its perforations, cleanly, to the last tooth, and slid it across the glass with the wrong number on it.

"The check is good?"

"Yes."

She compared the signature with the license, folded the check once and put it in the cardigan pocket. Then she cut two pieces of gray mat board on a board cutter older than both of us and laid the chart between them in its tissue. She taped the edges with brown paper tape and put the whole parcel inside a black garbage bag against the weather. She started to fold the end of the bag over and stopped, because I had put my hand out.

"It won't bend like that," I said.

"It will not bend at all if you carry it flat," she said, "which you will, to your door, in this weather, without a coat." She put the cigar box in a paper sack from the Acme. "That is your business."

On the television the men in white had won, or lost; a coach was shaking hands down a line. She spoke a word to it in Hungarian, quietly, and took a five-dollar bill out of the register and laid it beside the ledger to wait for the barber.

"Pull the door hard," she said, without turning around. "It sticks."

* * *

I took the trolley back, because I had eleven dollars and a check register I did not want to open, and walked down from Thirty-Third Street with the black bag held flat against my front like a tray of drinks. By the time I came up the museum steps it was nearly five and the sleet had turned to plain rain. The upper gallery smelled of coffee and Sterno and wet coats; the caterers were folding the tables. The dean's-office woman went by with an armful of foil pans and doubled back.

"Did you eat that cake?"

"I ate it," I said, with it in my pocket.

"Good girl," she said, and went on toward the elevators. The carousel had run out of slides, and the projector fan was still going.

Gloria was at the coat rack with a roll of foil. She was wrapping the leftover ziti in individual squares, quick hard folds with the heel of her hand, stacking them into a shopping bag; she kept working when I came in. She had both her shoes off; they stood side by side under the rack, navy pumps, one heel worn to the plastic.

"I wore two different earrings all day," she said. She held out her palm. In it lay a pearl stud and a small gold hoop. "All day. The priest looked right at me during the reading. Not a word out of the whole pew. Ray said, when I asked him, he said he thought it was a style." She closed her hand. "A style. Fourteen years."

"I didn't notice."

"You didn't notice because you were looking at the ceiling." She tore another square of foil. "Where'd you go?"

"Out."

Then she looked at me.

"There's a pencil in your hair."

"The clip broke."

"In the car?"

"In the car. A pencil was what there was."

Her eyes went from the pencil to the garbage bag held against my chest. She kept looking. It was a look I knew from the age of nine. She had been sent to stay with us the summer her mother had the twins, and she had given me exactly this look through the screen door when my father's taxi pulled away for Sicily and I went straight back inside to finish my cereal. She did not say anything at all.

"It's a map," I said.

"I didn't ask."

"It's a Venetian chart, sixteen eighties. The Venetians sounded the whole coast with a—"

"Okay." She went back to the foil. "How much?"

"Not much."

"You've got the check face. You had it at the Volvo place." She creased a square in half with her thumbnail. "Did you eat anything?"

"I have cake."

"I bet it's in your pocket." She set another square in the bag. "Doug Fessler came looking for you. I said ladies' room. Two hours, the ladies' room. And Ray's been sitting in the car with the engine running since the reading, so. Take these to the house. There's no room in my freezer and Ray won't eat ziti two days running, he says it sits." She pushed the shopping bag toward me with her stockinged foot. "And do the machine."

"What machine?"

"His machine. Tía Nilda called the house Thursday. She forgot, she's seventy-eight, she dialed him up to tell him what time the memorial was. And there he is, telling her to leave a message." Gloria's hands stopped on the foil for a second and went on. "She had to go lie down. And it's full anyway, people call and it just beeps at them. Change the message. Put your own on it, or the plain one, the robot one. I don't care. Just so nobody gets him. You still have a key?"

"I have a key."

"So go." She found my coat on the rack without looking, unhooked it, and held it open behind me, low, at the height you held a coat for a child.

* * *

The house was on a narrow street off Baltimore Avenue, the fourth in a row of twelve, with a porch that sagged toward the middle like a hammock and a front window that still had the radiator under it. When I was small I used to kneel on the radiator cover in my socks to watch the corner for taxis. The grille printed little diamonds on both my knees, and they took an hour to fade. It was nearly dark when I got there; the streetlight in front of the house had come on and was humming. The key stuck in the lock the way it always had and turned when I lifted the door by the knob, the way it always had, and I went in with the rain behind me.

Two months of mail lay on the hall floor in a drift. I stepped over it.

In the kitchen I put the black bag flat on the table and the paper sack beside it. When I opened the refrigerator to put away the ziti, it was full already: foil and Tupperware and a Pyrex dish of something under plastic wrap, with a strip of masking tape on the lid that said DOROTHY / RETURN DISH. The neighbors had been in, with a key I did not know about. I stood in the white light of the refrigerator with Gloria's shopping bag, moving a jar of pickles and a carton of eggs with a January date on it to make room. I put the ziti in on top of Dorothy's dish, and it was still a little warm through the foil and I put it in anyway.

I took off the wet coat; in the jacket pocket under it the napkin had glued itself to the lemon cake. I peeled it off in pieces, standing at the counter, and I ate the cake with my fingers in four bites without sitting down. It was good. It was much better than it had looked, sharp and soft, and the glaze cracked when I bit it, and I was annoyed about that too.

The answering machine sat on the counter beside the wall phone, where I had put it in December of 1991. It was the Panasonic with two little smoked-plastic doors, one cassette for the greeting and one for the messages, because the man in the store had said the two-tape kind was easier for older people and my father had been offended for a week. Its red light was blinking fast. I pressed PLAY and leaned on the counter. The tape rewound with a thin whine, and clicked.

"Emilio, it's Pat. From next door. Oh, God, I'm sorry, I'm calling the house. I don't know why I'm calling the house. Mara, honey, if you get this, I've got your Tupperware, I mean his, the blue one. He shoveled my walk. When he was home. Okay. Bye now."

Beep.

"This is the Free Library of Philadelphia, Walnut Street West branch, with a message for Emilio Reyes. The following items are overdue." A woman's recorded voice, then a man's recorded voice reading a title, flat as a train station: "*Wild Horses*." Then the woman again, cheerful. "Please return items at your earliest convenience."

Beep. It was a Dick Francis. He read them on planes.

"Emilio! It's me. Nicosia." A man, shouting cheerfully over a bad line. "It rains here like a punishment, and I found your drawings from Kourion, the whole box, ninety-two, under my bed. Can you believe it? Under my bed. What do you want I do with them? I send, or you come and get? You come and get. We eat at the place by the walls. Call me."

Beep. He had not heard. There was no reason he should have; it was Nicosia, and it was raining.

"Hi, this is the department office, this is for Mara Reyes, I guess, or whoever's doing the arrangements. Upper gallery, Saturday the fourteenth, two o'clock. The coffee's taken care of. Thanks."

Beep. A woman who did not leave her name only breathed, said, "Oh, Emilio," and hung up. There was a long silence with a television in it. Then a crackle like paper burning.

"Ah. Hello. This message is for the family of Professor Reyes. Ms. Reyes, I hope. It's Halloran, ringing from Naples." A pause; the line hissed. "I'm very sorry indeed to be ringing about paperwork, of all things, and I'm sorry, I should have rung sooner, since January I've been meaning to. The difficulty is the permit. The *permesso* for the Campanian survey season is still in Emilio's name. The Soprintendenza want another name on it by the twentieth of April or the season lapses, and then of course one begins again from nothing, which takes, well. Years, sometimes. I wondered whether the family would be sending anyone. Anyone at all. It needn't be an archaeologist, strictly, for the paperwork. Or you could fax, if it's easier, the number at the office is zero-eight-one, five-five-two, oh, I'll give it again at the end. I'll try you again. It's six hours later here than with you, which I always forget, so forgive me if I've woken anyone. Oh, the fax. No. I'll ring. Well."

Beep. "End of messages," said a woman's synthesized voice.

I wrote nothing down. When I pressed ERASE and held it, the second tape whined back to its beginning; the light stopped blinking and went to a solid red. That was half of what Gloria had asked. The other half was a button on the left marked GREETING, under its own little door. I knew which one it was. I had shown him.

I pressed it.

The greeting tape clicked and turned. There was a breath first, a hiss of room, and a radio somewhere in the house as it had been six years ago, playing something with violins.

"Hello, you've reached the Reyes house, which means you've reached a machine, because I'm out somewhere digging a hole in somebody else's country. Or I'm in the basement looking for the Christmas lights. Tell me who you are and where you're calling from, and say it slowly, and I'll find you." And then, a little away from the microphone, lower, in the voice he used when he thought only I could hear: "Mara? Which one stops it?"

Click. Beep. The machine waited for me to speak.

I stood with my hand on the counter. Outside a car went by slowly in the rain with its radio on, and the sound of it came up through the floor and went away down the street.

I pressed it again and listened to the breath at the beginning this time, and to the violins, which were Brahms or something like Brahms. In the middle, where he said *somebody else's country*, he smiled; you could hear the smile; it pulled the vowels wide. I listened to my name. I had been twenty-six and standing beside him at this counter with the cardboard box from the store and the instructions open, and I had pointed, and he had pressed the wrong button, and I had laughed, and he had not asked to do it over, then or ever.

Beep. The machine waited.

The third time, I pressed GREETING and listened to him with my finger on the button below it, the one marked RECORD GREETING. It needed only pressing. Hold the button and speak after the tone, the instructions had said, beside a drawing of a little man with a speech balloon. You have reached the Reyes house. Please leave a message. Anyone could have said it. Gloria could have said it in her stocking feet.

"Mara?" he said. "Which one stops it?"

I took my finger off the button. I opened the little smoked door with my thumbnail. The microcassette sat in its well, no bigger than a matchbook, its two white hubs and the brown ribbon of tape stretched between them behind a window of clear plastic. I pressed the eject lever and it came up into my fingers. It weighed less than the cake had. I read the label, which was blank, and put it in my jacket pocket where the napkin had been, and buttoned the pocket.

Then I went upstairs, because it was the next thing.

His bedroom was cold; the radiator had been turned down. The bed was made, badly, with the spread pulled up over the pillows in a long lump. His 1966 Seiko lay on the nightstand where he had always taken it off at night, face up on a folded handkerchief, its steel band coiled beside it like a dropped ribbon. The hands said ten past four. It had stopped at some ten past four in January, morning or afternoon, and had not been wound since.

The case was scratched in fine circles where it had knocked against thirty-odd years of trench walls, and the crystal had a nick at two o'clock. My thumbnail found the crown. I turned it forward between finger and thumb, feeling the small dry ratchet of the mainspring taking up, and when it grew stiff I stopped. The second hand twitched and went on. I held it to my ear and it was ticking, quick and light. Then I set it by the red numbers of the clock radio and buckled it on. It was far too big; it slid down over the knuckle of my thumb, and I pushed it back up and it slid down again.

His Pentax sat on the shelf over the bureau, between a Cypriot lamp with a chipped nozzle and a row of paperbacks swollen from the bathtub. It was the black-and-chrome K1000, its worn strap wound around it in the same three turns for as long as he'd had it. I took it down; it had the weight of a good brick and smelled of leatherette and light machine oil. I lifted the rewind knob and the back sprang open on nothing; the film chamber was empty and clean, the pressure plate bright. I closed it. The advance lever went through its stroke with the same soft ratcheting sound the watch had made, a size larger, and I put it back on the shelf with the strap turned as he had left it.

Downstairs I did not turn on the kitchen light. Rain ran on the window over the sink. The garbage bag lay flat on the table with the chart inside it between its boards. Beside it the grocery sack had slumped open, and the Phillies box showed at the top, square and brown, the notebook in it with the dead elastic across its cover. The watch ticked loose against the bone of my wrist. On the counter the machine had found nothing in the well where its greeting belonged, and its light had changed from a steady red to a slow blink. Every second or so the red light came on and went off, and each time it lit the lid of the cigar box.
