Bells came ahead of the light, the herdsman's ewes moving about the hollow's farther end in the dark and cropping as they went, so that their bells sounded in no order at all, one, then three together, then a long space of nothing; behind the bells came the cold, which on that flank of Parnassus rose from the turf at the end of every night however hot the day before had been; and behind the cold came the thyme, crushed under my own feet as I picked my way past last night's fire to the edge of the grass, where the dew had soaked the straps of my sandals by the tenth step and grayed every stem as far down the hill as the eye went. The summit took the sun first and held it, gold, while everything under it was still blue.

The light worked down the long screes a stride at a time, finding the firs on the western ridge, then the hut, then a hump under a cloak in the lee of the woodstack that was Felix, who slept out every night from choice and had never yet been awake for this. Below the hut the string stood at its hobbles, cropping in a line with their backs to the cold. Valens was already among them, going down the line with a hand laid on each rump in turn as he came to it, so that no mule was surprised by him; at the black he stopped to lift her near fore and look at it.

"Is it hot again?" I called down.

"Cool. She'll do the troughs." He let the foot go and went on.

We had been up on the grazing since the snow left the high ground. In the month after the consultation a stranger had sat most of a morning on the step at the lion spouts in Delphi, with a rag tied round his head under his hat and a Corinthian's way with his vowels, asking the women at the jars which house kept the tall foreigner with something heavy and bright inside her clothes. Kallia had answered him herself from under a brimming jar, with half the lane listening.

"Gone," she had said. "Gone to Kirrha at the turn of the month and onto a boat for home, the god go with her. She ate more than she carried, that one, and broke two of my jars."

That night Kallia packed my bag for me in the storeroom, folding each thing smaller than I would have folded it. Before light I was at Valens's yard, and on the evening the mules started up for the summer pasture I followed them in a carter's hood, walking behind the dun, which carried Valens's fig in its sawn-off jar, roped upright between two water skins and nodding at each step.

It was July now, by my own count, which nobody else on the mountain kept.

Below the edge of the grass the mountain fell away in long steps of rock and scrub to the plateau, which lay at the bottom of them as flat and pale as the lake bed it had once been. On its opposite edge, where it ended against a low brow of cliff, a patch of shade stayed where it was while the light moved; I had been inside it once since the consultation.

Valens had been waiting below the cliffs on the morning that followed, with two mules and a bundle of split pine, and we climbed with nothing said between us that was not about the path.

"Keep to the inside at the third turn," he said, at the second. "It's ice under the wet there." And at the top: "The snow's rotten in the hollows. Walk where it's gray."

The plateau was still half under snow, gray and sunk in the hollows, with water running beneath it everywhere, so that the whole plateau ticked and trickled like a kitchen when the heat comes on in a cold house. At the cave, melt was coming off the lip of rock above the entrance in a broken, glittering sheet; we passed under it with our heads down and were through with our shoulders soaked.

I had brought the flashlight, which all winter had lain in the deepest corner of my bag; that morning I had put it uppermost.

The daylight went farther in than I had ever seen it go, with the snow at the mouth throwing it in. Valens followed with a pine he had not troubled to light, and far in, where the roof dropped and the floor climbed to meet it, the dim gave out, and I dug in the bag for the flashlight and slid the switch forward.

The beam was yellower than it had been in May, and narrower, with a brown edge where the batteries had begun to tire. I shone it along the crack, whose far end had always been black to me. The beam reached past the bend and stopped a yard beyond, on a face of gray stone that closed the crack from wall to wall. The stone sweated, and along its foot lay a crust of something pale and soft-looking, like old bread; on its face, in a clot the size of two fists, hung a crowd of daddy longlegs, all threads and joints, which began in the light to come apart, unhurriedly, one leg at a time.

"That is the end of it," I said. "It goes no further."

"Was it ever further?"

"Once."

That was the whole of the crack: a yard of it past the bend, a wall, the spiders, and behind the wall the mountain going on into itself as far as the mountain went.

Valens's steps had halted when the beam showed; presently I heard them start again, and he arrived at my shoulder and watched what the beam was lying on, the wet stone and the long legs moving over it, while I held it there; the thing in my hand he left to me.

It was shut, as I had wished it, and I switched the light off, and we stood together in the dark, and I waited to see what I would feel about it. The melt had gone through my himation at the shoulders and lay cold down my spine. I began to shiver with it, slowly, from the middle outward, then hard enough that he heard my teeth.

"The water's coming off that lip faster than it was," he said in the dark. "We want to be out under it before it's on us properly, and off the cliff while the sun's still on the turns, or they'll freeze under us."

On the way down he spoke of the path, and in Delphi of the mules; since then, four months, not a word about the light.

Below me now the shade under the cliff was going, and the plateau was gold from edge to edge. I left the grass and made my way down to the fold, where the herdsman, Mnason, sat with the pails.

The fold was a ring of thorn hurdles on the floor of the hollow, with one gap in it the width of a ewe. He sat at the gap on a slab of rock, in a goatskin cap with the ears still on it, and as the ewes came through he caught each by a hind leg and milked her from behind into a pail between his feet, her tail switching at his face and his face not caring; I had the stone opposite. In two months I had learned to do it badly and then less badly, to take the kicks on my forearm rather than in the pail, and to talk.

"Talk to her," Mnason had said over the gap on my first day there, and on every morning after. "Talk, and she lets down. Sing, if there's nothing in your head. It makes no odds what."

My ewes heard about whatever came into my head, in Greek, and took it no worse than his. The milk rang on the bottom of the pail and then hissed into the froth, and the steam stood up off it in the chill.

"Not her." A ewe with a torn ear had come through on his side of the gap. "She kicked me at the shearing. You have her."

She kicked me too, once, on the forearm, and then let down for me as sweetly as any of them while I described a snowplow to her.

When the last ewe had gone through, he poured both pails into the great pot outside his own hut and peered into it for some time with his cap pushed back.

"That boy of yours sleeps like the dead," he said. "A wolf could walk off with him, and your man would be up at those mules with his back to it. I've said so. Nobody listens to me up here but ewes." He wiped his knife on his thigh. "Wants turning."

He crossed the hollow toward our hut, where Valens's fig stood in its jar on the sunny side of the wall, inside a ring of stones built round it against the mules. Valens was there before him, with the string left to itself behind him.

"A twig," said Mnason. "Off the end, there, where it'll grow again. My mother turned her milk with fig, and hers did."

"Cut your own."

"There's no fig nearer than the gorge, and that's half a morning down and the whole of one back up."

"Then it's the gorge."

"One twig." He measured it for him, a finger's length, with his thumbnail. "You'd not miss it."

"It would."

The jar was between them. The tree was two hands taller than it had been in the yard below Delphi. It had put out leaves as broad as a man's spread fingers, and under the leaves a dozen figs the size of olives, green and hard and furred.

Mnason went to his own hut and fetched out a dried kid's stomach on a string. He cut a piece of it into the milk and hung over the pot, and told me about it in a voice I had not heard him use to the ewes.

"My mother never did it so. Fig, she turned it with, every day she lived, and it set as sweet as a girl. This'll taste of kid. You'll put it in your mouth and taste the kid in it, and you'll know whose doing that was."

"He's had his eye on that tree," Valens said, once the old fellow was out of earshot, "since we got here."

"He wants to make cheese."

"He'll be over those stones some night."

Mnason was sixty if he was a day, and could not, to my knowledge, cross the hollow in the dark without walking into his own fold. Valens touched the figs in turn with a fingertip, lifting the leaves to come at them. Felix had drifted across from the woodstack with his cloak still round him and stood watching that, instead of going for the water skins.

"When are they ripe?"

"End of the summer."

"What's in them now? If I had one now, what would it do to me?"

"There's a milk in them while they're green. It takes the skin from your mouth."

"Does it? Did it yours? Would it take a mule's?"

"Eat one and find out." Valens left us for the mules.

* * *

At noon Felix drove the string down to the troughs under the plateau's rim, which was half a day's errand there and back, and Mnason lay down in the shade of his hurdles with his cap over his face, and the hollow belonged to the flies.

Valens was shaving, as was his habit every few days at midday, sitting on the woodstack with a bowl of water beside him and the knife he had taken in the ditch, by feel, with no glass. He drew the blade up his throat against the grain in short scrapes, rinsed it, went over the place with the flat of his left hand, and moved on. I sat against the hut wall in its strip of shade with a sandal of Felix's that had lost its sole on the scree.

"He'll walk the next one off as well." Valens kept the knife going.

"He goes down a scree as a goat would, if a goat were in a hurry and wore sandals," I said.

The sun stood straight overhead, hot enough that the thyme smelled from where it grew, with nobody treading on it.

"The light in the cave," he said, between strokes.

The flashlight lay in the bag in the hut, uppermost, as it had ridden since spring. I went in for it, and on my return he finished the stroke he was on, rinsed the blade and wiped both hands dry on his tunic before he would accept it. It was gray plastic, ridged along the barrel for the grip, with a switch that slid under the thumb, and it weighed about what a hen's egg weighs. He held it a little off from his body in the whole of his hand, the way he held anything small that was not his.

"Under your thumb," I said. "Push."

It lit, and in the noon sun it made nothing anyone could see; then he cupped his other palm over the front of it, and his fingers lit red along their edges, all of them, with the dark of the bones down their middles. The batteries were the kind with the pink rabbit on the packet, the one in sunglasses who went drumming through other people's commercials and would not stop. He lifted the palm and laid it on again, and the red came and went in his hand.

"No fire," he said.

"None."

"What's in it, then?"

"Two tubes, with something shut up in them that makes the light out of itself, bit by bit, till there is none left."

He found the switch again with his thumb and slid it back, and the red left his fingers.

"What's it for, there? In your country."

"For the dark. For finding the keyhole when you come home late, and the fuse box when the lights go out."

"Then don't burn it," he said, and returned it to me.

In all the weeks of the grazing I had told it to myself a dozen ways, walking behind the string or sitting at the gap in the fold with my head full of sheep, and none of the ways began where I began now.

"I never came off any ship," I said.

"Nobody thought you had."

"Or out of Syracuse. Kallia made Syracuse."

"I know that too."

"You said a name on the cliff," he said. "In the snow, the day we went up. Philadelphia."

"That is my city."

"In Asia."

"Further than Asia. Further than anywhere you have heard of, and that is the smaller part of it." The flashlight lay in my lap. "Where I live, they count the years from the birth of a man in Judaea, and by their count the year I left was nineteen hundred and ninety-eight."

Half his face was shaved and half was not, and he turned the whole of it on me.

"Counted from what?"

"From a teacher in Judaea. The governor there had him killed, forty years ago or so, and his people went on talking about him after. Long after, half a thousand years after, a monk did the arithmetic back to his birth and started the count from there, and got it wrong by four years, most likely, but by then nobody minded."

"Judaea," he said. "Titus has just burned it."

"Not that part. That part goes on."

He waited, and I, who had begun with a monk's mistake and could not see how to get from there to anywhere, did the sum aloud, and that was worse still.

"From now," I said, "nineteen hundred years, and twenty-seven more. Twenty-six, and some months. I left in May. It is a month we have; it comes after April."

"I know May."

He still held the knife, with water running off the blade. A fly was walking on the rim of the bowl, and I watched the fly and told him the rest in one go, to have it out before he could stop me.

"The cave on the plateau," I said. "It had a door in it. For one afternoon. Nobody could have seen it; it was a cold, coming from the crack where no cold should be. I crawled in there in May, in my year, with my bag on my front and that light in my teeth, and I crawled out of it into yours, into October, with snow on the way. Then it shut. It has stayed shut. You have seen it shut, in the spring. You saw the spiders."

When the fly left the rim he was still looking at the cave's dark mark across the plateau, down there in the heat, until I thought he had not understood one word, and then he was facing me.

"Do they still have mules?"

"Yes," I said. "Fewer. In these mountains the olives still come down on them. At home there is a gorge so deep that the river at the bottom of it is a whole day's walk down, and people pay to be carried down it on mules, and back up, to look."

"Who leads them?"

"Men in hats. They walk in front."

He wet the knife and started on his left cheek.

"Who's emperor?" he said, under his ear.

"Nobody. No emperors anywhere. There are a few kings left, who cut ribbons on bridges and wave from carriages."

"Who's got the legions?"

"No legions either. Armies. Every country keeps its own."

"Who mends the roads?"

"Everybody pays a little to the town, and the town pays men to mend them, and they are never finished."

"Somebody's paid, then. And the god? Does he still sit, here?"

"His house fell in, centuries before I was born. An earthquake had some of it, and men wanting stone for their own walls had the rest. People come from everywhere to walk about in what is left of it, and they pay to."

"Who keeps the spring?"

"It still runs. There is a fence round it now, and you may not drink."

He shaved along the line of his cheekbone, where the graze from the road had healed into a pale seam no longer than a barley grain, and went round it. "Is the sea still salt?"

"Yes. Dirtier, and with fewer fish in it."

"Who's looking for you, there?"

"Nobody yet. My rent will come due, and somebody at my work will ring my telephone, and get my own voice on a tape, saying I am not at home."

"That's true, then."

He finished. He rinsed the knife, dried it on his tunic and sheathed it at his back, then sat with his clean face turned up into the heat, giving nothing away.

"And the heavy one?" he asked. "What the boy calls silver."

I wore it next to my skin still, out of habit. Up here no one would take it, but I had gone on wearing it as I had in Kallia's crowded house, and it had rubbed a raw patch on my breastbone that I had stopped noticing. I unslung it and gave him the camera by its body. He put the wrong end to his eye first and got nothing but his own lashes in the glass; then he turned it round and found the plateau, and the shadowed notch, and the cliff above it.

"It shows less than my eye does," he said.

"It keeps what it shows."

"Where?"

"Inside it, in the dark, on a strip as long as your arm. Nobody in this world can bring it out."

"The boy says it was your father's."

"It was."

"He'll be wanting it, then."

"He will not," I said.

His thumb went toward the glass, toward the dent in the rim where the man's head had been, and I had his wrist turned aside before it got there. One print on that glass and I might as well never look through it again. He handed the camera back and said nothing about my hand, and I slung it on again.

"At Alexandria there's a fellow," he said, "builds a temple door that opens by itself when the priest lights the fire on the altar. A shipmaster off the grain run saw it. Birds that sing with water in them. A wheel the steam turns."

"This was not made in Alexandria."

"It was made somewhere." He picked a thread of thyme off the woodstack and put it in his mouth. "And the way back. The god gave you a mountain and a tomb. And Theano? What was hers?"

"The town."

"Which?"

"Pompeii."

He tried it over. "Where's that?"

"In Campania. On the bay below Neapolis, below a mountain they grow wine on. A day from Puteoli by the coast road, or less."

"Neapolis I know of. Puteoli." He had a scuffed patch of earth before the woodstack, worn bare by his own boots, and he drew in it with the heel of one: the gulf, the heel of Italy, a great sweep round it, a mark. "There?"

"Further up. There."

He moved the mark. "What's in it? Who has it?"

"Two magistrates a year, elected; they paint the candidates' names on the house walls in letters a foot high, and the bakers vote for whoever likes bread. Fullers, bakers, a forum with a temple at one end, and the rooms inside painted a deep red, with birds and fruit on it. Tombs along the roads out of every gate. Somewhere among them, a door that is only a picture of a door."

The rest of what I knew stood behind the name, where it had stood since Theano's step: eight years on from this summer, and a few weeks over. I left it standing where it was; his question had been about the road.

"And you go in at this door," he said, "and come out in your year, counted from the Judaean."

"Yes."

"With the light, and the box with the eye in it."

"Yes."

"How will you know the door, among all those tombs?"

"If it is open, by the cold, as I knew it at the crack. If it is shut I shall not know it from any other wall."

He sucked the thyme stalk. Up on the summit a pair of choughs were playing in the wind over the rock, falling and catching themselves and calling. He had heard all of it, the count, the cave, the door in the town, and kept none of it as true; he had put it away as Kallia put away a lodger's tale of a sister who married a magistrate. I had told it as badly as a thing could be told, and might as well have held my tongue. I had not thought he would be kind about it.

"To get on a ship you need a man," he said.

"Yes."

"And somebody to stand in front of you when you land. In Italy a woman belonging to nobody is anybody's."

I hiccuped, out of nowhere, so that my shoulders jumped.

"Hold your breath," he said. "In Italy a man with no paper belongs to whoever says so. My paper's at Corinth. The book with my name in it."

"Celer is at Corinth."

"So's the brother. Her brother. His house has a porter on the door and a dog in the porch. The freedman told me which house. I'll stand up in the brother's court for his sister when it's heard, and he'll get me into the archive for it, and pay the road."

"You told the freedman no."

"That was last autumn."

"And if the record is not there? If Celer has had it taken out of the book?"

"Then there's the men who saw me get the sword. Half Corinth sat on those benches. They've not all died."

"And she will be in the house," I said. "His sister."

"It's her brother's house."

"That is not what I asked."

"She'll be in it."

I hiccuped again, through the breath I was holding.

"A court's full of people. Nobody brings a knife into a crowd that size," he said. "On a road there's three men." He picked the thyme from his teeth and looked at it. "When the grazing's done, in the autumn, we leave for Corinth. Before the rains. We winter there. The sea opens in the spring, and we sail. As man and wife."

The hiccup took me exactly on the last word of it, so that I seemed to answer him with it.

"As man and wife," I said.

"No shipmaster takes on a woman alone. He'll take a wife."

"Is that all it is? Something to say to the shipmaster?"

"It's what he'll ask," Valens said. "At the gangway."

"And in Corinth? All winter, in the brother's house? What am I there?"

"My wife. The porter will ask, and the brother, and his cook, and that's what they'll all be told."

"I meant to winter in Delphi." My voice was steadier than I was. "Euthydikos has a bowl for me when the god closes for the year. It would have paid my crossing."

"Delphi's put you on a boat. Leave yourself on it."

"And the money?"

"The brother pays his witness's road. There's the silver from the ford. And the string comes down with us. What's not wanted I'll sell at the Isthmus, where they'll pay for mules hardened on a mountain."

I had it in my head already: Euthydikos's bean-sheller off his stool by the bay tree, down at the lane gate some winter morning to say the bowl was ready for me, and Kallia at the pot telling him the Syracusan had sailed. The priest's bowl could keep its lead; it had kept it this far.

"And Felix?" I said.

"He comes."

I had kept the boy out of my plan for four months; he went into it now in two words, and stayed.

"When I find the door," I said, "I shall go in by it. You understand that. I shall go home."

"Why?"

It was a plain question about a plan, and I had no plain answer to give it.

"Because it is my own," I said. "There is a house with my father's things in it that nobody else will know what to do with, and my work, and my name on a door. Because I was not meant to be here at all."

He studied me, and none of what he thought reached his face.

"Then I'll wait outside it," he said.

My hiccups had gone, somewhere in the course of it, and I had not noticed them go.

* * *

In the worst of the afternoon heat I took the skins up to the seep above the hut, where a thread of water ran from the rock into a basin somebody had cut for it before Mnason was born. I filled them, and then sat on the basin's lip with my skirt pulled up and looked at my legs.

I had not shaved them since my last morning in Philadelphia, with a pink plastic razor out of a bag of ten, and in the nine months since, the hair had come in along my shins dark and soft and longer than they had been since I was twelve. The women of Delphi did something about theirs: Kallia's neighbors singed it off with a lamp flame or tore it out with hot pitch, and compared the two at the spring, and I had listened with my jar and done nothing. I turned my shin to the sun and studied it as I would a bronze left too long in its crate after a dig. Inside that one look I decided it was pitch or nothing, and that I would not stand in a herdsman's hollow tearing out my legs with hot tar for a muleteer, and that was the end of it.

I washed instead, the whole of me, standing in the basin with my chiton spread on the rock, in snowmelt so cold it emptied my chest, and the sun had me dry before I was dressed again.

The string returned up the hollow in the long gold of the afternoon with Felix at the head of it, talking to the black, who walked with her ears laid back so as not to hear him. He had found a stick of juniper on the way and peeled it while he walked, and its white showed from far down the hollow.

Valens met him at the hobbling line, and told him while they took the halters off.

"When the grazing's done the string goes down to Corinth, and we winter there. Come spring, there's a ship. You're with us."

"Ship where? Italy? Is it Italy after all?"

"Italy."

"Capua's in Italy."

"So's a lot," Valens said.

"Corinth, though!" Felix let the black's rope go, and the black wandered away to roll. "The real one? On the neck, with a sea each side? One of the boatmen told me they drag the ships across from one sea to the other on a stone road, whole, on rollers, with the masts still in them. Will we see that? Who'll have the string while we're there? And is there an arena at Corinth, a proper one, in stone? Have you seen the fights in it?"

"Once or twice."

"Once or twice! I'd have seen every one. You'd never take the lot to Corinth. Which would you sell?"

"Some."

"Not the black."

"Nobody'd give me anything for the black."

Felix looked at her, rolling with her four hooves at the sky, the lame one kicking as high as the sound ones. He seemed to find that satisfactory, and swung round on me.

"Have you been? You'd have passed through it, from Syracuse. Everybody from Syracuse comes through Corinth, or round by the cape, and nobody goes by the cape if he can pay for the road. Which way did you come?"

"Another way."

"There isn't another way." He had the juniper stick by its thin end and was swishing the air with it. "When are the games at the Isthmus? This spring coming, or the next? They have a boys' race. Has anybody told you? A whole race of nothing but boys. I've beaten the dun from the troughs to the top three times since the full moon, and she was carrying."

"Was she trying?"

"She was carrying," Felix said, and the dun got no more from him than that.

He made for the junipers along the hollow's upper rim, and soon the sounds that reached us from there were the sounds of a fight. I watched it while I helped Mnason lift his new cheese out of the pot. Felix had Mnason's wicker drainer on his left arm for a shield, and he was fighting the biggest juniper on the slope, which had the reach of him. He kept his feet planted wide, the way the Elateian had stood at the gymnasium, lunging and dancing back out of range and lunging again, and calling out his own name to the crowd each time he struck: "Felix! Felix of Kirrha!" The crowd was ewes.

"When you've beaten that," Mnason shouted up at him, once he saw what had become of his drainer, "you can fight my cheese."

The juniper won. Felix lunged too far on a patch of loose scree, his front foot went, and he sat down hard in a long rattle of stones with the drainer bouncing after him. When he limped down to us both his shins were skinned down the front from the instep up, with grit ground into them, and he had his chin up.

"It slipped," he said.

"The juniper did?"

"The ground." He offered the drainer back to its owner, who took it and looked at its broken rim, then at Felix, and found nothing to say to either. "It's nothing."

I washed his shins at the water skin, with a rag and cold water, and picked the grit out with a thorn, one piece at a time.

"In the boys' race they run up the stadium and down it again," he told me, unasked, staring at the sky. "A man said the Corinthian boy won it last time by a head, and they gave him the pine, and his father cried in front of everybody. I'd not cry. Would you cry, if it was me?"

"I might."

"Only a little," Felix said. "Not in front of everybody."

He did not flinch once where he could see me looking. When he took me to be looking elsewhere, he did, each time. Valens had the black's near fore up across his thigh by then and was paring at it, and he kept on paring.

"Did you see?" Felix called to him. "I had it on its back foot."

"I saw the juniper," Valens said.

At sundown Mnason brought the cheese over to our fire, as he had done every few days since our first week, in its rush basket, with the whey still weeping out of the weave onto his wrist. He set it on the stone by the fire, sat down on our woodstack, which in fairness was his, and cut the cheese into four with the knife he had meant for the fig.

"Tastes of kid," he said. "You can taste it."

"It's good," Valens said, which was the most I had heard him say to Mnason in a week.

It tasted of milk and salt, fresh and squeaking against the teeth. On Ninth Street, in the Italian Market, a man in a paper hat had once passed me ricotta across his counter on the back of a knife, still warm, and it had squeaked the same; the herdsman's was sharper, and had a hair in it. The cheese was good and Mnason said it was not and ate most of it.

"Green fig'll strip your lips," he told Felix, through a mouthful. "My brother ate six once, at the vintage, for a bet."

"Six! Was he sick? Did his lips come off?"

"He won the bet," said Mnason.

"Have you been to Corinth?" Felix said. "Ever? When?"

"Once. It was full of Corinthians."

"And in the winter?" I asked him. "When the snow comes, where do you go?"

"To my son's, at the foot of the gorge, where his wife can count every mouthful into me. Six months of her, and then the snow goes and I come up here to the ewes, who don't count." He licked his thumb. "My son's wife is from Amphissa."

He said it as though that told me the rest, and perhaps it did.

Felix ate standing, with one shin held clear of the fire because it hurt and he would not say so. We had barley bread Valens had baked in the ashes that morning, black on the bottom, and a fistful of the wild greens Mnason picked along the rim and boiled with salt, which were bitter in a way that grew on you, and a little of the wine from the skin, which did not. The sun dropped past the western ridge, the firs went black against it, and the dark rose on the plateau like water in a basin filled from below, until only the summit at our backs still had light on it.

Mnason heaved himself up, put his knife away in his belt, and eyed Valens over the fire, and then me.

"Your man's shaved," he told me.

He was away to his ewes before I had to answer it.

"He's called me that since our first night." Valens had his face to the fire.

Felix lay down where he was, between the fire and the woodstack, with his cloak pulled over him and his skinned shins sticking out of the bottom of it toward the warmth, saying that he was not tired.

"The ships on the rollers," he said. "Do the sailors stay on them, going over the land? Do they row?"

The sailors walked alongside, Valens said. By then Felix was asleep, openmouthed.

The herdsman's dog barked once near the fold and was cursed for it. Valens laid a root of juniper on the embers and watched it catch.

"You do not believe a word of it," I said.

"I believe that cave had a light in it with no flame. I was there to see it."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is yours."

"That is not believing."

"It'll get you on a boat."

"And if you see it open? The door."

"Then I'll believe it," he said, and rose, and went to the string.

* * *

While he walked the line in the dusk, as he did every night, trying the hobbles and standing a while where it ended, his face to the dark of the plateau, which was the way up to us for anybody coming, I took myself into the hut.

It was dry stone shoulder-high, roofed with fir poles under brush and turves, and so low that I could stand upright only under the ridge pole. His rush baskets hung in a row from pegs, and the smell of whey was in the stones. At the back he had made a bed by driving four forked stakes into the earth floor and laying poles across the forks, with cut bracken heaped on the poles and three fleeces on the bracken; all summer it had held me and kept its opinion to itself. Enough light came in at the doorway from the fire to see shapes by, and the shine of things.

When Valens passed the doorway once more I said his name, and he stopped outside it.

"Come in."

"The boy," he said.

"The boy is asleep with his mouth hanging."

He ducked through the doorway with his head down and straightened a little too soon, so that the top of his head met the roof poles and dry needles and earth rained on the pair of us. He told the roof something in Latin, and stood with his head bowed between the poles, picking a needle out of his eye, and I laughed, not much.

"Mind the roof," I said, too late.

"It's lower than me," he said.

I had meant to be graceful about the rest of it, and I got my belt off well enough. Then I took the chiton by its skirt and dragged it upward past my face, and somewhere at the top the cloth caught on itself and trapped my arms inside it. While I fought it my elbow went out sideways in the dark and found something that grunted.

"That was my eye."

I fought free of the chiton with my hair in a tangle, and there, on its strap, against my breastbone, the Pentax hung as it had hung all afternoon.

He looked at it, and then at me.

"You wear it to bed."

"I wear it to everything."

He took the strap two-handed and drew it off me, slowly, keeping it clear of my hair, and hung it on the peg beside the cheese baskets with the lens turned to the wall. With the weight gone, the raw place on my breastbone stung in the cold, and he put his thumb on it. His tunic came off in one pull, as he did whatever was only a matter of doing it. He was very large in that small hut, and pale where the sun had not been at him. I took hold of his belt where he had dropped it and threw it into the corner, so that his hands would find no work in it.

We got onto the bed by stages: I lay down first and drew him by the arm. He followed me down with his elbow hitting the wall, and settled his weight onto the poles a piece at a time; and the fork at the head of the bed, which had borne me since we came up, split along its grain with a dry crack.

The poles at that end rolled out of it, and the head of the bed went down, and my head with it. His palm was under my skull before the floor was, and the floor took the back of his hand instead; the bracken and the fleeces and the two of us went sliding down the slant the poles made and fetched up in a heap by the stones of the wall, with my feet still in the air on the good end and one of the fleeces across my face.

He laughed.

It started somewhere low in him and went on, helpless, and shook the poles under us so that more of the bed gave way, and that set us off harder. I laughed with my face against his ribs and could not stop, nor could he; every time one of us had nearly stopped, the other set off again.

"That's the fork," Valens said, when he could.

"You have had the whole summer to look at that fork."

"I was watching the figs."

A pole at the foot rolled off its fork on its own and hit the floor, and we were gone again. His teeth showed by the doorway's glow, and not one was missing; they were white and square and even, set in his gums like a row of new tiles. I would have bet money he had gone his whole life without coming within a mile of a toothbrush, or of a dentist with his little mirror and his opinions, and I lay in the wreck of the herdsman's bed and resented them.

His hand was still under my head; I drew it out and held it up to the doorway. The skin had gone from two of the knuckles, and there was earth from the floor in the raw places, and a splinter of bracken stalk. I picked the bracken out with my teeth, then the grit, a grain at a time, spitting it aside; I put my mouth to the place, and somewhere in that the laughter left him.

His breathing was loud in the hut, and the fleece had slid off my face. His knuckles stayed at my mouth, and he had gone quite still, the way he went still on the road when he had heard something.

"Here?" he said.

"Here."

For a while his hands were not careful. They came at me hard, on my back and in my hair, and his mouth with them; then they remembered themselves and were careful, which was worse, and better.

He kissed me, and his mouth tasted of the herdsman's wine and the thyme he had chewed at noon. I pulled at him until his weight was on me properly, and what poles were left under the fleece shifted and creaked and took it. The night air at the doorway was cold on my arm, and he was hot from my shoulder to my feet, rough with hair across the chest, and his palm traveled down my side over my ribs and my hip, stopped there, and waited; I put my own hand over it and took it where I wanted it. He learned me after that as a man learns a road he means to take again at night, slowly, all of it, feeling where it led and where it gave, and he hurried no part of it. His mouth went down my throat to my breast, and lingered. His beard was already growing back since noon, and it dragged on my skin.

In that country only luck stood between a woman and a child, and I had not counted a day since October; it went through me, and I did nothing to stop it.

I had him in my hand, hot and heavy, and a sound came from him against my breast, low, that I had no name for. I drew him into me, slowly, all of him, and he held there on his forearms with his mouth by my ear until I moved under him first.

It was slow, and stayed slow, and the pole under my back rocked on its one good fork. Down in the hollow a ewe woke and complained, and her bell went, once, and stopped. The bracken crushed under us and gave up a green, bitter smell, and through the fleece its cut ends pricked my back in a hundred small places that I felt together and each one separately, as I felt the doorway's draft and the heat of him and the grit of the earth floor under the soles of my feet, and the sound of the fire outside, and his breathing going ragged, and my own.

I came with my heel hooked behind his knee and a fistful of the fleece, and he held still for me through it, as he could hold still for anything; then the stillness left him, the whole of it at once, and the care went with it. He drove into me hard, and I wanted it, and pulled him to me, and he made a sound into the fleece, long and broken, and all his weight came down onto me and stayed.

He made no move to see to the bed, which lay as it had fallen, the fork split, the poles off it, half the fleeces on the floor; he lay in it with his arm across me and his face in my hair, and let it.

"Was it the pole?" he said, some while later. "Under your back."

"It was everything under my back. The herdsman will want his bed."

"He can have the end that's standing."

The fire outside had sunk, and his heart was slowing under my palm.

"In your country," he asked, with his mouth in my hair, "do they marry at the gangway?"

"At a table, mostly. In front of a clerk. Somebody writes it down. And here?"

"Here you say it before witnesses, and then everybody eats. Corinth has clerks."

His arm went heavy across me, and stayed heavy.

Some time after that I eased out from beneath it by inches, found my himation by feel among the fleeces, and stepped barefoot onto the wet grass. The stars were down to the ridges all round, more of them than there was dark between, and the summit stood black against them with no light on it anywhere. The string slept on its feet in a row along the hillside, and the fire had gone to embers.

Felix had turned over in his sleep. He lay on his back now, snoring, with his skinned shins to the coals and one arm flung over the grass toward the fig's jar, and his lips had swollen fat and shining in the ember light. In his open hand lay one of Valens's green figs, with one bite taken out of it.
