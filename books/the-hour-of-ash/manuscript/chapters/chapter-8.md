"And once we're past the last house you say nothing about men, not husbands and not sons, whatever they've done to you this winter," Kallia said. "Up there you talk about your feet, or the cold, or you hold your tongue. Wind it tighter than that, or it'll be round your ankle by the second turn of the stair."

She was down on her heels by the hearth with one foot drawn in against her, winding a strip of old blanket over her shoe, under the arch and up round the ankle and back, and her hands did it while her eyes were on the pot. I had strips off the same blanket and my boots under them. The first one I pulled hard enough to satisfy her came apart in my hands.

"Why nothing about men?"

"Because it's the mountain. Tear another; there's more blanket than there is evening."

It was a few days after the turn of the year, by a reckoning I kept to myself and nobody in Delphi would have recognized, and the coldest week since I had come down off Parnassus. The frost had not gone off the court all day. Up on the gallery the Thebans had the shutters closed and were singing behind them, the same song about the girl from Corinth, which they had been improving all winter without making it any shorter. When Kallia stood up they stopped long enough for one of them to call down that he hoped she would bring them back some of the god's wine.

"The pot's on the hearth," she told the shutters. "If the house burns down, you'll pay for the house."

The flashlight stayed where it was, in the bottom of the duffel under the stair. Thirty or forty women with pine torches would know a light that was not fire the moment they saw it, and I had no story for it in Greek or in anything else. I went up the mountain with what the girl had left behind her: the long tunic, the plaited cord, and her brown himation over everything, pinned at my shoulder and drawn up over my head. Kallia had pulled ivy off the terrace wall below the spring that afternoon and twisted it into two rings. She set hers on her kerchief without ceremony. The other lay on the woodstack, and when nobody else claimed it I put it on, and it was wet, and cold water ran down behind my ear.

The lane was full of women. They came out of every gate along it with their torches unlit and their cloaks pulled up, in twos and threes, and the talk ran up and down between the walls as loud as a market. Two gates up from Kallia's, a small quick woman with a great deal of black hair came out and fell in beside us, holding her skirts clear of the frozen slops with both hands.

"Kallia, it's not going to snow, is it? Tell me it's not. I've my husband's sister's sandals on."

"Then you should have borrowed her boots."

"She hasn't got boots. Who has boots?" She looked at mine, which showed for a step under the hem, a bundle of gray rag, and looked away again, satisfied that they were nothing. "She'll have my eyes if they come back spoiled. They're new. She's not worn them to the spring yet."

"Nikaso," Kallia said, "you've a sieve of mine to mend before you borrow anything else in this lane."

So this was the wife of the man with the sieve. I had stood in her lane on my first night with a jar on my hip while Kallia banged on her door, and she had been asleep. She was younger than I had made her in my head, and she had a nose that had been broken once and set a little to the left, so that her face seemed always to be on the point of asking something.

"Is she the Syracusan? Does she talk?"

"When she likes."

"I talk," I said. "Not well."

"Well, nobody up there talks well. You'll be with us, then, for the wine. Kallia carries it and I hold it, and you drink what's left." She hitched her skirts higher. "They squeak, do you hear them? New leather squeaks. If it snows they'll stop squeaking, and then I'm a dead woman."

At the head of the lane we came out into the street that climbed through the top of the town, and the women from the other lanes came into it, and the whole crowd of us went up it together with the noise doubling every time a gate let out more. Men stood in their doorways to watch. Some of them had lit torches to see their wives and daughters off, and held them out over the street so that the light went along the faces under the hoods, one face and then the next, and a boy ran beside us in the gutter shouting his mother's name until she cuffed him home.

At the last gate but one, an old woman sat on a block of stone by her door with a basket of barley in her lap and flung it out over the snow by handfuls. The crows had come down to it out of the dusk, a dozen and more, black on the white, walking about on the frozen crust with their shoulders up.

"Thieves," she was shouting at them. "Black thieves, every one of you. That's not for you, and you know very well whose it is." She threw another handful. "Go on, then. Take it. Choke on it."

She kept her free hand on her knee the whole time, and she did not look at us as we went by. Nor did anyone look at her.

The last house of Delphi was a long low building with a stable at one end, and the street became a path at its corner, and there the men stopped. A man stood at the corner holding a gray mule by her headstall, with her face turned in to the stable wall so that the torches would not set her off. It was the gray, so it was Valens; he had his own back to us as well, like every other man in the street, and I saw only the gray's ears go back and forward at the drums and his hand go up her neck.

The drums had started somewhere at the front, before the corner, where I could not see. There were two of them at first and then four, round frames of hide beaten with the flat of the hand, and under them a pipe that went up and down on two notes and never seemed to stop for breath. Somebody at the front had a fire in a pot. The women went up to it in turn and lit their torches at it and came away, and the light came back along the line toward us, torch from torch, until Kallia held hers out to the woman ahead and came away with it burning, and Nikaso lit hers from Kallia's, and I lit mine from Nikaso's, and the pine caught with a noise like a sheet being shaken out.
