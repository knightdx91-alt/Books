"And once we're above the last house you say nothing about men, not husbands and not sons, whatever they've done to you this winter," Kallia said. "Up there you talk about your feet, or the cold, or you hold your tongue. Wind it harder than that, or it'll be round your ankle before you're halfway up the stair."

She was down on her heels at the hearthstone with one foot drawn in against her, winding a strip of old blanket over her shoe, beneath the instep and up round the ankle and back, and her hands did it while her eyes were on the pot. I had strips off the same blanket and my boots under them. The first one I pulled hard enough to satisfy her came apart in my hands.

"Why nothing about men?"

"Because it's the mountain. Tear another; there's more blanket than there is evening."

"And if they ask me things? Where I was born, and who my people are?"

"They'll ask you everything, every step up and every step down." She jerked the knot at her own ankle to try it. "You tell them what you told the clerk. Then you talk about your feet."

It was a few days past the turn of the year by my private reckoning, and the coldest week since October. The frost had not gone off the court all day. Overhead the Thebans had their shutters closed and were singing behind them, the song about a girl of Corinth that they had been improving all winter without making it any shorter. When Kallia stood up the singing stopped, and a shutter opened.

"Bring us back some of the god's wine, Kallia, and we'll forgive you the lentils."

"There's lentils in the pot," she told the shutters. "If the house burns down, you'll pay for the house."

The flashlight stayed where it was, at the bottom of my bag under the stair. Thirty or forty women with pine torches would know a light that was not fire the moment they saw it, and I had no story for it in Greek or in anything else. I was dressed in the girl's clothes, with her brown himation pinned at my shoulder and drawn up to cover my hair. Kallia had twisted two rings of ivy off the terrace wall below the spring. She set hers on her kerchief without ceremony; the other waited on the woodstack for me, and it was wet when I settled it on my head, so that cold water ran down behind my ear.

"Where on the plain do they dance?" I said.

"Where they danced the last time." She caught the pin at my shoulder, two fingers on the bronze and none on me, and moved it higher. "Hitch that up, or you'll be on your face before the stair."

The lane had filled with women. The Thyiades, the town called them, the god's wild women, though the ones in sight were mostly worried about their hems. They spilled from every gate in twos and threes, torches unlit, cloaks pulled up; the talk went back and forth between the walls as loud as a market. Two gates up from Kallia's, a small quick woman with a mass of black hair came out and fell in beside us, holding her skirts clear of the frozen slops in two fists.

"Kallia, it's not going to snow, is it? Tell me it's not. I've my husband's sister's sandals on."

"Then you should have borrowed her boots."

"She's no boots. Who has boots?" She glanced at mine, which showed for a step under the hem, a bundle of gray rag, and lost interest, satisfied that they were nothing. "She'll have my eyes if they come back spoiled. They're new. She's not worn them to the spring yet."

"Nikaso," Kallia said, "you've a sieve of mine to mend before you borrow anything else in this lane."

This, then, was the wife of the man with the sieve. The night I came, I had stood in her lane with a jar of water dragging at my arm while Kallia banged on her door, and she had been asleep. I had imagined her older, and someone had once broken her nose, which had mended a little to the left, so that her face seemed always to be on the point of asking something.

"Is she the Syracusan? Does she talk?"

"When she likes."

"I talk," I said. "Not well."

"Well, nobody up there talks well. You'll be our third, then, for the wine; it's one skin to three of us. Kallia carries it and I hold it, and you drink what's left." She hitched her skirts higher. "They squeak, do you hear them? New leather squeaks. If it snows they'll stop squeaking, and then I'm a dead woman."

"Do it at the top, where it's flat," Kallia said. "Nobody will carry you down."

At the head of the lane we came out on the street that climbed through the upper town, where the other lanes emptied into it, and the noise doubled at every gate. Men stood in their doorways to watch. Some held torches over the street to see their wives and daughters off, and the light went along the faces under the hoods, each in turn. A boy ran beside us in the gutter shouting his mother's name until she cuffed him home.

At the last gate but one, an old woman sat on a block of stone by her door with a basket of barley in her lap and threw it over the snow a fistful at a time. The crows had dropped to it out of the dusk, a dozen and more, black on the white, walking about on the frozen crust with their shoulders up.

"Thieves," she was shouting at them. "Black thieves, every one of you. That's not for you, and you know very well whose it is." She threw another handful. "Go on, then. Take it. Choke on it."

She kept her free hand on her knee the whole time and gave us not a look as we went by. Nor did any of us spare her one.

The street ended high in the town at a long low building with a stable at one end, the last roof in Delphi, and there the men stopped. A man stood at its corner holding a gray mule by her headstall, with her face turned in to the stable wall so that the torches would not set her off. It was the gray, so it was Valens; he had his own back to us as well, like every other man in the street, and I saw only the mule's ears go back and forward at the drums and his hand go up her neck.

The drums had started at the front, out of my sight. There were two at first, then four: round frames of hide struck with the palm. Under them a pipe climbed and fell on two notes and never seemed to stop for breath. At the front a woman carried fire in a pot. The women approached it by turns, and the light passed from torch to torch toward us. Kallia held hers to the woman ahead and came away with it burning; Nikaso lit hers from Kallia's, I lit mine from Nikaso's, and the pine caught with a noise like a sheet being shaken out.

The path took the cliff in its switchbacks, as it had in November. Then I had been towed up it on a mule's tail; now I had a torch in my fist, and the women ahead of me took the turns at a pace I would have called reckless on a stepladder, singing. The drums came back late off the rock, so that every beat arrived twice. Above me the torches folded back on themselves at each turn. Below me the town shrank to a smudge of smoke with a few sparks in it, and after that I watched the rock.

Ahead of me a tall woman wore a fawnskin knotted over her cloak at one shoulder, dappled side out, and its little black hooves knocked together at her hip as she climbed. Whoever dressed it had left fat on the belly before the tawing, and the hide there had gone stiff as card and was splitting where the ribs had been. Half the women near me carried a stalk of giant fennel instead of a torch, with ivy wound down it and a pine cone jammed on the end, and they leaned on them on the steep parts, which I suspected was not the intention.

Near the front an old woman had a winnowing basket riding on her head, a broad shallow scoop of woven reed with a cloth tied over it, and she carried it up a cliff in the dark without putting a hand to it. An empty jar would not stay on mine the length of Kallia's court. I watched that basket for most of the climb, and it rode the switchbacks as level as a tray in a good restaurant.

"They've stopped," Nikaso said, at the third bend.

"What have?"

"The sandals. They've stopped squeaking." She held up a foot into my torchlight. The snow had begun there, on the cliff path, a hard old crust in the angles of the rock, and her red straps had gone dark with it. "That's the leather drinking. Once it's drunk it never forgets."

"Wrap them," I said. "Wrap them over, as we have."

"Over new sandals? Then nobody sees them."

"Who is there to see them, in the dark?"

"The god," she said, shocked. "And Philista, who leads us up, and she tells everyone at the lions."

Kallia climbed past both of us with the wineskin slung across her back on a cord, and her stride never altered for the ice.

"Is it true that in Syracuse the women go to the baths with the men?" said Nikaso, on the next turn, with no breath to spare for it.

"Not in my family."

"His sister says they do. She says the Romans have spoiled everything over there, and the women walk about the streets with their faces out like men." She considered it for the length of a switchback. "She's never been anywhere. She's never been to Kirrha."

Philista was waiting at the head of the cliff with her drum on her hip: lean, old and upright, her hood thrown back, posted at the top of the path like a teacher at a classroom door after the bell. She inspected each of us as we came onto the edge.

"No Athenians," she told each of us as we passed her, by way of a greeting. "The roads are shut, they say. They say it whenever it suits them. For once he'll be woken properly, and nobody from Athens standing at my elbow to tell me how it's done in Athens."

Behind us the woman who had offered to singe my legs at the bath hauled herself onto the edge on all fours with her torch held clear of the drop in one fist, and stood, and shook out her skirts.

"I've left his mother the dough," she said to anyone who would listen, "and if she lets it rise past the rim, I'll smell it from here. She'll do it to spite me. She'll stand over it and watch it go."

"Sostrata, is it true he comes to the new brides first?" said a girl beside her, very young, with her ivy crown down over one eyebrow. "My husband says he does. He says I'm to tell him everything after."

"Your husband says plenty, Timo, for a man who's been married since the vintage. He comes to whoever's awake. Keep your eyes open and your mouth shut, and tell your husband nothing; that's how mine's lasted twenty years."

The plain lay gray under the stars, with the black line of the firs along its far side. In November the crossing had taken a mule most of a morning. Now the drums set the pace, and the women crossed the snow at something between a march and a run, torches leaning back, breath going up white into the light. Before we arrived I had guessed where we were going. Below the pen at the foot of the firs lay a stretch of level ground, swept bare by the wind and ringed with rocks. Philista went to the largest rock, and the old woman knelt and lodged her basket on top of it.

Everybody who could had carried wood up on her back. I had one of Kallia's bundles, split oak, roped in a sling across my shoulders, and the whole length of the cliff I had wanted to be rid of it. The women piled it in the middle of the ring, with much argument about how it should go. A girl brought the fire up from the pot and blew on it, and the oak caught slowly, and then with a roar.

Then the drums stopped, and the pipe went on alone a while on its two notes, and stopped too.

"What is in the basket?" I said to Nikaso.

"The god." She said it as she might have said water was wet. "His cradle. You'll see."

Its bearer untied the cloth. In the basket there was a round loaf, a wrinkled apple out of a winter store, a twist of raw wool and a sprig of ivy. She lifted it in both hands.

"He's heavy this year," she said.

"He's had a good autumn." Philista bent to the basket. "Wake up, little one. Your brother's gone away for the winter and left you the house. Wake up and have it!"

Then she began a cry, high and long, *euoi*, and every woman at the fire answered it, and up went the basket. It was the toss any Phocian woman gave a basket of threshed barley in a yard in summer, a lift and a jerk to let the wind take the chaff. The loaf jumped and came down again. The apple rolled.

"It is bread," I said.

"It's bread as well," said Nikaso.

Up it went again, and again, with the women crying out at each toss, and the drums came in under the cry. They were waking a god with a farmyard chore, and nobody seemed to find that strange but me.

After that it was dancing.

The drums went faster, and the women went about the fire, each by herself, with her head back and her torch or her fennel stalk held out at the full stretch of her arm, stamping, turning, crying out when Philista cried. "Louder," she was shouting. "He's slept since the vintage. Louder!" Hair came down. Hoods fell back. A woman went by me with her eyes shut, her mouth open and her ivy over one ear. Another went by with both arms over her head and her torch dripping pitch down her wrist. The snow at the ring's edge was kicked into the fire and hissed. The whole business was loud and clumsy, hot on the side toward the fire and freezing on the other, and the smoke kept changing its mind.

Kallia pulled off her kerchief. Her hair came down gray to her shoulder blades; it had been under a kerchief every day since October, and I had begun to think of it as something she did not have. She stepped into the ring with her arms held out stiff from her sides and her chin up, and she danced as she scoured a pot, all of her at once, with no expression on her face.

Nikaso had the skin. She worked the thong loose from its neck, drank from the leg, and passed it across. The wine was thick and black and unmixed. It tasted of the pitch the skin was lined with and, a little, of goat, and it went down into me like a hot stone. It made my eyes water. I passed the skin back; she drank again, and was shoving it at me once more while I was still coughing.

"Kallia's share," I said.

"Kallia's dancing. Look at her. She's the best of all of us at it, and she'll hit you with a pot if you tell her so. Drink."

The second mouthful went down easier than the first. Then, because the drum was in the ground and the ground was in my feet and my feet were the only part of me that had been warm since the stair, I joined the ring.

Every December two brothers from Vermont sold Christmas trees from the corner lot at Twenty-second and Pine and slept in a camper parked beside them, and they burned their offcuts all night in an oil drum with holes punched round the bottom. Coming home from the bus I used to stop beside it for a minute with my hands out to the drum, among strangers, in the smell of burning fir.

"Stamp," Sostrata told me, going by with her skirts in both fists. "Stamp hard. Nothing else warms the feet."

My boots kept time with the drum, and my arms did whatever they liked. The women circling the fire circled me as they circled each other, and none of them looked to see whether I was doing it right, since there was no right; when the cry went up I cried out with the rest, a word I had met in Euripides and never had cause to say out loud, and in my mouth it had the Delphian vowels, not the book's.

When the oak had burned down to a red heap, the women came to it by twos and threes to put new pine to the coals. I lit mine with theirs, took a second pine off the pile as some of the others did, and headed out between the rocks beyond the ring, where women had been slipping off into the night all evening in pairs to squat in the snow and coming back hitching up their skirts. Nobody watched them go, and no one watched me. Kallia was in the middle of the ring with her back to me and her gray hair swinging, and Nikaso had the skin.

Past the pen the firs began, and the drums went behind me into the trees like a party through the wall of the next apartment. Under the firs the snow was deeper and softer, and there was no wind in it at all. My torch lit the trunks nearest me, red on one side; every other trunk was black. The path the mules had found in November lay buried under the snow, and I climbed by the slope of the ground and the dark gap overhead where the trees thinned. The climb went on and on. My breath was loud in my ears and the rags on my boots were heavy with snow, and twice I had to stop and knock it off against a root.

The arch was where it had always been. The drift inside it had built up since November, long and smooth, nearly to the lip. I crossed its crest crawling, with the torch held out to one side, and slid down the inner face into the cave on the seat of my borrowed himation.

Inside, the wind and the snow stopped at the arch, and the drum with them. There was the drip, somewhere far back, dropping into its pool, and the torch.

I crossed into the hall at a walk. The great pale stone in the middle slid past on my right, its shoulder wet and shining. Beyond it the floor rose. I climbed the ridged rock keeping a hand to the wall, which was cold and furred with damp. I had been here twice. I knew the roof would come down on my left before it did. When it came I stooped under it, and there was the slab in front of me, leaning where it had leaned, with the crack running the height of it into blackness.

At the bend, low down, there were pale marks on the rock: short raw scratches through the gray, all slanting one way. They were the only new thing in the cave. They were mine; Kallia's little shovel had made them in November, when I was sure there was earth down there to dig. The rock had been unmarked since it was rock, and in one afternoon I had scored it like the bottom of a saucepan. I laid my thumb in one of the scratches. It was an ordinary feeling, the edge of a scratch in stone, and my thumb knew it from a hundred bad repairs that had reached my bench from other people's hands.

Then I knelt at the crack's widest, at the floor, and eased my hand in flat, palm to the dark, up to the wrist.

The torch burned. Deep in the hall the drip fell, and fell again. The rock chilled my knuckles and the air chilled my palm, and there was no telling the one cold from the other; it was the cold of a mountain in January with snow on its back. My skin felt nothing move. Nothing reached me out of the dark. I waited for it with my cheek on the slab while pitch from the torch crept down my thumb; I left my hand in there until I had been fair to it.

Then I withdrew the hand and tucked it inside the himation, under my arm, against my side, where it ached as it warmed.

I stayed on my knees a while with the hand in my armpit. This time I hit nothing, and there was nobody to laugh. Once the torch began to gutter I lit the second pine off it and crushed the first out on the rock, and in the new light I looked at the crack again: a black gap the height of the slab, a crack like any other. My knees were soaked. Down on the plain Nikaso would be drinking my share.

I retraced the ridged rock, the hall and the slope to the arch, and crawled back over the drift. Outside, the stars had gone. The dark beyond the trees was soft and close. The first flakes drifted down through my torchlight, large and far apart, and the drums had stopped.

Once I was clear of the trees the flakes were close together and the wind had found them. The ring below the pen was full of moving torches. Women were hunting for their hoods and their fennel stalks in the trampled snow, and Philista stood on her rock beating her drum flat and hard, not for dancing, like a cook banging a pot to bring the household in. The fire had sunk and was hissing.

Nikaso found me before Kallia did. She held the skin up to my torch and squeezed it flat between her hands, and the leg gave up nothing.

"I kept it for you as long as I could," she said. "Then I couldn't."

"You drank all of it?"

"A little was left in the bottom, and the skin was getting cold, and it seemed a pity to carry it down."

Kallia came up behind her with her kerchief back on and the gray hair pushed up under it anyhow, and took stock of me. She looked at the knees of the himation, which were black with wet, and at the pitch on my wrist, and said nothing. She held her torch up into the snow coming down past it and studied the flakes, a woman judging how long a job would take. Then she went to help Philista get the women into a line.

"You," Philista said to me as I passed her rock, "with the ivy over your eye. Fix it, or you'll walk off the edge of the world, and I'll have to tell Kallia." Then, to the rest: "Down! Torches lit, all of you, and down, before it shuts the stair. Keep the woman in front where you can see her heel."

We straggled toward the cliff with the wind at our backs. The snow came past us sideways and lit up in each torch as it went by, and between the torches the dark was total, with only the voices in it, the women in front calling out the names of the women behind. Nikaso held on to a fold of my himation. I held on to Kallia's, which she allowed.

"Your knees are wet through," Kallia said.

"I knelt."

"In there." It was not a question. Then, further on, because the wind had dropped for a breath and she could be heard without shouting: "Was it shut?"

She had known, then, where I went. All winter she had asked me about water and wool and pots, and of the mountain she had said only what the town said.

"Yes. It was shut."

For a while she gave her attention to the snow.

"If it's something you want out of that mountain," she said, "there's a god in the town who answers questions. He doesn't answer them in winter. Once the road's clear of snow he sits down in his house and answers whoever's paid."

"Women?"

"Women can't go in to him. A man goes in for you, with your question in his mouth, and brings you out the answer."

"What man would go in for me?"

"Whoever you can get." The wind came back, and she raised her voice over it without seeming to raise anything. "It costs."

"How much?"

"More than you've got. Ask a priest. He'll tell you more than it is."

"Have you asked him anything? The god?"

She gave me no answer. She walked on with her torch held up, and the wind took the flame flat and let it go again, and presently she stopped and turned the torch on the woman behind Nikaso.

"Hold that lower. You're blinding the girl."

At the head of the cliff the line bunched and stopped. Philista had gone to the edge, and two younger women had climbed down with torches, down the first of the switchbacks. We stood in the snow and watched their two lights go down the rock and stop at the turn. One of them came back up alone. She had snow to the thighs and her torch was nearly out.

"The next bend's gone," she said, high and clear, so that it carried down the whole line. "There's a drift across it to the height of my head. I got under the edge of it, and there's ice under that, and when I pushed my stick beyond the ice to feel for the path, there was none."

The other woman came back after her, slowly, on her hands and knees.

For a while everybody talked at once. A woman behind me began to cry. "My two are with his mother. She'll have them in her bed with the dog."

"Then they're warmer than you," said the woman next to her. "They'll be glad of a night off from you."

"Could we go down the long way? By the villages?"

"In this? You'd be under a drift by the second village, and nobody would find you till the shepherds' dogs did, in the spring."

"Give me a light, someone. Mine's gone out."

"Mine's going. Hold it close to mine, then, and don't breathe on it."

"My brother will bring the men up the stair in the morning," another woman said.

"No man comes up here," Philista said. "Not tonight, nor any night. If one comes up in the morning, he can carry my drum down. We stay. Back to the pen. Get under its wall, and bring every stick that will burn."

"And the god in the basket?" I asked Kallia, once the line had started back. "What becomes of him now?"

"He rides down on her head in the morning, and they put him back in his house till the next time." She shifted the empty skin on her shoulder. "Somebody has to carry him, as somebody hauls the water."

We recrossed the plain with the wind in our faces now. I bent my head into it, followed the gray heel of Kallia's rag in front of me, and thought about money. All the money I had in the world was two brass coins from 1998 with a poet on the bigger one; everything else I had was either borrowed or could not be shown. In Philadelphia my pay had landed in a bank at Fifteenth and Market every other Friday without my lifting a finger, and what I was worth in Phocis was anybody's guess.

The pen had walls of stacked stone to the height of my chest and no roof, and a drift in the corner nearest the plain. We kicked the drift out through the gateway and built the fire in the gap where the hurdle had been, so that the wall took the wind and the fire took the gate. There was not much left to burn. The last of the oak went on before anything else. After it the fennel stalks went on singly, ivy and pine cones and all, and the god's wands burned fast and bright with a smell of aniseed, like a licorice store on fire.

Then everybody took off her shoes.

They did it together, sitting on their cloaks, unwinding rags gone stiff with ice and working sodden sandals off their feet, and they set the sandals soles-in round the embers to dry. The ring of them steamed. In the firelight they were one dark leather and one shape, a whole ring of the same sandal, and the women put their bare feet to the fire with their toes spread and groaned at it. For a while, as Kallia had said it would be, the talk was all of feet.

"Look at that. I've three toes gone white."

"Mine are blue. Look, there, the little one's blue."

"Put them on Timo. She's warm; she's still burning from the wedding."

"Take your feet off me," Timo said. "Get them away. They're like fish."

"If I take them off they'll freeze to the ground, and then you can explain it to my husband."

"You said a man," someone objected from the wall. "Up here."

"I said husband. A husband isn't a man."

Nikaso set the red sandals in the place of honor, nearest the coals.

"You'll cook them," Sostrata said, to the ring at large.

"Cooked and dry is better than raw and wet."

"Not so near," I said. "If leather dries too fast it shrinks. It will curl and crack."

"And if I leave it wet it rots on my feet and I lose a toe, and then she has my toes and the sandals both." She pushed them a finger's width nearer, as a concession to me. "Who taught you sandals?"

I kept my boots on. I unwound the outer rags from them, which were ice, and left the inner ones, which were only wet, and I drew my hem across the whole bundle and tucked my feet under me. The others had their own feet to think about. Kallia, beside me, had her shoes off and her rags hung over her two hands toward the fire, turning them like flatbreads on a griddle, and steam came off her knuckles.

Across the fire a big woman sat with her feet out and a black goat-hair cloak round her like a tent, the shepherds' kind, with the snow lying unmelted on its shoulders. I would not have known her face. But she had kept her sandals by her own feet instead of at the fire, and I knew the sandals: the left strap had been mended with a thong of paler leather and knotted twice over the instep, the second knot tighter than the first. I had studied that knot at the spring every morning for weeks, while the woman who wore it stood beside me and talked about the cold, and I had given her nothing but the top of my head until she stopped trying.

She was not looking at me now. She was chewing something she kept in a fold of her cloak, slowly, with her eyes shut.

The trouble began when Timo's skirt caught.

It was only the hem, which she had spread out to dry too near the embers, and it only smoldered. But she shrieked and leapt up.

"I'm burning!"

"You're smoking," Sostrata said. "It's not the same thing."

Everybody near her had leapt up too, and the pen rose out of a ring of sandals and settled again in a different place. When they reached for their sandals there were fifty-nine sandals and a stone.

"Whose is the stone?"

"An Athenian's," said Philista.

"That's mine."

"Yours had a nail in the heel. Feel it. That's no nail."

"Who's got the one with the strap through the toe? I want the one with the strap through the toe."

"You've two right feet on you, girl. Look at them."

Timo examined her feet. She had two sandals on that both bent the same way, and she rolled on the cold stones in them like a sailor. Sostrata reached over, pulled one off her and tried it on herself. Her heel stayed out.

"That's no Delphian sandal. That's off a child."

"It's off my mother," Timo said, "and she'll want it back."

"Then what's on your other foot?"

"No idea. It was by me." She held the foot up to the fire and turned it. "It's got a knot in the strap. Whose has a knot in the strap?"

"Not that one." The big woman spoke without opening her eyes. "Mine are here."

Kallia said nothing through any of it. She sat with her rags in her hands and let it go by her like weather, until a sandal landed in the fire, kicked by a heel in the scramble, and lay on the coals with its strap beginning to fry. Then she reached across and fished it out with a burning fennel stalk, flicked it clear, and fed the stalk to the fire.

"And who's going down that stair barefoot in the morning?" she asked the pen. "Who's to carry her?"

After that the sandal went unclaimed. It lay where it had landed, smoking faintly, while each woman in the pen decided it was some other woman's.

It was Nikaso who wept. She had fished the red pair out of the ring when Timo caught fire, and now she was holding one of them up to the light. It had done what wet leather did by a fire. The sole had curled up at toe and heel, and the red straps had shrunk tight and shiny and turned liver-dark. When she tried it against her foot it sat on her toes like a hat.

"She'll have my eyes," Nikaso said. "Both of them. She'll have them out with a spoon."

"Give it here," I said.

"What for? It's ruined. Look at it."

"The other one is not ruined yet. Give me the other."

Kallia spoke over her rags. "Give it her, and hush. She'll do it if you hush."

Timo had a little flask of hair oil in her bosom, warm from her skin, and she surrendered it silently when I asked for it, which I would not have done in her place. I put a drop of it on the second sandal, where the sole was beginning to lift, and worked it in with my thumbs, slowly, out of the fire's reach, coaxing the leather flat again and holding it there while it warmed in my hands. It smelled of Timo's hair and of hot hide.

"What are you doing to it?" said Nikaso. She was as wary of my hands as a market woman with a pickpocket in view.

"Wet leather keeps whatever shape it dries in," I said. "So you choose the shape for it first, before it chooses one for itself, and then you wait."

"How long?"

"Longer than you will like."

I bent and held, bent and held. I kept the strap wound round two fingers until the sole lay nearly flat on my palm, and then I let her have it.

Nikaso put it on, and it went on. She put her weight on it, with the other foot bare on the cold stones, and studied it.

"Now I'll have one eye," she said.

I leaned back on the wall, and the himation cracked. The drift at the arch had soaked it, and while I worked on the sandal the side turned from the fire had frozen; where it folded under my arm it broke along the fold like a biscuit. It had set hard from the shoulder to the hem. Plutarch had a story of women caught by snow on this mountain, whose clothes froze so hard that they snapped when anybody tried to unfold them; I had taken it for a moralist embroidering, and I sat in the god's pen with a cracked cloak on my knees and owed him an apology.

Sostrata had found the same thing. She got out of her cloak and stood it on its hem in the snow, and it stayed there by itself, the shape of her shoulders, with nothing inside it.

"There," she said. "It can keep the wind off you while I warm up."

"Stand it by me," said Nikaso. "It's warmer company than his sister, and it's got her shoulders."

The last of the fennel was going when Philista stood up in the gateway and struck her drum once, hard, with the butt of her palm.

"He's woken," she said. "He's had his dance and his wine, and he's gone back into his basket to sleep it off. The rest of the night's ours. Say what you like."

"Then I'll say about mine," Timo said, and did, at once, as if it had been waiting in her mouth all night. "He's a potter's son, from the lower town, and we were married at the vintage, and he cried in the doorway when I left with the torches. He wanted to walk me to the plain. I said, you can't, you're a man, and he said he'd walk behind with his eyes shut. He's to wait at the top of the street when it's light, with a hot stone in a cloth for my feet. He promised."

"Mine would bring a cold one," said Nikaso, "and ask me where his supper was."

"Mine will be asleep on the flour sacks," Sostrata put in, "with his mother's dough climbing out of its pan beside him, and the two of them snoring at each other." She had her feet in Timo's lap, and Timo had let them stay there. "Whose man will climb that stair with a stone for your feet? None of them will. Not if the god himself sent for them."

"Mine will." Timo found me over the fire. "And yours, Maira? Did he mind you coming?"

"There is no he."

It went quiet in the pen. Timo's mouth stayed a little open. A woman behind Nikaso, a stranger to me, stopped wringing out her rag to look.

"No husband?" Timo stared. "At your age?"

"Not even a dead one?" asked Sostrata.

"Not even that."

"Then who sends you up mountains?" Nikaso said.

Kallia was turning her rags to the fire. She had supplied dead people and a boat that got in ahead of the winter; she had supplied no husband, and she did not supply one now. The rags steamed in her hands.

They were all looking at me. I had been looked at by the women of Delphi at the bath and in the salt row, and I had felt it go one way before; in the light of the last fennel I could not tell which way this was going to go.

"Well," Sostrata said, "then she'll have to make do with ours." And she began to sing.

It was a baker's song, with a tune that lifted at the end like a question, and all of them knew it but me. "My man gets up in the dark for his oven," she sang, "and he's home in the dark when he's done, and his mother's in the bed between us, keeping it warm."

"Knead it, knead it," the pen came in, slapping their knees on the beat, "the oven's gone cold!"

"Nikaso's man sleeps in his sandals," she sang, "and his feet are warm, and the rest of him's not."

The refrain came round again, louder.

"That's a lie," said Nikaso. "He takes them off for the bed."

"Just the one," said Sostrata, and went on. "The bride's man went to bed at the vintage, and he's feeling about for the bride there still."

Timo screamed and threw a sandal at her, and it was somebody else's.

"Philista's man went off to the Athenians—"

"He did," Philista said, with deep satisfaction. "They can keep him."

"Knead it, knead it, the oven's gone cold!"

Sostrata worked her way round the pen like that, with a verse for every woman there who had a man, and when her eye came to Kallia it went past her without a verse to the woman beyond, and Kallia went on with her rags; that was as near as anyone in the pen came that night to keeping Kallia's rule. Then Sostrata turned to me, held up her hand for quiet, and got it.

"The Syracusan's man stayed home in Syracuse," she sang, slowly, "and he—"

She stopped, with her hand still up, and waited.

They all waited. Even the big woman had opened her eyes. Behind me the snow whispered down on the wall, and the fennel ticked in the fire.

"And he was so small," I said, into the gap where the tune should have gone, since I could not sing to save my life, "that I lost him on the boat."

The pen went to pieces. Timo went over sideways into Nikaso, and Nikaso's good sandal went out into the snow. Sostrata beat her own knees with both fists and had no breath left to sing the next line, so Philista sang it for her and beat her drum with it, and the women roared the refrain at the sky, louder than they had roared anything for the god. Kallia bent her face over her rags and did not raise it. I was laughing with them. The laugh started down under the wine, and there was no stopping it, and I did not try, and when it was done my face ached in the cold.

"Was he?" Timo said, when she could. "Truly? That small?"

"Smaller," I said. "I have made him bigger for the song."

"Again," said Timo, with her face wet. "From the oven. Give her a verse of her own."

They sang it twice more, and by the last of them I had the refrain.

Afterward the fire was a red line in the gateway with nothing left to give it, and the night came in over the wall and settled into the pen like water over a sill. Philista went round on her knees and pushed us together.

"Close," she said. "All of you, with your backs to the wall, and your cloaks over the lot. Any woman who sleeps by herself tonight wakes up dead, and I'll not carry her."

They lay down where they were in one long heap under the wall, legs over legs and backs to bellies, with their cloaks thrown over everything and the stiff ones laid on as lids. Kallia lay down at the outer edge, on her side, with the wind at her back and her dried rags folded under her cheek.

"Wake me when it's light," she said, to the heap in general. "Those Thebans will have the pot scraped dry by now, and burnt the scrapings."

I sat at the end of the wall in the cracked himation with my knees drawn up and waited, as I had waited at the spring and in the council hall, to be told where I went.

The stranger lifted her head out of the heap.

"You," she said. "You at the lions."

"Yes."

"Each morning I tell you about the weather, and each morning you look at my feet." It did not seem to have troubled her much. "You're letting the cold in at this end." She took my wrist, not hard, and pulled, and raised the edge of her cloak with her other arm. "Come here, and bring your feet."

Her cloak came over me and shut out the snow. Beneath it was her heat, a great store of it, and the smell of goat hair and woodsmoke and another woman's sleep, with the coarse black hair of the cloak against my face. Somebody's shoulder settled against mine. Somebody's hand came across in the dark and drew the cracked edge of my himation down over my boots and withdrew, and I could not tell whose it was.

"It was worse the winter the spring froze," the stranger said, into my hair. "The lions had ice in their mouths a hand long. We broke it with a hammer to get at the water."

"It is very cold," I said.

That was the whole of what I had said to her in a month of mornings.

"This? This is nothing. You should have been up here the time before last. We had a woman lose the end of her nose." She worked her knees in behind mine. "Not that she ever used it much."

She seemed to think that was enough talk. Her breathing went long and slow against my neck, and soon I could feel she was asleep. Under the cloak it was as warm as a kitchen with the oven going.

"Maira." It was Nikaso, on my other side, very low. "Where did you go, before? When we were dancing."

"To the cave."

"On your own? At night?" She thought about that. "What for?"

"To see if it was open."

"It's a cave," said Nikaso, and yawned against my shoulder. "It's always open."

I lay awake long after the others. The snow hissed on the cloak over us like sand thrown at a window. Further along the heap Timo was whispering to Sostrata, and Sostrata was whispering back.

"And if his mother's in the house when I get back?"

"Then you give her the stone. Give it her before he can, and say it was your idea. A man's mother forgives you anything for something warm, the first winter."

"And the second?"

"Go to sleep."

Later Sostrata talked in her sleep about the dough, and Nikaso's bare foot found my calf and kept to it for the warmth, and from down the wall, now and then, Timo said her husband's name under her breath, until Philista lifted her head out of the heap.

"He can't hear you from Delphi, girl. We can."

I listened to all of it, with my wrist still warm where the stranger had taken hold of it.

When I woke the snow had stopped. I raised the edge of the stranger's cloak an inch and saw the pen in the first gray light: the fire a black ring in the gateway, and Nikaso's one good sandal standing up in the snow outside it where it had landed. Everything else was white and still. All down the length of the wall the women lay heaped together asleep, and the steam rose off them into the cold.
