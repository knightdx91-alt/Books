"And once we're above the last house you say nothing about men, not husbands and not sons, whatever they've done to you this winter," Kallia said. "Up there you talk about your feet, or the cold, or you hold your tongue. Wind it harder than that, or it'll be round your ankle before you're halfway up the stair."

She was down on her heels at the hearthstone with one foot drawn in against her, winding a strip of old blanket over her shoe, beneath the instep and up round the ankle and back, and her hands did it while her eyes were on the pot. I had strips off the same blanket and my boots under them. The first one I pulled hard enough to satisfy her came apart in my hands.

"Why nothing about men?"

"Because it's the mountain. Tear another; there's more blanket than there is evening."

"And if they ask me things? Where I was born, and who my people are?"

"They'll ask you everything, every step up and every step down." She jerked the knot at her own ankle to try it. "You tell them what I told the clerk, and not a word over."

It was a few days past the turn of the year by my private reckoning, and the coldest week since October. The frost had not gone off the court all day. Overhead the Thebans had their shutters closed and were singing behind them, the song about a girl of Corinth that they had been improving all winter without making it any shorter. The singing stopped, and a shutter opened.

"Bring us back some of the god's wine, Kallia, and we'll pay you the month early."

"The month's due on the day, wine or none," Kallia said. "If the house burns down, you'll pay for the house."

I left the flashlight at the bottom of my bag under the stair. I was dressed in the girl's clothes, with her brown himation pinned at my shoulder and drawn up to cover my hair. Kallia had twisted two rings of ivy off the terrace wall below the spring. She set hers on her kerchief without ceremony; the other waited on the woodstack for me, and it was wet, so that when it went on my head cold water ran down behind my ear.

"Where on the plain do they dance?" I said.

"Where they danced the last time." She caught the pin, two fingers on the bronze and none on me, and moved it higher.

The lane had filled with women. The Thyiades, the town called them, the god's wild women, though the ones in sight were mostly worried about their hems. They spilled from every gate in twos and threes, torches unlit, cloaks pulled up; the talk went back and forth between the walls as loud as a market. Midway along the lane a small quick woman with a mass of black hair came out and fell in beside us, holding her skirts clear of the frozen slops in two fists.

"Kallia, it's not going to snow, is it? Tell me it's not. I've my husband's sister's sandals on."

"Then you should have borrowed her boots."

"She's no boots. Who has boots?" She glanced at mine, which showed for a step under the hem, a bundle of gray rag, and lost interest, satisfied that they were nothing. "She'll have my eyes if they come back spoiled. They're new. She's not worn them to the spring yet."

"Nikaso," Kallia said, "you've a sieve of mine to mend before you borrow anything else in this lane."

This, then, was the wife of the man with the sieve. The night I came, I had stood in her lane with a jar of water dragging at my arm while Kallia banged on her door, and she had been asleep. I had imagined her older, and someone had once broken her nose, which had mended a little to the left, so that her face seemed always to be on the point of asking something.

"Is she the Syracusan? Does she talk?"

"When she likes."

"I talk," I said. "Not well."

"Who talks well, up there? You'll be our third, then, for the wine; it's one skin to three of us. Kallia carries it and I hold it, and you drink what's left. And these squeak, do you hear them? New leather squeaks. If it snows they'll stop squeaking, and then I'm a dead woman."

"Do it at the top, where it's flat," Kallia said. "I'll not lay you out on a stair."

At the head of the lane we came out on the street that climbed through the upper town, where the other lanes emptied into it, and the noise doubled at every gate. Men stood in their doorways to watch. Some held torches over the street to see their wives and daughters off, and the light went along the faces under the hoods, each in turn. A boy ran beside us in the gutter shouting his mother's name until she cuffed him home.

At the last gate but one, an old woman sat on a block of stone by her door with a basket of barley in her lap and threw it over the frozen ruts a fistful at a time. The crows had dropped to it out of the dusk, a dozen and more, black on the white rime, walking about with their shoulders up.

"Black thieves," she was shouting at them, "every one of you. That's not for you." She threw another handful. "Go on, then. Take it. Choke on it."

She kept her free hand on her knee the whole time, and her eyes on the crows.

The street ended high in the town at a long low building with a stable at one end, the last roof in Delphi, and there the men stopped. A man stood at its corner holding a gray mule by her headstall, with her face turned in to the stable wall so that the torches would not set her off. It was the gray, so it was Valens; he had his own back to us as well, like every man who had come that far, and I saw only the mule's ears go back and forward at the drums and his hand go up her neck.

The drums had started at the front, out of my sight. There were two at first, then four: round frames of hide struck with the palm. Under them a pipe climbed and fell on two notes and never seemed to stop for breath. At the front a woman carried fire in a pot. The women approached it by turns, and the light passed from torch to torch toward us. Kallia held hers to the woman ahead and came away with it burning; Nikaso lit hers from Kallia's, I lit mine from Nikaso's, and the pine caught.

The path took the cliff in its switchbacks, as it had in November. That time I had climbed it in the dark behind the gray; now I had a torch in my fist, and the women ahead of me took the turns at a pace I would have called reckless on a stepladder, singing. Every drumbeat arrived twice, once from the drum and once off the rock. Above me the torches folded back on themselves at each turn. Below me the town shrank to a smudge of smoke with a few sparks in it, and after that I watched the rock.

Ahead of me a tall woman wore a fawnskin knotted over her cloak at one shoulder, dappled side out, and its little black hooves knocked together at her hip as she climbed. It had been tawed with the fat still on the belly, and the hide there had gone stiff as card and was splitting where the ribs had been. Half the women near me carried a stalk of giant fennel instead of a torch, with ivy wound down it and a pine cone jammed on the end, and they leaned on them on the steep parts, which I suspected was not the intention.

Near the front an old woman had a winnowing basket riding on her head, a broad shallow scoop of woven reed with a cloth tied over it, and she carried it up a cliff without putting a hand to it. An empty jar would not stay on mine the length of Kallia's court. I watched that basket for most of the climb, and not one turn unsettled it.

"They've stopped," Nikaso said, at the third bend.

"What has stopped?"

"The sandals. They've stopped squeaking." She held up a foot into my torchlight. The snow had begun there, on the cliff path, a hard old crust in the angles of the rock, and her red straps had gone dark with it. "That's the wet getting in, and she'll know; she knows if I've so much as breathed on her loom."

"Why not wrap them over, as we have?"

"Over new sandals? What's the good of them, wrapped?"

"Who is there to see them, in the dark?"

"The god," she said. "And Philista, who leads us up, and she tells everyone at the lions."

Kallia climbed past both of us with the wineskin slung across her back on a cord, and her stride never altered for the ice.

"Snow caught the women up here once, when my mother was a girl," Nikaso said behind her. "They sent men up in the morning to fetch them, and the men's cloaks froze so hard on the climb that they broke in two when anybody tried to unfold them."

"Is it true that in Syracuse the women go to the baths with the men?" said Nikaso, on the next turn, with no breath to spare for it.

"Not in my family."

"His sister says they do. She says the Romans have spoiled everything over there, and the women walk about the streets with their faces out like men." She considered it for the length of a switchback. "She's never been anywhere. She's never been to Kirrha."

Philista was waiting at the head of the cliff with her drum on her hip: lean, old and upright, her hood thrown back, posted at the top of the path like a teacher at a classroom door after the bell. She inspected each of us as we came onto the edge.

"No Athenians," she said, by way of a greeting. "The roads are shut, they say. They say it whenever it suits them. For once he'll be woken properly, and nobody from Athens standing at my elbow to tell me how it's done in Athens."

Behind us the woman who had offered to singe my legs at the bath hauled herself onto the edge on all fours with her torch kept clear of the drop, and stood, and shook out her skirts.

"I've left his mother the dough," she said to anyone who would listen, "and if she lets it rise past the rim, I'll smell it from here. She'll do it to spite me. She'll stand over it and watch it go."

"Sostrata, is it true he comes to the new brides first?" said a girl beside her, very young, with her ivy crown down over one eyebrow. "My husband says he does. He says I'm to tell him everything after."

"Your husband says plenty, Timo, for a man who's been married since the vintage. Tell him nothing; that's how mine's lasted twenty years."

"Husbands," said Philista. "On my cliff, and the god not woken."

"He comes to whoever's awake," Sostrata told Timo, a good deal lower.

The plain lay gray under the stars, with the black line of the firs along its far side. The drums set the pace, and the women crossed the snow at something between a march and a run, torches leaning back, breath going up white into the light. Before we arrived I had guessed where we were going. Below the pen at the foot of the firs lay a stretch of level ground, swept bare by the wind and ringed with rocks. Philista went to the largest rock, and the old woman knelt and lodged her basket on top of it.

Everybody who could had carried wood up on her back. I had one of Kallia's bundles, split oak, roped in a sling across my shoulders, and the whole length of the cliff I had wanted to be rid of it. The women piled it in the middle of the ring, with much argument about how it should go. A girl brought the fire up from the pot and blew on it, and the oak caught slowly, and then with a roar.

The drums stopped, and the pipe went on alone a while on its two notes, and stopped too.

"What is in the basket?" I said to Nikaso.

"Him." She said it as she might have said water was wet. "That's his cradle. You'll see."

Its bearer untied the cloth. In the basket there was a round loaf, a wrinkled apple out of a winter store, a twist of raw wool and a sprig of ivy. She lifted it in both hands.

"He's heavy this year," she said.

"He's had a good autumn." Philista bent to the basket. "Wake up, little one. Your brother's gone away for the winter and left you the house. Wake up and have it!"

She began a cry, high and long, *euoi*, and every woman at the fire answered it, and up went the basket. It was the winnowing toss, a lift and a jerk to let the wind take the chaff. The loaf jumped and came down again. The apple rolled.

"It is bread," I said.

"It's bread as well," said Nikaso.

Up it went again, and again, with the women crying out at each toss, and the drums came in under the cry.

After that it was dancing.

The drums went faster, and the women went about the fire, each by herself, with her head back and her torch or her fennel stalk held out at the full stretch of her arm, stamping, turning, crying out when Philista cried. "He's been asleep three months," she was shouting. "Louder!" Hair came down and hoods fell back. A woman went by me with her mouth open and her ivy over one ear, and another came past with both arms over her head and her torch held so far back that the woman behind her had to duck. The snow at the ring's edge was kicked into the fire and hissed. The whole business was loud and clumsy, hot on the side toward the fire and freezing on the other, and the smoke kept changing its mind.

Kallia pulled off her kerchief. Her hair came down gray to her shoulder blades. I had seen it only once before, plastered flat at the bath, and I had begun to think of it as something she did not have. She stepped into the ring stiff-elbowed, with her chin up, and she danced as she scoured a pot, all of her at once, with no expression on her face.

Nikaso worked the thong loose from the neck of the skin, drank from the leg, and passed it across. The wine was thick and black and unmixed. It tasted of the pitch the skin was lined with and, a little, of goat, and it landed in my stomach like a coal. I passed the skin back; she drank again, and shoved it at me once more.

"Kallia's share," I said.

"Kallia's dancing. Look at her. If it sours in the skin while she's at it, who gets the blame? Me. Drink."

The second mouthful went down easier than the first, and after it I joined the ring, mostly for the sake of my feet.

Every December two brothers from Vermont sold Christmas trees from the corner lot at Twenty-second and Pine and slept in a camper parked beside them, and they burned their offcuts all night in an oil drum with holes punched round the bottom. Coming home from the bus I used to stop beside it for a minute with my hands out to the drum, among strangers, in the smell of burning fir.

"Stamp," Sostrata told me, going by at a heavy trot. "Harder. I've his mother under each foot, and look how warm I am."

My feet went with the drum as well as they could, and my arms did whatever they liked. The women circling the fire circled me as they circled each other, and none of them looked to see whether I was doing it right; when the cry went up I cried out with the rest, a word I had met in Euripides and never had cause to say out loud, and my mouth made it Delphian.

When the oak had gone to coals, the women came in little knots to put new pine to them. I lit mine with theirs, took a second off the pile as some of the others did, and headed out between the rocks beyond the ring, where women had been slipping off into the night all evening in pairs to squat in the snow and coming back hitching up their skirts. Nobody watched them go, and no one watched me. Kallia was in the middle of the ring with her back to me and her gray hair swinging, and Nikaso had the skin.

Beyond the pen the firs began, and the drums went behind me into the trees like a party through the wall of the next apartment. Under the firs the snow was deeper and softer, and there was no wind in it at all. My torch lit the trunks nearest me, red on one side; every other trunk was black. The path the mules had taken lay buried under the snow, and I climbed by the slope of the ground and the dark gap overhead where the trees thinned. The climb went on and on. My breath was loud in my ears and the rags on my boots were heavy with snow, and twice I had to stop and knock it off against a root.

The drift inside the arch had grown, long and smooth, nearly to the lip. I crossed its crest crawling, with the torch held out to one side, and slid down the inner face into the cave on the seat of my borrowed himation.

Inside, the wind and the snow stopped at the arch, and the drum with them.

I crossed into the hall at a walk, and beyond the middle of it the floor rose. I climbed the ridged rock keeping a hand to the wall, which was cold and furred with damp. Twice before I had been this far, and I knew the roof would come down on my left before it did. When it came I stooped under it, and there was the slab in front of me, propped against the wall as before, with the crack running the height of it into blackness.

At the bend, low down, there were pale marks on the rock: short raw scratches through the gray, all slanting one way. They were the only new thing in the cave. They were mine; Kallia's little shovel had made them in November, after the last of the earth was out. The rock had been unmarked since it was rock, and in one afternoon I had scored it like the bottom of a saucepan. I laid my thumb in one of the scratches. It was an ordinary feeling, the edge of a scratch in stone.

I knelt at the crack's widest, at the floor, and sent my hand in to the wrist.

The torch burned. Deep in the hall the drip fell, and fell again. My knuckles were on stone and my palm was in the dark, and the two of them were as cold as each other, which was January cold and no more. I kept my cheek on the slab while pitch from the torch crept down my thumb; I left my hand in there until I had been fair to it.

I withdrew the hand and tucked it inside the himation, under my arm, where it ached as it warmed.

I stayed on my knees. There was nobody to laugh. When the torch began to gutter I changed it for my last pine, and in the new light I looked at the crack again: a black gap the height of the slab. My knees were soaked. Down on the plain Nikaso would be drinking my share.

I retraced the ridged rock, the hall and the slope to the arch, and crawled back over the drift. Outside, the stars had gone. The dark beyond the trees was soft and close. The first flakes drifted down through my torchlight, large and far apart, and the drums had stopped.

Once I was clear of the trees the flakes were close together and the wind had got in among them. The ring below the pen was full of moving torches. Women were hunting for their hoods and their fennel stalks in the trampled snow, and Philista stood on her rock beating her drum flat and hard like a cook banging a pot to bring the household in. The fire had sunk and was hissing.

Nikaso found me before Kallia did. She squeezed the skin flat between her hands, and the leg gave up nothing.

"I kept it for you as long as I could," she said. "Then I couldn't."

"You drank all of it?"

"A little was left in the bottom, and the skin was getting cold, and either way it was my fault."

Kallia came up behind her with her kerchief back on and the gray hair pushed up under it anyhow. She looked at the knees of the himation, which were black with wet, and went to help Philista get the women into a line.

"Your ivy's slipped," Philista said to me as I passed her rock. "Put it straight. I bring my women down crowned." Then, to the rest: "Torches lit, all of you, and down, before it shuts the stair."

We straggled toward the cliff with the wind at our backs. The snow came past us sideways and lit up in each torch as it went by, and between the torches the dark was total, with only the voices in it, the women in front calling out the names of the women behind. Nikaso held on to a fold of my himation. I held on to Kallia's.

"Your knees are wet through," Kallia said.

"I knelt."

"In there." Then, further on, because the wind had dropped for a breath and she could be heard without shouting: "Did you find what you went for?"

"No. It was shut."

She let a dozen paces go by.

"If it's something you want out of that mountain," she said, "there's a god in the town who answers questions. He doesn't answer them in winter. Once the road's clear of snow he sits down in his house and answers whoever's paid."

"Women?"

"Women can't go in to him. A man goes in for you, with your question in his mouth, and brings you out the answer."

"What man would go in for me?"

"Whoever you can get." The wind came back, and she raised her voice over it without seeming to raise anything. "It costs."

"How much?"

"More than you've got. Ask a priest. He'll tell you more than it is."

"Have you asked him anything? The god?"

"Who would I send in?"

She walked on, and the wind took her flame flat and let it go again.

At the head of the cliff the line bunched and stopped. Philista had gone to the edge, and two younger women had climbed down with torches, down the first of the switchbacks. We stood in the snow and watched their two lights go down the rock and stop at the turn. One of them came back up alone. She had snow to the thighs and her torch was nearly out.

"The next bend's gone," she said, high and clear, so that it carried down the whole line. "There's a drift across it to the height of my head. I got under the edge of it, and there's ice under that, and when I pushed my stick beyond the ice to feel for the path, there was none."

The other woman came back after her, slowly, on her hands and knees.

Everybody talked at once, and for a while I could not sort one voice from the next, until single ones came clear of it. A woman behind me began to cry. "My two are with his mother. She'll have them in her bed with the dog."

"They'll be warmer than you," said the woman next to her, "and glad of a night without your feet in their backs."

"Could we go down the long way? By the villages?"

"In this? You'd be under a drift by the second village, and the shepherds' dogs would be the ones to find you."

"Give me a light, someone. Mine's gone out."

"Mine's going. Hold it close to mine, then, and don't breathe on it."

"My brother will bring the men up the stair in the morning," another woman said.

"No man comes up here," Philista said. "Not tonight, nor any night. If one comes up in the morning, he can carry my drum down. We stay. Back to the pen. Get under its wall, and bring every stick that will burn."

"And the god in the basket?" I asked Kallia, once the line had started back. "What becomes of him now?"

"He rides down on her head in the morning, and they put him back in his house till the next time." She shifted the empty skin on her shoulder. "Somebody has to carry him, as somebody hauls the water."

We recrossed the plain with the wind in our faces now. I bent my head into it, followed the gray heel of Kallia's rag in front of me, and thought about money. All the money I had in the world was two brass coins from 1998 with a poet on the bigger one; everything else I had was either borrowed or could not be shown. In Philadelphia my pay had turned up in a bank downtown every other Friday without my lifting a finger, and what I was worth in Phocis was anybody's guess.

The shepherds' huts were the width of the plain away, somewhere in the dark. The pen had walls of edge-set stone to the height of my chest and no roof, and a drift in the corner nearest the plain. We kicked the drift out through the gateway, dragged the hurdle aside, and built the fire in the gap, so that the wall took the wind and the fire took the gate. There was not much left to burn. The last of the oak went on before anything else. After it the fennel stalks went on singly, ivy and pine cones and all, and the god's wands gave us mostly smoke. The pith in them took the fire and nursed it a long while before any flame showed. Prometheus had stolen fire in one of these; I had always pictured it blazing in his fist, and it must have smoked under his cloak.

Then everybody took off her shoes.

They did it together, sitting on their cloaks, unwinding rags gone stiff with ice and working sodden sandals off their feet, and they set the sandals soles-in round the embers to dry. The ring of them steamed. In the firelight they were one dark leather and one shape, a whole ring of the same sandal, and the women put their bare feet to the fire with their toes spread and groaned at it. For a while the talk was all of feet.

"Look at that. I've three toes gone white."

"Mine are blue. Look, there, the little one's blue."

"Put them on Timo. She's warm; she's still burning from the wedding."

"Take your feet off me," Timo said. "Get them away. They're like fish."

"If I take them off they'll freeze to the ground, and then you can explain it to my husband."

"You said a man," someone objected from the wall. "Up here."

"I said husband. A husband isn't a man."

Nikaso set the red sandals in the place of honor, nearest the coals.

"You'll cook them," Sostrata said.

"Cooked, she can't prove anything. Wet, she'll smell them from the lane."

"Not so near," I said. "If leather dries too fast it shrinks. It will curl and crack."

"And if I leave it wet it rots on my feet and I lose a toe, and then she has my toes and the sandals both." She pushed them a finger's width nearer, as a concession to me. "Who taught you sandals?"

I kept my boots on. I unwound the outer rags from them, which were ice, and left the inner ones, which were only wet, and I drew my hem across the whole bundle and tucked my feet under me. The others had their own feet to think about. Kallia crouched beside me with her shoes off and her rags hung over her two hands toward the fire, turning them like flatbreads on a griddle.

Across the fire a big woman sat with her feet out and a black goat-hair cloak round her like a tent, the shepherds' kind, with the snow lying unmelted on its shoulders. I would not have known her face. But she had kept her sandals by her own feet instead of at the fire, and I knew the sandals: the left strap had been patched over the instep with a square of paler leather, sewn on with a thong and set on askew. I had studied that patch at the spring every morning for weeks, while the woman who wore it stood beside me and talked about the cold, and I had given her nothing but the top of my head.

She was chewing something she kept in a fold of her cloak, slowly, with her eyes shut.

The trouble began when Timo's skirt caught.

It was only the hem, which she had spread out to dry too near the embers, and it only smoldered. But she shrieked and leapt up.

"I'm burning!"

"You're smoking," Sostrata said. "It's not the same thing."

The women near her had leapt up too, and the pen rose out of a ring of sandals and settled again in a different place. When they reached for their sandals there were fifty-nine sandals and a stone.

"Who put a stone in the ring?"

"An Athenian," said Philista.

"That's mine."

"Yours had a nail in the heel. Feel it. That's no nail."

"Who's got the one with the strap through the toe? I want the one with the strap through the toe."

"You've two right feet on you, girl. Look at them."

Timo examined her feet. She had two sandals on that both bent the same way, and she rolled on the cold stones in them like a sailor. Sostrata reached over, pulled one off her and tried it on herself. Her heel stayed out.

"Some child's hopping home barefoot for want of this."

"It's off my mother," Timo said, "and she'll want it back."

"And on the left?"

"How should I know? It was next to me." She stuck the foot out at the fire. "It's got a patch on the strap. Who has a patch on her strap?"

"Not that one." The big woman spoke without opening her eyes. "Mine are here."

Kallia said nothing through any of it. She stayed crouched and let it go by her like weather, until a sandal landed in the fire, kicked by a heel in the scramble, and lay on the coals with its strap beginning to fry. Then she reached across and fished it out with a burning fennel stalk, flicked it clear, and fed the stalk to the fire.

"And who's going down that stair barefoot in the morning? Who's to carry her?"

The sandal went unclaimed after that. It lay smoking faintly on the stones while each woman in the pen decided it was some other woman's.

It was Nikaso who wept. She had snatched the red pair out of the ring when Timo caught fire, and now she had one of them in her lap. It had done what wet leather did by a fire. The sole had curled up at toe and heel, and the red straps had shrunk tight and shiny and turned liver-dark. When she tried it against her foot it sat on her toes like a hat.

"She'll have my eyes," Nikaso said. "Both of them. She'll have them out with a spoon."

"Let me have it," I said.

"What for? It's ruined. Look at it."

"The other one is not ruined yet. Give me the other."

"Give it her, and hush," Kallia said. "She'll do it if you hush."

Timo had a little flask of hair oil in her bosom, warm from her skin, and she surrendered it silently when I asked for it, which I would not have done in her place. I put a drop of it on the second sandal, where the sole was beginning to lift, and worked it in with my thumbs, slowly, out of the fire's reach, coaxing the leather flat again and holding it there while it warmed in my hands. It smelled of Timo's hair and of hot hide.

"What are you doing to it?" said Nikaso. She was as wary of my hands as a market woman with a pickpocket in view.

"Wet leather keeps whatever shape it dries in," I said. "So you choose the shape while it is still soft, and then you wait."

"How long?"

"Longer than you will like."

I bent and held, bent and held. I kept the strap wound round two fingers until the sole lay nearly flat on my palm, and then I let her have it.

Nikaso put it on, and it went on. She put her weight on it, with the other foot bare on the cold stones, and waited for it to fail.

"Now I'll have one eye," she said.

I leaned back on the wall, and the himation cracked. The drift at the arch had soaked it, and while I worked on the sandal the side turned from the fire had frozen; where it folded under my arm it broke along the fold like a biscuit. It had set hard from the shoulder to the hem. I had taken Nikaso's story on the stair for nerves, and I sat in the god's pen with a cracked cloak on my knees and owed her an apology.

Sostrata's cloak had done the same. She got out of her cloak and stood it on its hem in the snow, and it stayed there by itself, the shape of her shoulders, with nothing inside it.

"There," she said. "It can keep the wind off you while I thaw."

"Stand it by me," said Nikaso. "It's warmer company than his sister, and it's got her shoulders."

The last of the fennel was going when Philista stood up in the gateway and struck her drum once, hard, with the butt of her palm.

"He's woken," she said. "He's had his dance and his wine, and he's gone back into his basket to sleep it off. The rest of the night's ours. Say what you like."

"I'll go first," Timo said, and did, at once, as if it had been waiting in her mouth all night. "He's a potter's son, from the lower town, and we were married at the vintage, and he cried in the doorway when I left with the torches. He wanted to walk me to the plain. I said, you can't, you're a man, and he said he'd walk behind with his eyes shut. He's to wait at the top of the street when it's light, with a hot stone in a cloth for my feet. He promised."

"Mine would bring a cold one," said Nikaso, "and ask me where his supper was."

"The baker will be asleep on the flour sacks," Sostrata put in, "with his mother's dough climbing out of its pan beside him, and the two of them snoring at each other." She had her feet in Timo's lap, and Timo had let them stay there. "Not a man in this town will climb that stair with a stone for your feet, Timo, not if the god himself sent for him."

"Mine will." Timo leaned to me across the fire. "And yours, Maira? Did he mind you coming?"

"There is no he."

It went quiet in the pen. Timo's mouth stayed a little open. A woman behind Nikaso, nobody I knew, stopped wringing out her rag to look.

"No husband?" Timo stared. "At your age?"

"Not even a dead one?" asked Sostrata.

"Not even that."

"Who sends you up mountains, then?" Nikaso said.

Kallia was no help. She had supplied dead people and a boat that got in ahead of the winter, but no husband, and she did not add one now.

They were all looking at me, and in the light of the last fennel there was no telling which way it was going to go.

"Well," Sostrata said, "she can have the loan of ours." And she began to sing.

It was a baker's song, with a tune that lifted at the end like a question, and all of them knew it but me. "My man gets up in the dark for his oven," she sang, "and he's home in the dark when he's done, and his mother's in the bed between us, keeping it warm."

"Knead it, knead it," the pen came in, slapping their knees on the beat, "the oven's gone cold!"

"Nikaso's man sleeps in his sandals," she sang, "and his feet are warm, and the rest of him's not."

The refrain came round again, louder.

"That's a lie," said Nikaso. "He takes them off for the bed."

"Just the one," said Sostrata, and went on. "The bride's man went to bed at the vintage, and he's feeling about for the bride there still."

Timo screamed and threw a sandal at her, and it was somebody else's.

"Philista's man went off to the Athenians—"

"He did," Philista said. "And they can keep him."

"Knead it, knead it, the oven's gone cold!"

Sostrata worked her way round the pen like that, with a verse for every woman there who had a man, and when her eye came to Kallia it went past her without a verse to the woman beyond, which suited Kallia; that was as near as anyone in the pen came that night to keeping Kallia's rule. Then Sostrata turned to me, held up her hand for quiet, and got it.

"The Syracusan's man stayed home in Syracuse," she sang, "and he—"

She stopped, with her hand still up, and waited.

They all waited. Even the big woman had opened her eyes. Behind me the snow whispered down on the wall, and the fennel ticked in the fire.

"And he was so small," I said, into the gap where the tune should have gone, since I could not sing to save my life, "that I lost him on the boat."

The pen went to pieces. Timo went over sideways into Nikaso, and Nikaso's good sandal went out into the snow. Sostrata beat her own knees with both fists and had no breath left to sing the next line, so Philista sang it for her and beat her drum with it, and the women roared the refrain at the sky, louder than they had roared anything for the god. Kallia bent her face over her rags and did not raise it. I was laughing too. The laugh started down under the wine, and when it was done my face ached in the cold.

"Was he?" Timo said, when she could. "Truly? That small?"

"Smaller," I said. "I have made him bigger for the song."

"Again," said Timo, with her face wet. "From the oven. Give her a verse of her own."

They sang it twice more, and by the last of them I had the refrain.

The fire was down to embers in the gateway with nothing left to give it, and the night came in over the wall like water over a sill. Philista went round on her knees and pushed us together.

"Close," she said. "All of you, with your backs to the wall, and your cloaks over the lot. Any woman who sleeps by herself tonight wakes up dead, and I've never lost one yet."

They lay down where they were in one long heap under the wall, legs over legs and backs to bellies, with their cloaks thrown over everything and the stiff ones laid on as lids. Kallia lay down at the outer edge, on her side, with the wind at her back and her dried rags folded under her cheek.

"Wake me when it's light," she said. "Those Thebans will have the pot scraped dry by now, and burnt the scrapings."

I sat at the end of the wall in the cracked himation with my knees drawn up and waited to be told where I went.

The stranger lifted her head out of the heap.

"You," she said. "You at the lions."

"Yes."

"Each morning I tell you about the weather, and each morning you look at my feet." It did not seem to have troubled her much. "You're letting the cold in at this end." She took my wrist and pulled, and raised the edge of her cloak with her other arm. "Come here, and bring your feet."

Her cloak came over me and shut out the snow. Beneath it was her heat, a great store of it, and the coarse black hair of the cloak against my face. Somebody's shoulder settled against mine. Somebody's hand came across in the dark and drew the cracked edge of my himation down over my boots and withdrew, and I could not tell whose it was.

"It was worse the winter the spring froze," the stranger said, into my hair. "The lions had ice in their mouths a hand long. We broke it with a hammer to get at the water."

"It is very cold," I said.

That was the whole of what I had said to her in a month of mornings.

"This? This is nothing. You should have been up here the time before last. We had a woman lose the end of her nose." She worked her knees in behind mine. "Not that she ever used it much."

Soon she went heavy against my back, and she was asleep. Under the cloak it was as warm as a laundromat in February.

"Maira." It was Nikaso, on my other side, very low. "Where did you go, before? When we were dancing."

"To the cave."

"On your own? At night?" She thought about that. "What for?"

"To see if it was open."

"It's a cave," said Nikaso, and yawned against my shoulder. "It's always open."

The snow hissed on the cloak over us like sand thrown at a window. Further along the heap Timo was whispering to Sostrata, and Sostrata was whispering back, near enough that I got about half of it.

"And if his mother's in the house when I get back?"

"Give her the stone, before he can, and say it was your idea. The baker's mother let me off a whole burnt batch for one, the first winter."

"And the second?"

"Go to sleep."

Sostrata talked in her sleep, a word of it about the dough, and Nikaso's bare foot found my calf and kept to it for the warmth, and from down the wall, now and then, Timo said her husband's name under her breath, until Philista lost patience.

"He can't hear you from Delphi. We can."

When I woke the snow had stopped. I pushed the stranger's cloak up an inch and saw the pen in the first gray light: the fire a black ring in the gateway, and Nikaso's one good sandal standing up in the snow outside it where it had landed. Everything else was white and still. All down the length of the wall the women lay heaped together asleep, and the steam rose off them into the cold.
