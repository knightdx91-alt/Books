Of everything the Romans ever thought of, the roads and the arches and the concrete included, Saturnalia was the best.

Evidence for it came to me all festival long, our fifth in that street, and I gathered it the way I gathered other people's broken pots, without anyone asking me to.

The first piece came with our bread, at daybreak on the opening morning. The bakery facing us sent the loaves across in a flat basket each day. Their slave who kept the oven all night carried it: heavy and slow-moving, gray at the temples, the insides of both forearms glazed pink and shiny from years of reaching into heat. In all our time on that street he had brought the basket to my plank bareheaded, taken his coppers and gone. That morning a cap of brown felt sat on his head, round and soft and brimless. It was the cap stamped on the reverse of Brutus's coin, where it stood between two daggers for liberty. Behind him in the bakery doorway his master wore its twin, and nothing in that early light, at the width of a street, said which of the two men owned the other.

"Cerdo, look at you." Grata was at her marble, coaxing the fire under her pots, and the words were for him, for me and for the street. "The very spit of your master, and him dearer than you by half, that's the festival for you, and the aediles can't tell a thief from a decurion, which in this town is no great change, so mind it doesn't go to your head, Cerdo, you'll want that head for the oven."

"It was his idea," said Cerdo, "and he's in his."

"He's in his all right, he had it on before a loaf was baked, and he'll not take it off for the Emperor."

I gave him his coppers, and then, since I had been turning the idea over for a month and the cap decided me, I asked him to our table. "The night after tomorrow, for the festival, if you will come to us." He stood studying the coppers in his palm.

"Ask him," he said, with a jerk of his chin at his master.

I crossed to the baker. He was a narrow man with flour in the hair of his arms and a cold he had nursed since the olive harvest, and he heard me out with his cap pulled down to his eyebrows.

"Have him," he said, "and send him home for the second firing, because Saturn can go without bread for the festival but the street can't, and nobody else of mine knows that oven."

"I will send him back."

"Feed him, he eats like a mill," he added, and blew his nose on his fingers and went in.

Before midday the cap was on every head over the age of ten in our street: Grata's on top of her hairpins, the fuller's over his bald patch, his lads' at the vats, freckled gray with what they trod. At the spout I stood in mine behind a girl balancing a jar, and she turned round and showed me, with her chin, a woman three places back in the same brown felt.

"That's mine," she said, "my mistress, and she's stood behind me since daybreak and can't say a thing about it." She hitched the jar higher. "Tomorrow I'll give her my place, and she'll owe me for it."

"I heard every word," came from three places back.

"You were meant to, mistress."

"And when it's over?"

"I carry yours," the girl answered, and faced the spout again.

For the length of it nobody in the town was supposed to know, looking along a line from behind, which of the heads in it could be sold. The togas were folded away in chests, the courts and the schools were shut, and a slave might say what he liked to his master and be answered as a man in the same hat.

My customers paid me in wax, and before the festival was two days old the old cobbler's shelf held more light than our shop had burned in all our years there. A widow from the alley behind the Stabian baths had brought me her lamp to mend three times that year. She came to my bench in her cap with a bundle of tapers in a cloth and laid it down as tenderly as if it held her grandchildren.

"Four, and good wax, the honey's still in it, smell it, and everybody that owes pays in wax at the festival, so that's for the lamp."

"You paid for the lamp in the summer."

"The first time I did. This is for the third mending, and the second's on account." She pushed the bundle nearer my hand. "And don't you go giving them back, that's bad luck at the festival, it goes into the wax and comes out in the year."

The tapers were tallow-yellow and slightly bent, rolled by hand round a twist of rush. I stood them up with the others, and the shelf smelled of a hive.

Next morning I took Lucius to the forum. The eastern colonnade had been let out to sellers of small figures in clay: gods and dogs and actors' masks, fat old men, babies in baskets, and mules, which was where Lucius stopped and would go no farther. I bought him one with panniers and a dun coat painted over the white slip, and while he lifted it to the light I tipped it to see its seam. It had been pressed in a two-part mold, the halves joined wet and trimmed by somebody with too many to finish, and the blade had taken the tail with it. For Aelia I chose a doll whose arms and legs hung on wooden pegs and swung, though her right leg was fitted in her left socket.

"Your knife has had the tail off this one."

"It was a mule," said the stallholder, who had one blind eye and sharp opinions in the other, "and they lose them, ask any carter."

"And this one's legs are on backward."

"So she walks crooked, and so does my wife, and I've had my wife thirty years and nobody's offered for her yet." He asked two coppers for the pair. "And the boy gets a walnut for his trouble."

At the fountain four men sat dicing on the basin's step in broad day, a heap of coppers between their feet, and among them sat an aedile's attendant whose work in any other month was to break up precisely that.

"Not a word, mender," he called, rattling the dice, "the aedile's dicing too, over at the basilica, and losing." He threw, and swore by four gods, and laughed.

Behind them a ring of small boys was bowling walnuts at a pyramid of walnuts.

"He can have these." The boy with the biggest heap pushed three into Lucius's hand. "He's too little to win any." Holding the mule and the walnuts, Lucius had no hand to spare for me, and kept close at my side the whole road back, joined to my skirt by no more than habit.

Grata had put a cap on Valens too, and he wore it as he wore everything, without remarking on it. There was bark on his shoulders from cutting her firewood, since no one carted that week.

"Did they keep the festival at the school in Corinth?" I asked.

"The lanista served our supper. One night. We had his couch and he had our bench." He tipped the skin on its peg to his mouth. "The food was better."

"Only the food?"

He considered. "He gave us a pig."

"For all of you?"

"For the troupe. It was a small pig."

"I have asked the baker's man, Cerdo, to our supper tomorrow."

He hung the skin back on its peg. "Give him the seat that looks at the door."

"Why?"

"He'll want to."

Felix came home that evening while the lamps were being lit, with Albucius's leave on a tablet in his belt and a sack slung on his back. He had started a conversation at the fullery, and I heard him carry it on at Grata's, at a pitch that reached the baths, with a street that had not yet answered.

"Ten days! He's given the troupe ten, and last night he served us himself, in a cap, carrying the pork round on a board like a girl at a wedding, and he dropped half of it, and the bear got it." The sack hit our step, and walnuts poured from its neck and went rolling off under the shelving. "Those are for Lucius, where is he, is that him, look how long he's got!"

Lucius came pelting past my plank, the clay mule in his fist, and was snatched aloft by the ribs and lifted until his hair brushed the beam. He shrieked, so Felix brought him down and sent him up again.

Valens came with the lamp from the mules. "Where will you sleep?"

"In the straw, where else, if the dun's kept my place." Felix settled Lucius on his hip. "He feeds us like a duumvir, meat every third day, and has the black bitten anybody lately?"

"The fuller's boy."

"Good."

Grata kept a cup with four knucklebones in it on her counter for the festival, and Felix had it in his fist before Grata had poured for him.

"Venus or nothing," Grata warned him, "every face different and you're king of this street till the last day, and it's not been thrown this winter, so don't you go thinking it, a boy with walnut shells in his hair, but throw, then, if you're throwing."

He threw standing, elbow high, which was how he threw anything.

Grata held a lamp over the bones where they lay on her marble.

"Venus," she said, "gods help us all, a gladiator for king and him with less sense than a goose, so somebody find him a crown before he finds one himself."

The fuller's lad fetched a wreath of bay off the tree by the vats. Grata jammed it on over Felix's cap and down round both ears, and the street cheered him for nothing better than that he was standing in it. He took the cheer as owed, then swung about to face me where I stood at our door, the wreath down over one eye.

"Here's my first law: tomorrow night in your house not a soul lifts a finger who's ever been bought, and everybody who's never been bought waits on them, and that's you, and that's the Chaldean when he comes."

"Then you will be among the waited-on," I said.

"So I will." That had not occurred to him, and it pleased him enormously. "That's the best law anybody ever made."

"And nobody waits on me, as usual," said Grata to the street.

* * *

The next proof was comedy, and the best of that was on the stage at the crossing.

Fenn arrived from Puteoli at breakfast, a day behind Felix, perched on the jars of the fish-sauce carter's cart in his traveling tunic, nursing a jar of his own. The carter handed him down at Grata's. "Mind his jar for him, he's nursed it since Puteoli and wouldn't part with it to piss."

"The Chaldean," Grata announced. "Hold on to your purses, he'll have your futures off you." Fenn stood in the roadway and turned slowly round on his heel, taking in the caps and the dice and the boughs nailed over the fullery door, until Felix bore down on him in his wreath.

"It's the Mummers," Fenn said, dropping into English, "it's New Year's Day on Broad Street, and all it wants is a banjo and a string band in feathers."

"You're on the wrong coast for a banjo."

"I'm on the wrong everything." He hefted his jar. "Falernian, and it cost me an arm off a man on the quay who swore on his mother, and if it's vinegar we drink it regardless, because I don't pour good money in the gutter."

Felix reached him and delivered the law in person.

"You're the Chaldean, and you've never been bought, and who'd pay for that face, so you wait on me tonight, and him, and the oven man opposite, the mender's asked him, and you'll pour, shaking hand or not."

Fenn heard it out, head tilted, then straightened the wreath on Felix's head with great gravity.

"Majesty," he said in his Greek, which came out stately even in our street. "I shall wait on your table with my own hands, and if they shake, the wine shall reach the cups by a longer road."

The noise had brought Valens out of the stable. Fenn bowed to him from the waist, the bow he kept for Valens.

"Sir, I am to be your servant tonight, I hear."

"I heard. The wood I'll cut anyway," said Valens.

Walking down to the crossing that afternoon, Fenn kept at his elbow.

"How have the roads been since the rains?"

"Bad past the salt pans. The sea's taken a bite out of the road there, and the carts go single. The inn at Herculaneum waters its wine. It waters its hay as well."

"How does a man water hay?"

"He leaves it out." Valens stepped round a dog. "And sells it by weight."

"And what is hay fetching at Herculaneum?" asked Fenn, and got an answer to the copper.

"You came on the fish cart," said Valens.

"From Puteoli, sitting on a jar of sauce."

"Sit at the back of it going home. The load shifts on the hill above Herculaneum."

The stage was a platform of boards on two carts where our street crossed the long street running south. A painted cloth hung behind it on poles. By the ninth hour the crossing was packed wall to wall and up onto the fountain's rim. Fenn came back from a bakery sill with a bagful of hot rolls and passed them round to whoever stood near, strangers included. Lucius ate half of his and held the rest up in his fist above his father's head, out of Valens's sight and well within the birds'.

He rode high on his father, heels tucked in at the armpits. I had Aelia in the sling, wide awake, gnawing the doll's crooked leg. Felix had climbed onto the fountain, wreath and all, to have the best view in the street and be seen having it, and Fenn stood at my elbow in his hat.

"What's on the bill?" he asked me in English, tipping his hat back to see over the heads.

"A baker, which is all anybody would tell me."

"A baker, in this town," he said, and his eyes travelled the packed crossing to the two bakeries selling rolls off their sills, "there'll be a riot."

The cloth was painted with an oven and a shelf of loaves. A flute player sat on the tail of a cart, and the players were three, without masks. The baker was short, a cushion strapped round his middle, flour on his face and arms, and a peel on his shoulder. His wife was a big woman whose laugh carried to every wall of the crossing without her seeming to try. The third was a youth with long legs and a hopeful face. The crowd hissed him the instant he showed round the cloth, because they knew what he had come for.

"Wife!" cried the baker. "I go to deliver the loaves. Guard the house."

"With my life, husband."

"Guard the dough."

"With my life."

He kissed her at length, on both cheeks and then on the hand, and set off with the loaves by marching on the spot, while the crowd groaned at him. He had not marched three steps before the youth had slipped round the cloth's edge with his arms wide.

"Behind you!" a woman bawled from the crowd, and half the crossing joined in. "Behind you, baker!"

"Under the lid!" cried a boy. The baker turned, but the youth was gone; nothing remained of him but a long leg vanishing into the kneading trough at the front of the stage, and the wife seated on its lid, fanning herself.

"Abbott and Costello," Fenn said in my ear, in English, "Costello in a laundry hamper at the Earle when I was eleven, the same bit exactly, and I swear to God they stole it."

"My peel," said the baker, "I forgot my peel, and since I'm home I'll start tonight's dough."

"The dough's resting."

"It's rested long enough. Off." He shooed her from the lid, threw it open, and plunged both arms into the trough to the elbow.

The trough squealed.

"What's that noise?"

"The leaven, husband, the leaven's very lively this year, it's the festival, it gets in everywhere."

He kneaded harder, and a man at my back shouted, "Knead him, baker, lean on him!" The trough groaned, and under his fists the youth sneezed; a cloud of flour went up into the sun and hung there over the front row, and the front row lost its breath.

Beside me Valens laughed out loud, in a crowd of strangers.

The baker drew from the trough, by its ankle, a long white leg. He examined it, and then he examined his wife.

"Wife, what is this?"

"By Juno," she said, "by Ceres, by the Lares of this house, I never saw that leg in my life."

"She's lying!" yelled a man on the fountain's rim. "Look at his feet, baker!"

The rest of the youth followed his leg, white from his hair to his toes, and stood blinking at the baker with flour falling off him in small drifts. The baker stared at him, and the crossing held still and waited.

Then the baker dropped to his knees and threw his arms round his wife's legs.

"Forgive me, I doubted you, you, the best wife in Pompeii! And look what the gods have done for the festival, the dough has risen into a man, and I nearly took my peel to it." He turned on his knees to the white youth. "Forgive me too, and here, have a loaf."

The crowd came apart, men hanging on each other's necks; Fenn had his hat off and was beating it on his leg, and up on the fountain Felix howled and nearly went in.

Valens was weeping. I had him in profile: his face streaming under the eyes into his beard, his mouth wide, his shoulders going. Both his hands were up on Lucius's shins to keep the boy steady, and he had none to spare for anything else. The sound he made had been a laugh at the start, and then it was nothing I knew. He stood with his son on his shoulders in the middle of all that laughter, and he could do nothing about any of it.

With the baby in the sling I had nothing to give him but my place at his side, and I held it.

Lucius felt it first and saw it after. He had been intent on the stage. Now he peered down, then hung forward above his father's face, holding on by a fistful of hair, until his nose touched his father's from above. For some breaths he hung so. Then he brought round the fist with the half roll in it and pushed the bread at his father's mouth.

It struck Valens on the lip, and Lucius pushed again, harder, the way he pushed bread at Aelia when she cried. Valens's mouth opened, and he took it.

He chewed. Lucius hung over him, watching him chew, and waited; when his father had got it down he showed him the empty hand, open, so his father would know it was all gone.

On the stage the baker gave the white youth his loaf, the wife gave the baker a kiss, and the flute player blew something shrill, and all three bowed to the crossing and began throwing nuts. Valens loosed one shin, drew a hand once down his face, and set it back.

"He all right?" Fenn asked me in English, in the noise, having only then seen it.

"He's fine."

Fenn turned his eyes up to Valens then, the hat crushed in his hand, and for once he let a thing be.

Felix rejoined us near the baths, wet to the knee.

"Were you watching, I was in, nearly, I had a foot in and a man had me by the belt, and the flour, the flour going up, I'll do that to Albucius, I'll get him in a trough!" He ran on ahead to tell the street.

We came home past the theaters. Lucius sat aloft, fingers wound in his father's hair. "The baker has bad knees," said Valens, at the theater steps, his first words since the crossing. "He won't drop on them like that for many more winters." The baker stayed behind us on his stage.

* * *

The table was the last piece of evidence, and I had made it myself.

It was my plank, dragged to the center of the floor, with Grata's spare bench along its near edge and our stools along the far. I lit every taper on the shelf. There were nineteen by then, of every length and color wax came in, and I stuck them upright in their own drippings along the plank's edges and on the sill; the shop smelled of honey, of lentils and pork from Grata's pot, and of Fenn's Falernian, which was not vinegar.

"It's a temple." Felix paused on the threshold with his wreath pushed back. "Are we eating or praying?"

"You've burned a month of mending there," was Grata's verdict, "and you'll be sat in the dark at the Kalends with your needle, and serve you right."

Cerdo came at lamp-lighting. He had washed; his hair was wet at the temples under the cap. He had a round loaf under one arm, scored into eight wedges and stamped at the center with the bakery's mark, and handed it in to me from the step without stepping over.

"Off the best of the batch, and he'll never miss it."

"Come in, your place is the top of the plank."

He took in the lit room behind me, the people in it, and the end of the plank, where our only stool with a back stood waiting.

"Sit," said Felix from the bench, "that's the king's law, you sit and he sits and I sit and they fetch, and who's minding your oven?"

"The boy," said Cerdo. "He'll let it die, and I'll be on my knees at the mouth of it till daylight, bringing it back."

Cerdo sat, perched on the stool's front edge, his burned forearms on his thighs.

I carried the first bowl to him myself, with the best of the pork in it. Fenn followed with the jar, poured for him, and made him a deep bow, Puteoli-fashion. Cerdo looked at the bow. Then he studied the bowl.

"I have had no guest to pour for in years, sir," said Fenn. "Forgive me if I spill."

"It's in the cup."

Cerdo ate the first mouthful slowly, with an eye on the doorway, and the second faster, and after that he ate the way his master had promised.

"You're the carter," Cerdo told Valens between mouthfuls. "The four mules."

"Four."

"The dun's the best of them."

"She is."

Valens lasted till the loaf. When it wanted cutting he was halfway up from his seat with his knife out, and Felix grabbed a fistful of his tunic.

"Sit. You've been bought."

"Once."

"Once is plenty." Felix relieved him of the knife and passed it over to me. "She cuts."

Valens sat back down with his hands spread on the plank and nothing for them to do, and ate the piece I gave him.

Fenn's gift for Lucius came out when the bowls were empty. It was a ball the size of a large apple, made by a saddler on the Puteoli waterfront from a drawing in wax. Two long pieces of white hide, each pinched at the waist, were curled together and joined with red thread in a single seam, the stitches neat and pulled too tight. At the very middle, under my thumb, something yielded a little and then refused.

"What's in the heart of it?" I asked him in English.

"Cork," said Fenn, "off a jar of Chian, you need cork in the middle or it's no ball at all, and it wants yarn wound round it before the hide goes on, only try telling a saddler about yarn, so you've got wool."

Lucius took possession of it at once. "Ball," he told his father. "Tata. Ball." Valens studied it from his side.

"What's the use of it?"

A dish of olive pits sat by Fenn's elbow. He pushed the bowls back, swept a space clear with his forearm, and began laying them out.

"Permit me, sir." His Greek was at its most courtly. "It is a game, and I shall need a little room."

Grata came across for her pot and stopped at my shoulder, eyeing the stool with the back to it.

"The bakery's man in the good seat," she said in my ear, which for Grata was a whisper the fountain could have heard. "You've a soft head under that cap, and he'll go back to his mat by the oven tonight and lie there thinking about your stool, and be no use to the baker till the Ides, and I had a girl once, before my Secundus, and a cap and a cup at the door every year was all she got, and she slept the sounder for it." She hoisted the pot onto her hip. "Send him home with the sausage end, that's the sweetest."

Fenn set a crust of the loaf at the corner closest to him. He laid four pits in a square tilted so that one corner sat on the crust, and made a dab of lentil in the middle.

"You," he told Lucius, and gave him a fifth pit, "are the man on the hill."

Lucius set the pit on the lentil, exactly in its middle, pressed it home with a fingertip and then took a step away to show it was done. More pits went out beyond them until the field ran off the plank onto Grata's bench and Felix had to move his cup.

"Here, at this crust, a man stands with a club, and here, on the little hill, stands one who throws it at him."

"At him?"

"Past him, as close to him as he dares and as fast as his arm will send it, and if the man with the club strikes it, he runs." Fenn walked two fingers along to the first pit. "Here, and these nine of the opposing side try to catch the ball and get it to this corner before he does."

"What does it weigh?"

"Five unciae and a half, near enough."

Valens's hand came out, open. In time Lucius gave up the ball into it, though not his claim to it. Valens weighed it.

"This is lighter."

"The fellow in Puteoli had never held a real one. He did his best by it." Fenn shifted the pit on the hill a fraction.

"What's underfoot?"

"Grass, and bare earth where they run, raked smooth."

"Who rakes it?"

"A man with a rake," said Fenn, enchanted. "Every day, between games."

"If the thrower hits him," Valens asked next, "this one with the club, what protects him?"

"A cap." Fenn pointed through the candles at the felt on Valens's head. "Wool, with a peak at the front for the sun."

Valens touched his own cap, and let the hand drop.

"Has it killed one?"

Fenn's fingers stopped on the plank. "Once, before my day, a player for Cleveland, which is a city, took it on the side of the head and died in the morning."

"And the thrower."

"Pitched nine more years."

Valens let that go by. A big finger went among the pits and pushed the one on the little hill back to the spot Fenn had moved it from.

"Go on."

Fenn kept on, through the three strikes and the four wide ones, in a Greek made for Plato and now explaining a foul ball, with English leaking out of it wherever the Greek gave way. "What's the Greek for an umpire?" he asked me, and I gave him *brabeus*, the judge at the games. Then the plank turned into Brooklyn, on the first of October, nineteen fifty.

"I was sixteen," he went on, switching to English for me and moving pits at speed, "and they wouldn't let you stand more than a hundred deep in front of the appliance store on Frankford Avenue, so I was the hundred and first and got it over a policeman's shoulder. Bottom of the ninth, tied, Dodgers on first and second, Snider singles up the middle, and here comes Abrams round third to win it, here." A pit swung round the square's corner under his finger. "And out here's Ashburn, Richie Ashburn, who can't throw, ask anybody, he throws like my sister, and he's running in before the ball's off the bat." He returned to Greek for Valens. "The runner was here, sir, and the ball came from here, and he was out at the crust by the length of this table."

"Why did he run, with it coming?"

"The coach at the corner waved him round."

"And whoever waved him."

"They blamed him for it all winter."

"They would."

"Then the tenth, Sisler, home run, three in, the pennant, and I walked home to my mother's and I don't believe my feet touched the sidewalk." He sat back, short of breath. "We lost the Series four straight, but never mind that."

Cerdo had followed none of the English and most of the Greek, and he had stopped eating to watch Fenn's hands.

Felix was out of his seat and standing over the pits.

"Do they pay them, how much, is it better than a lanista pays?"

"Some of them far better," Fenn answered, "and their faces are printed on little cards, which boys trade in the street."

"Their faces? Could you get my face on one?"

"I could have it stamped on a coin, if you would only be patient."

"Can a man change sides," Valens asked, "if he wants another?"

"Not unless his side sells him," said Fenn. "The club owns him from the day he signs until the day it lets him go."

"And he plays."

"For that money he would play on his knees."

"They play it at night as well," Fenn told Valens, "under lights on poles taller than this house, a hundred lamps to a pole, as bright as noon."

"Somebody's up those poles all night, then," said Cerdo, "and sleeps when the rest of you are up."

Valens considered the field of pits.

"How much oil does that take?"

Fenn put his forehead down on his arms among the pits. "Oil," he told me, coming up crimson, "he wants the oil reckoned, that's the first sensible question anybody's asked me about baseball in thirty-five years."

"How many come to watch?" Valens asked.

"Thirty thousand on a Sunday, all of them yelling at the *brabeus*."

"What do they yell for?"

"His blood, mostly," said Fenn. "They never get it."

Valens had not finished with the pits.

"Show me how the stealing goes."

"That is for another evening, sir, and another jar."

"In the spring I'm hauling to Puteoli, and I'll come up your stair."

Fenn turned a pit over on the plank with one fingertip. "I shall get olives in."

The king had held his peace as long as he could bear. He mounted Grata's bench with his wreath slipping.

"Now a story. Valens tells how he won his freedom."

"When it finished, I hadn't fallen down," said Valens.

"Tell it properly."

"That was all of it."

"Shortest tale ever told at a festival table, and the best," Grata told the plank, "and you could learn from it, Felix."

"The Chaldean tells the king's stars next, and will I be famous?"

Fenn rose and inspected him across the candles. "Majesty," he began, "you have Mars well placed, and Venus in a dice cup. You will be famous in every town where you are not known already."

"What does that mean?"

"It means do not marry before thirty."

For a breath Felix was away at the fullery's shut door. Then he made his next law. "Everybody sings, and the guest first."

Cerdo sang sitting, eyes shut, a song from the hill villages behind Nuceria about a goat that ate the priest's wreath off the altar and was made priest itself. His voice had gone to smoke at the oven years before and scraped in his throat, but he knew every verse. Before the end Grata was beating time on the plank and Felix was singing the goat. Aelia, in my lap, stared along the tapers on the plank's edge and crowed at each flame in turn.

"The mender," said Felix, when the goat had its priesthood.

"I do not sing."

"The king says."

"Then let the king choose another thing; I will mend his sandal, or read him every wall as far as the forum."

"Sing."

"Felix, I cannot carry a tune as far as the door."

"Be a sport," Fenn put in, in English. "It's a party."

"I sang, lady," said Cerdo. "You heard what it was."

I gave Fenn the jug.

I sang them the one song I had every word of, the one the organ played in the middle of the seventh inning at the Vet. My father had taken me once, on a school night in May. He rose for it, a frank in his fist, and sang louder than anybody in our section, and we were gone in the eighth to beat the traffic on the expressway. It started too high, and a line later it had sunk too low and wandered about down there; I heard it wander and could not call it back. Felix's mouth fell open. "That'll be a hymn to one of their gods," said Grata, "and he's welcome to it," and she covered the ear of Aelia's that was nearer me. Lucius hugged his ball and did not take his eyes off me.

Fenn came in on the peanuts, loud, in a saloon baritone with no shame anywhere in it, every word in its place, and drowned me out, and I was grateful and kept on underneath. On the line after the peanuts he flung both arms wide over the candles and sang the beams the one about never getting back, as loud as his lungs would let him.

My voice stopped there. Nothing I did would start it again.

Fenn sailed on without me, rooted for the home team alone, struck the side out with three blows of his fist that set the pits jumping, and held the last note of the old ball game till his breath gave out. The table had not understood one word, but it beat the plank and cheered him anyway, and Cerdo beat it hardest of anyone.

"Worst song I ever heard," said Felix, delighted, "so do it again."

"I haven't sung that out loud since Eisenhower," said Fenn, mopping his forehead with his sleeve.

I sat and drank my wine.

* * *

When the tapers on the sill had burned to stubs, Fenn's jar was empty, every drop as promised, and Grata had sent over one of hers, and the talk had broken into pieces. Fenn had Grata's palm open in the lamplight and was reading her fortune off it in Latin.

"I'm making this up as I go," he confided sideways, in English. "Don't tell her."

"You will live to a great age, madam," he told her, "and eat lentils without end."

"Any midwife would have told me that, and cheaper," said Grata, and left her palm in his.

"The street stays up," Felix told the dark from our doorway, and the dark paid him no heed. Valens had Lucius asleep on him with the ball squeezed between them, and Aelia slept in her basket under the bench with the doll's crooked leg in her mouth.

The jug stood empty in front of Cerdo.

When it ran dry he had a laugh going at something of Grata's, and he was up with the jug before it finished. He crossed to the wine jar by the shelving and tipped it. He filled the jug and served the plank with it, cup by cup. Fenn's cup rose to meet it without Fenn's head turning from Grata's hand. Felix's rose mid-law, and then the jug was at my shoulder, and mine rose too.

He filled it to the brim without looking, and stopped at the brim.

I thanked him and drank.

The cup was still at my mouth when I understood. At the sound of wine pouring into the jug my arm had raised the cup, at the right height, and waited. He had filled it, and I had thanked him the way I thanked Grata for my change. He had already moved on toward Valens, and wine ran down over my knuckles.

Felix saw only that his guest was standing.

"Cerdo, that's a fine, sit down and drink it off, every drop of it!"

The table roared at him. Valens lifted the jug out of Cerdo's hands and stood it on the plank. Cerdo sat down grinning, drank off his fine, and dried his mouth with the back of his burned wrist. The talk went on. No one at that table had seen anything but a guest forgetting himself and paying for it, which was a joke, and a good one, and the table had its fun out of it.

"Look at the color of you," Grata called over to me. "Blame the Falernian, menders have no head for good wine."

"It is the wine," I said.

I sat with my wet fingers in my lap. I had been pleased with myself since lamp-lighting: the bowl carried to him first, the stool with a back, the nineteen tapers. My hand had not been told any of that; it had heard the jug filling and lifted the cup.

Opposite me Cerdo was explaining the bakery's flue to Fenn, with gestures.

"It goes up, see, then over, so, and the smoke sits in the bend and won't come out, and I told him at the Kalends, and he says it draws."

"If it does not draw," said Fenn, "he must straighten it."

"You tell him, he takes a Chaldean's word."

"I shall put it in his stars."

"Cerdo!" The shout crossed from the bakery at the hour of the second firing, in his master's voice, through the shutter. Cerdo was up before the second shout, and I pushed into his hands the honey cakes I had tied up in a cloth for him, all that were left. He unknotted the bundle, chose two, and returned the rest to me.

"For the boy on our mill," he said. "He slept his day off."

"Take them all."

"He's ten, he'd be sick." He put the two away in his tunic. To Felix he said, "Majesty."

"Come back next year," said Felix. "I'll be king again."

"If he lets me." To me Cerdo said, "Good pork," and then our sill was empty.

"Straighten that flue!" Fenn called after him.

I watched him cross from our doorway; halfway over he pulled the felt cap off his head and stuffed it in his belt, then went in at the baker's door, which shut behind him.

"Mind you don't wake my bench," Grata said at the door. She took her pot home, and her empty jar, but left her bench, because Fenn was asleep on it, face to the plaster, hat tipped down and the felt cap beneath.

"Best night of the whole festival," Felix told me, yawning. He carried a blanket through to the mules, still crowned, and lay against the dun in the straw, and she shifted over for him. Valens carried Lucius through to his pallet, and by his return I had started to clear.

Valens stacked the stools. "Cerdo sang well."

"He sang terribly."

"He knew the words."

"I knew mine."

"You stopped."

"I forgot the rest."

He let it alone, and I scraped the bowls into Grata's pot for the morning and gathered the cups to take out to the step. As I made to clear the pits from the plank, his hand got there first and spread over them.

"Leave those."

"They will bring in mice."

"He's to show me the stealing. Tomorrow, before he goes."

"He said in the spring, at Puteoli."

"Both." The hand came away.

I left the field where Fenn had laid it and wiped the plank round it. I carried the cups out to the step and rinsed them in the bucket, singly. It was late and cold on the step and the cups took a while. I had made my case all week, and I believed it; I still believed it at the bucket, rinsing, with the cup I had held out going round in the water among the others.

The tapers inside were down to their last finger of wax, and I blew them dark, every one, until only the brazier was left. Back on the step for the last cup, I found the street dark both ways as far as the fountain. Under the bakers' shutter the oven was showing a strip of light on the stones again, and somebody was feeding it.
