Rome reached me first through the curtains of a hired chair, as noise: a stonecutter's mallet going somewhere overhead until a man on a scaffold drowned it, bawling down for his bucket, and was drowned in his turn by a peddler crying that he would take broken glass for sulfur matches, and the peddler by a dog that kept pace with the bearers half the length of a street giving its opinion of them, and all of it, whenever the poles swung riverward, by the smell of the Tiber, which was black mud, old drains and wet rope.

Nothing on wheels came inside the walls by daylight except the builders' carts. Everyone else walked or was carried, and at the Capena Gate the brother's freedman had taken a single look at my belly and gone off to bargain for the chair. I had wanted to see this city since I was eight and found it in the R volume of my father's encyclopedia, a tissue overlay of the Forum laid across a color plate of its ruins, and now I was inside it, tipping uphill in a box that smelled of the last passenger's hair oil, with my knees together and both hands on the child, while Valens walked at the front poles with our bag on his shoulder.

"What is out there?" I said through the gap in the curtain.

"People." He stood aside for a porter with half a sheep across his back. "A temple. Another temple. They're building everything at once."

The freedman had arrived at the harbor wine shop in Puteoli on the morning of the Kalends of March and found us already at a table, with our bag between our feet and a jug we had been making last since dawn. He had paid for everything since without being asked, and he watched Valens, when he thought nobody saw, as a buyer might watch a mule he had been promised was quiet.

Felix had stayed in Pompeii with the string, which the contractor had taken for the spring, and with Grata to feed him. He trailed our cart as far as the Herculaneum Gate, kicking a stone along ahead of him, and at the gate he booted it over the verge and turned home for the mules.

The house the brother had taken for the suit stood high on the Quirinal in a street of blind walls and good doors. It belonged to a widow who let it by the month to people with business at law, and we had a month in it before the court sat. Her husband's family still lived in the hall, in a row of painted cupboards along one wall with their little doors shut; the crack of one showed the yellow wax of somebody's nose.

Plautia was standing by the rain pool when the bearers set the chair down. She kept her place while I was extracted from it, which took the porter and Valens both, and while I found my feet on the widow's marble, and I had leisure enough for her hair.

It was built up over her forehead in the Roman fashion, a high front of tight curls in rows, each row stepped back above the one below it, and the way it stood told me there was a frame inside: a crescent of cane, I thought, or of wire wrapped in hair, with every curl pinned to it separately and the whole of it lashed to the plait behind. Somebody had worked on it since before dawn, and it made her a hand taller and covered both her ears.

"You are welcome to this house," she said, "which is not my brother's, and not mine, but belongs to a widow who lets it, together with her husband's ancestors, to anyone who has a lawsuit and the rent, and who has promised us that the bedroom behind the garden is the quietest on the hill; it is yours, and tonight you will learn what the Quirinal means by quiet. How many months?"

"Five. Nearly six."

"I asked my brother to bring you. Shaking you up the Appian Way in that condition was his own idea."

"The carriage was very good. It was the chair that rocked."

She looked at the freedman, and the freedman found something that needed him in the vestibule.

"My brother has hired a hairdresser who does the hair of a consul's wife," she said. "I am supposed to appear never to have left Rome in my life. Do I?"

"You look taller."

"That was not my question." She crossed the marble then and took both my hands, and held them out from my sides so that she could see the rest of me, and her fingers were cold. "Valens. Does it weigh on her?"

He had dropped our bag. "She doesn't say."

"No. She would not." Plautia let my hands go and turned to him. "My brother had the freedman's purse counted out for two, and then for one again, and he would have sworn to you that it would be one, or none at all."

"We were at Puteoli on the Kalends."

"So the freedman wrote. He was a little hurt by it; he had brought a book along to read while he waited, and never got past the first column. Myrrhine will show you the room."

In the evening the men dined with our advocate, and Plautia and I climbed a ladder onto a corner of the roof where the widow kept rosemary in pots, a row of them along the parapet in good red ware with a stamped rim, one of which had been mended across the belly with lead staples by somebody who knew what he was doing. From there, over the near roofs and the smoke of the valley between, the second hill over lay dark already, pricked by a few lamps.

"That is a good pot," I said. "Whoever stapled it was better at his trade than whoever threw it."

"Take it. Take it home with you to Campania; you have more use for a pot than I do." She had her hand on its rim before she remembered. "It belongs to the widow."

"So it does."

She left her hand on the rim, then took it away and put both hands under her cloak. "When the judge sits, my brother's advocate will read my dowry out to the court piece by piece, as though I were the hold of a ship, and the advocate across the aisle will say that the sixth part he kept back from it was the price of my character, which the law allows him, and nobody will ask me a single thing, because I am the one person in the whole matter whom nobody needs to hear. What will they ask him?"

"His name."

"First. Then about me."

"And he will say what happened."

"He has no other way of speaking. In two years he never told me a thing was well when it was not, not the weather and not my husband's temper. Of everything about him, that alone ever frightened me."

She turned to the far hill. "The Esquiline. My son is over there with his nurse, in a house his father has taken for the year, because a candidate for the praetorship must be seen to dine in Rome. He was born the autumn after the fire, when half this city was still black, and his father's mother had him wrapped in a shawl that smelled of smoke for a month, because nothing in her house would wash clean." She did not point. "I have stood here every evening for eleven days. Did he come because you asked him to?"

"He brought me."

"You have not answered me."

"It is the only answer I have."

"Then I shall take it. On the day, you will sit beside me, and you will not touch my veil, whatever I do with my hands."

* * *

The Basilica Julia was bigger inside than the concourse at Thirtieth Street Station and much louder, because four courts sat in it at once, all of them unwalled. Two stories of arcades ran round a nave as long as a city block, and the nave floor was laid in slabs of colored marble, yellow and purple-veined and gray, which rang under the hobnails of everybody walking on it. The courts sat on wooden platforms, each with its own crowd packed round it, and between them hung curtains on rods that kept out nothing whatever. Ours was in the south aisle, between two piers. Behind the curtain on our left a court of a hundred men was hearing a dispute over a will, and somebody there had brought friends to clap for him; on our right, a gaming board had been cut in the floor, where two clerks' boys were throwing dice until an attendant moved them on.

The judge had a chair on the platform and three of his friends on stools below him, to advise him. He was a lean old senator with the broad purple stripe on his tunic, and he listened with closed eyes and his hands folded on his stomach, so that for most of the first hour I took him to be asleep. At the platform's corner a slave sat beside a water clock, a bronze bowl on a stand with a jar beneath it. When an advocate rose the slave pulled a peg, and the water began to run, and when the jar was full the advocate's time was gone.

Plautia sat on the front bench behind our advocate, her veil drawn up to the eyes, between her brother and me, with Myrrhine at my elbow. Her brother was a heavy man with her nose and none of her stillness. Valens waited with the witnesses along the pier.

Celer came in late with eight or ten friends about him, all talking. He was browner than at Corinth, and his toga was new again. He saw Valens before anyone, and his face lit.

"Valens," he said, and crossed to us. "They tell me it's Valens now. I've been practicing it on my steward. And Mara has come all this way." He smiled down at me past Plautia's veil. "You'll have found Rome's all stairs. We're born to it here; the rest of you suffer. Somebody should have warned you." He went to his bench, and his friends sat round him like a family at the theater.

Our advocate, when he rose, was a short, square man with a hoarse voice and no gestures at all, and he read the dowry out in it: so much in coin and so much in land, a Syrian girl named Doris, earrings, a lampstand on lion's paws.

"At the divorce," he said, "the defendant gave back all of that but a sixth, which he kept for misconduct. Misconduct proved by two coached house slaves, and by the defendant's word, which he gave very sorrowfully, and by nothing else. One other person under that roof knew what was true. He is here. The court can hear him tomorrow."

The jar filled. Celer's advocate rose.

He was young, with a beautiful voice, and he kept his left hand inside the fold of his toga and spent the right one on the air.

"The court will not hear that man tomorrow," he said, "nor on any day. He is my client's property, bought off the lanista at Corinth a full month ahead of Caesar's games, and paid for in silver." He raised the bill, two boards bound with cord, with the seals pressed over the cord in a row. "I need not remind this court that a slave is not heard against his master. If the lanista afterward, with Caesar watching and the crowd on its feet, chose to free a man he had already sold, the show was the lanista's, and the clerk only copied it into his wax. Written fair, a mistake is still a mistake."

"Then the court should see the copy," said our advocate, and handed up the leaf.

At that distance its color told me which it was, for the wax had darkened since Corinth.

"There is no seal on this." Celer's advocate turned it over when it had been passed to him in its turn. "It has been cut from a book, with a knife. Will my friend tell the court where he had it?"

"Gladly, when his client tells the court what has become of the rest of the book." Our advocate kept his voice exactly where it was. "It is the first book of Vettius and Caninius, in Caesar's Greek year. The seals are on its knot, and the knot is wherever the book is. The leaf has the day, the lanista, the man freed, and the names of everyone who stood by and watched."

Celer laughed. It was a warm, easy laugh, and two of his friends laughed with him before they saw that nobody else had.

The judge looked out at us. "How is it proved?"

"By the men whose names are on it."

The brother's silver had brought two of them from Corinth, and all morning they had kept the end of our bench in their traveling cloaks. The first was an old dyer whose nails were still blue at the roots. He gave his name, and it was the fourth name on the leaf.

"I witnessed for a dozen that afternoon," he said. "The magistrate had us all in a row by his table in the shade, a lot of old men with nothing better to do, and they were brought from the arena to our table one after another to be freed, with the blood still on some of them. That man's face is nothing to me. I remember a murmillo bled on my good cloak, and his lanista paid me for it, too much. That may have been him."

"Did the lanista free him?"

"It was the lanista's hand on him while his name went down in the wax. I saw that."

The second was a fat merchant who had been seasick, he told the court, from Lechaion to the Italian shore and had no hope of being well again before the autumn. His name was the seventh.

"That's my name, and my father's after it. I was there. Caesar sat in his chair on the tribunal with a cloth wound round his throat, to keep his voice for the evening, and he never said a word all afternoon. They carried the practice sword up to him, and he put out a finger and touched it."

Celer's advocate let both of them go without a question.

"Nobody doubts that these honest men sat in the shade at Corinth and watched a clerk write," he said to the judge. "Neither of them can say whose man was written down. My client's bill can."

The youngest of the judge's three friends had the bill on his knees. He was turning the first board toward the light from the arcade, and then he leaned up to the judge's chair and said something, and the judge's eyes opened again.

"My friend's uncle was consul that year, for his turn," said the judge. "Your bill makes him consul in a month he spent at his villa at Tibur, complaining of the fellow who had the office."

"A steward's slip of the stylus," the young advocate said.

"My uncle will be sorry to hear it," said the young man on the stool. "He'd have liked the extra months."

That laugh went all round our aisle and into the next court, Celer's bench included. He sent his slave over with a cup of something cool for the seasick witness, and while the clock's jar was being emptied he leaned out from his bench toward Plautia's brother.

"Your man has a carter's lungs. I'd hire him myself if I could get him away from you; mine sings."

The brother looked at him and said nothing, and Celer, quite content, sat back among his friends. Our advocate, without gestures, returned to the steward's slip, set it beside the leaf and the dyer's cloak, and talked hoarsely until the jar was full again. The judge put the witness off until morning and went to his lunch.

* * *

The widow's back bedroom was quiet until well after dark, and then the carts came.

Everything that could not cross Rome by day crossed it in the dark. It went past under our wall, on iron tires, over paving that threw the noise straight up: timber and lime, a train of wine jars clinking in their straw, a whole herd of something with hoofs, and the drovers swearing at all of it in more accents than I knew. The bed shook a little with the heavier loads. The child turned over and back, then pushed a foot out against my ribs; I lay holding it through my own skin and listened to Rome being fed.

"That one's losing a wheel," Valens said, close by my ear.

"From up here?"

"It knocks every turn." He was lying on his back with his arms folded under his head, and his elbow touched my hair. "Hear it. He'll have it off before he reaches the river."

We listened to the knocking go away downhill. The child shoved again, and I lifted his hand from the pillow and set it on the place.

"There," I said.

He lay still under it. It came again under his palm, a hard small push, and went, and returned an inch further round.

"That's a heel," he said.

"You can tell that through me?"

"It's the shape of one." He left his hand where it was.

"Where do I stand," he said, "tomorrow?"

"Where the dyer stood, next to the slave with the clock."

"And look at who?"

"At the judge."

"The judge keeps his eyes shut. And Celer sat on his left today, with the light behind him."

"You need not look at him at all. Look to the back," I said. "At the end wall, where the idlers lean. Talk to them."

He was silent until I thought he slept. A second cart went by with a bad wheel, or the earlier one returning; somewhere near the river a drover started singing.

"I'll talk to them," he said, and turned on his side toward the wall, and through the carts, and the singing, and the knocking of the next wheel and the next, he slept.

* * *

On the bench opposite ours a little man in a dun cloak had brought his dinner in a napkin, half a loaf and a raw onion the size of a fist. He peeled the onion with his thumbnail while our advocate was getting up and began to eat it in bites, and whenever the curtain beside him stirred, the smell reached us.

Celer's advocate objected again. "The court will remember whose property it is about to hear."

"I'll hear him first," the judge said, "and decide afterward what he's worth."

Valens went to the end of the platform in a tunic of the brother's, fresh from the fuller and tight across his back. He looked over the judge's head and ours, over the women's places and the men standing behind them, and past them down the aisle to the last pier, where the idlers leaned, in from the glare, and he said it to them.

"Gaius Iulius Valens. Freed at Corinth."

He pitched it as he would hail the string in a crosswind, and it went the length of the aisle and out beneath the arcade. Beyond the curtain, the reader of the will lost his place in it, and had to be told where he had got to. At the back, an idler who had been asleep against a pillar there came off it upright and asked the man next to him something, and the man next to him said the name back.

Plautia's hands were in her lap, and she had taken the fringed edge of her veil between two fingers.

"Where were you born?"

"Thrace."

Opposite, the little man in the dun cloak bit into his onion, and Valens's eyes went to it.

"They grew those," he said. "Every house. By the autumn you could smell the village before you saw it."

"The court is not buying onions," the judge remarked.

Our advocate supplied the next question, and the next, and Valens answered them as he answered everything, in as few words as would carry the thing across.

"How did you come to Corinth?"

"I was taken as a boy, and sold there, into the school."

"And freed?"

"At Caesar's games, the last afternoon. The wooden sword came down to me from the tribunal, and I was walked to the magistrate's table, and I stood while his clerk wrote."

"Whose hand was on your shoulder while he wrote?"

"The lanista's."

"And afterward, who paid you?"

"The legate's steward. On the Kalends, in silver, with my keep and a cloak for the winter, to go where the lady went, a pace back."

"In silver," our advocate repeated. "To his own slave." Then, to Valens: "Tell the judge about the night."

"It was summer. Vitellius had Rome." He kept his eyes off Plautia, and she kept hers off him; she had the whole fringe of her veil wound round her fingers now. "The steward came for me two hours after dark and said the master wanted me in the atrium. Every lamp in the atrium was lit, more than for guests. The lady was there by the household gods, in what she slept in, with a cloak somebody had put round her. The master had two of the house slaves standing at the pool. He asked them what they'd seen, and they said they'd seen me in her bed. Her hairdresser said it first. She was a girl, and she wept while she said it. The stable boy said it after her, to the steward. The master said he was sorry. He said it to me, and to the lady, and he said it to the gods in the cupboard."

"Were you ever in her bed?"

"I was never inside her door. I lay outside it, on the tiles, with my back against the wood. If she'd wanted out in the night she'd have had to step over me."

The man with the onion sneezed.

It was a sneeze that started far off and gave notice of itself. His head went back, and his hand rose with the onion still in it, and the whole aisle had time to watch him draw his breath for it before it came, and when it came it was enormous.

"Salve," said the bench round him, all together. "Salve," said the women's bench, and the idlers at the back. One of the judge's friends said it. The judge, eyes still closed, said it.

"Thank you," said the sneezer, half rising to bow left and right. "Thank you. And to you. Thank you."

Valens kept the next word ready and waited while the court finished wishing the man good health.

It started under the child: I felt it begin there, a jolt low down, and the child jolted back at it, and then it was up in my shoulders and would not leave them. I bent forward over the child, as far as it allowed, and gripped the bench's edge in my two fists, and the bench shook under them. Myrrhine's thigh was against mine, and I felt her feel it, and to my left Plautia went stiller than she already was. My eyes ran, and my nose ran with them. My heels had begun knocking on the marble by themselves, and nothing in my legs would stop them. Somewhere in all that I sweated through my shift, which was the worst thing yet and made it much worse. He sneezed again, smaller, and one old voice at the rear of the aisle said "Salve" to him all by itself, kindly, and that finished me.

The front of the court had gone to a blur. I heard the judge say, "Take the lady outside," and heard the beautiful voice of Celer's advocate say that the defendant had no objection whatever to the lady's being taken outside, none, and then there was a hand under my elbow and a staff close by my cheek, and one of the basilica's attendants was lifting me off the bench without any hurry at all; from his manner, this was his daily work. Myrrhine took my other arm, and together we walked the aisle past the sneezer, who twisted round on his bench to watch me go, wiping his nose on his wrist and very interested.

Behind me Valens's voice began again, level, on whatever came after the floor outside her room, and the curtain swung across it.

* * *

The attendant set me down on the Basilica's steps with a word to Myrrhine that I did not catch, and returned to his staff and his morning. I sat where I had been put. For a while the laughing went on by itself in my shoulders, without me, and then it ran down and left me with wet cheeks, a wet shift and a light, sick, scoured feeling, as after an hour's crying.

Myrrhine shook out the cloak she had over her arm and spread it on the step.

"Up a little. Sit on this. It's only my second-best, and the fuller on this street wants a sestertius to look at a stain, I asked him yesterday about the lady's, so I'll do it myself and he can whistle." She sat down beside me and took off one shoe, and then the other. They were Roman shoes, red, bought on the second day, and they had been hot coals to her ever since. "If you want to laugh again, laugh now, out here, where it costs nothing."

"Did he see?"

"Everybody saw. The judge saw, and he was asleep."

"Valens."

"He stood and waited for the bench to stop. He's good at waiting, that one. I was holding you up; I'd only an eye for you." She worked her toes on the warm marble. "She was shaking too, my lady. Under the veil. I had my arm round you and my hand on her. I felt it. You didn't see that."

"Laughing?"

"I didn't ask her."

I wanted to look at the step.

Beside my hip the step had a gaming board scratched in it, a grid of little round cups scored in the marble in three rows. Two porters in leather aprons were playing on its neighbor, with walnuts, and arguing about a walnut. In July of 1991 these steps had been a platform of broken brick and marble with grass growing in the joints. I had sat on the top of it with my sandals off and a peach from a stall near the station, twenty-six, three weeks into my first summer abroad, and found a board like this one where my hand lay, and put my fingertips into each of its cups in turn until a custodian in a blue shirt blew his whistle at me for sitting on the monument. I had left with my peach and my face hot. That board had been worn into shallow dishes by thumbs. This one still had the chisel in it, and my fingertips came away from its cups gray with grit.

"Is it a game?" said Myrrhine.

"I never learned the rules."

"That makes it a game." She stood, holding her shoes. "Will you go back in?"

"Not to the court."

"Walk, then. Your legs will stop shaking if you give them something to do."

She got me to my feet. I had known this place as stumps and lines in the grass, as columns that stopped at the knee and a guide's paper plan held flat against the wind, and it was all standing. Facing the Basilica's steps, where I had sat with my peach under three tall columns holding up a scrap of cornice and no more, the temple of Castor stood whole on its high platform, every column of it, with its roof on and the acanthus on its capitals painted, and men sitting on its steps to do business in the shade. The round temple of Vesta had a wisp of smoke rising from the middle of its roof. Outside it, a line of women waited with little dishes in their hands, and a girl in white above them was spooning gray ash into each dish from a crock: calf's ash, a horse's dried blood and bean stalks, against the Parilia, to be burned and stepped through. Ovid had stood in that line once with his hands full, and the women in it now gossiped and moved up.

"What are they buying?" said Myrrhine.

"It is given."

"Nothing's given," said Myrrhine, and went to look at the crock herself, barefoot, with her red shoes in her hand.

* * *

The stair to the gallery climbed inside the Basilica's far end, steep and worn. At the top of it the idlers, with the women who had no seats below, leaned on a parapet over the whole of the nave, and I squeezed in between two pillars. From the parapet the courts were four crowds round small platforms, their curtains moving whenever somebody pushed through, and I heard all of them at once, four rooms of one party.

Our judge's head shone bald under me, and the slave at the clock had changed his jar. Valens had not moved from his place. Celer's advocate had closed on him, with his free hand open.

The woman wedged in beside me had a swaddled baby sleeping in the crook of her arm. "Where's this gladiator?"

"The one standing still."

"Oh, him." She turned her shoulder, baby and all, to the will again.

"In the street behind her litter," the advocate was saying, "at the door of her bath, on the floor outside her room at night, so you tell us, with one door between you: a man young and strong, unmarried, used to taking what the crowd threw him, and she not thirty and, by every account but my client's, unhappy. Did you never want her?"

"I watched the street."

"The street."

"Who was on the roofs, and where the dogs were."

"And the steward paid you, you say. Masters give slaves an allowance; that is not wages."

"He paid me what he paid the cook's freedman. Ask the cook."

"I would rather ask you." The advocate turned a little, so that the benches had his profile. "You were a gladiator. For how many years did you kill men on the sand, for money, for the crowd's pleasure?"

Valens let the question go by. He stood with his arms hanging and his gaze at the aisle's end, and the water ran in the clock.

"That's not in the matter," said the judge. "Go on."

"As the court pleases," said Celer's advocate. "It knows my client's family, and it can see the witness."

Our advocate stayed on his bench. "The court is asked to take a consul's dates against a steward's," he said.

The water ran out. Celer's advocate let his hand drop and took his seat. Our advocate said he had nothing to add that the court had not seen with its own eyes, and the judge promised his sentence on the third day, in this place, at mid-morning. Then he put on his outdoor shoes, which a slave held for him, and the platform began to empty.

Valens stepped down and looked first at the place where I had been, and Myrrhine's place beside it, both empty. He stood looking at them while the crowd went round him, until our advocate returned for him and touched his arm.

* * *

He found me that evening in the widow's back bedroom with my shift soaking in a basin, which Myrrhine had offered to see to; I had refused her.

"Was it the sneeze?" he asked.

"The judge said salve."

He settled on the bed's foot and thought about it. "He did."

"I am sorry. After it began, you were gone. Everything was."

"They asked me the rest." He unlaced one boot.

"And you answered?"

"What they asked." He pulled the boot off. "The cook's freedman would've backed me. They never sent for him."

"Was Celer watching you?"

"All through. His feet never moved." He unlaced the other boot and set the pair under the bed with the toes outward, ready, the way he always set them. "At Corinth, when he was kept waiting for his dinner, his right foot used to go on the floor under the table until the dish came."

He was asleep before the lamp wanted trimming. Later Plautia scratched at the curtain and came in without waiting to be asked, with her built hair taken down for the night and plaited loose over one shoulder, which made her look younger and less sure of her hands. She carried something folded.

"Myrrhine tells me you will not let her have your shift."

"It is not her work."

"All the work in this house is hers; she has decided so." She laid the folded thing across the blanket at his feet, and I saw that it was a shift of her own, fine Egyptian linen with a border worked in green at the neck, much better than mine. "It will be long on you. Turn the hem up. Were you watching his face, when he said where he slept?"

"No."

"Nor was I. I had my eyes on my hands." She considered them now, in the lamplight. "Myrrhine says that I shook. I have not laughed where any friend of that house could see me in five years, and I had no intention of beginning on the bench of a court. What I did, I cannot say. I would be grateful if nobody told me." She went out as she had come, without a good night.

In the morning Myrrhine looked at the shift on me and said she would have it back off me before I left Rome, and wash it first, and that I was not to grow any bigger in it in the meantime. The shift was too long for me and I wore it anyway.

The judge took his three days, and in them Myrrhine and I walked the Sacred Way down and over the Velia to the valley beyond it, where Nero had had a lake.

The lake was gone, and in its place stood the beginning of the amphitheatre, not a third built. The outer wall rose in arcades of pale stone, two tiers of arches high in some places and a single tier in others, and above that it was all scaffold: poles lashed with rope in a lattice the height of the curve, planks laid across them, men on the planks, ladders between them, and above everything, at intervals, the arm of a crane with a great wooden wheel at its foot and men walking inside the wheel like hamsters. Oxcarts came in at a crawl, one behind the other, loaded with stone from the quarries in the hills toward Tibur, by road, at the pace of the oxen. The noise was the noise of the whole city poured into one valley. Everything was the color of the stone, the men included.

The blocks went up dry, without mortar. I watched a gang set one on the top course: they lowered it onto the one below, levered it into line, and then a boy with a ladle edged along the scaffold from a brazier and poured lead into the socket where an iron cramp tied it to its neighbor. The lead fumed white and sweet-smelling, and the boy blew on his ladle and trotted off to the fire for more. The stone had holes in it of its own, like bread. I stood at the barrier and watched them fill the next socket and the next.

It was a place for men to fight in, while Rome watched them. I was thinking that, in the white dust, when a carter bellowed at us to stand off from his oxen, and Myrrhine hauled me back, and the cart went past so close that the stone on it left its dust on my arm.

"And what will it be?" said Myrrhine.

"Games."

"They've a theater for games already. Two. I've seen them." She was barefoot again; the red shoes hung from her wrist by their laces. "Who pays for all that?"

"The emperor. Out of Judaea."

"He got it cheap, the emperor," said Myrrhine.

We went home by the Argiletum, for its shops, and she walked the whole of it barefoot, over whatever the paving had on it, talking. She had been given to Plautia at her wedding, out of her father's house, a girl of twelve to carry the bride's slippers.

"I had the boy on my hip the whole of his first year. He bit my ear and hung on, and laughed, and his ears went red, and I couldn't get him off."

"Will he know her?"

"He knows her. He'll make out that he doesn't, because the nurse will be watching." She stopped at a cobbler's stall and turned a sandal over, and put it down, and we went on. "He's his father's, that way. He'll have learned it."

At every crossroads on our road home, men were building heaps of straw and bean stalks higher than their heads, for the Parilia, which fell the morning after the judge's third day.

* * *

The judge gave his sentence in fewer words than our advocate had spent on the dowry's earrings, and without opening his eyes for it.

"I find for the plaintiff," he said, and behind the left-hand curtain somebody's friends began clapping for their own man and kept it up. "The defendant will restore the dowry whole, the sixth part he kept back for the lady's conduct together with the rest, within the time the law gives him. As to the bill of sale, I have given it the weight it deserves, and my young friend's uncle sends the defendant his compliments."

Plautia's brother leaned across to our advocate. "The sixth?"

"With the rest."

Nobody said the name of the witness. Plautia's brother cracked his knuckles, one hand and then the other, and rose. Plautia did not move until he had stood, and then she stood up beside him, and I saw the wound cloth come loose from her fingers.

Her brother faced Valens, now standing beside the bench.

"My sister will want to thank you, and make a poor job of it," he said, "so I'll do it now, and spare you both. Thank you. The freedman will take you to the Capena Gate whenever you choose, with his purse." He thought, and added, "And a better chair."

"I would rather walk to the gate," I said. "I should like to see the city once from outside a box."

"He can walk beside you and carry the chair's price, in case you change your mind at the first hill."

Celer came across the aisle to shake our advocate's hand, and the brother's. "Perfectly right, of course. I'd have found the same myself; I said so to my man at breakfast, and he'll swear I did." Then he went out first, still talking, and waited for us outside in the sun.

A slave stood on a ladder against the nearest pier there, tying a swag of greenery under the cornice for tomorrow. Celer stepped down two steps and along, round the foot of the ladder, and climbed back beyond it without once losing the thread of what he was saying to the brother.

"... and I'm told your man said something on the second day about the Cornelian law. Nobody wants the Cornelian law. Do we? My steward's old and wretched about it all, I've had him in tears twice; he'll see to the silver, he likes to be useful. We'd all like a quiet summer." One of his feet had begun to go on the step. He turned to Plautia with the air of having just thought of it. "The lady shall have the boy to dine at her brother's every market day while she's in Rome, and at Corinth whenever she sends for him, and the nurse with him, of course. And let's have no more talk of laws. I had him brought down this morning. I thought, why not begin today? It's spring."

Plautia gave him no word.

Celer kept Valens for last. "You've a carrying voice, Valens. I'd forgotten it."

"You never heard it," said Valens.

Celer laughed, delighted. "And Mara. I hear there's to be a child; my congratulations, truly. They grow so fast. You look round, and there's a boy at the bottom of the steps who wants his dinner." He descended to his litter, and his friends went with him.

The boy waited where the steps met the street, with a Greek pedagogue's hand on his shoulder. His toga had a purple border, and his hair had been wetted and combed flat, and his ears poked out on either side. He held himself very straight, and when the pedagogue released him he climbed toward his mother a step and then the next, each one counted out for him beforehand by somebody, and stopped two below her.

"Mother, I greet you," he said. "I hope that I find you well, and I am to say that I am glad to see you." He stared at her hair. "Your hair's different. Does Uncle's dog still bite? The one on the chain? Does it remember me?"

"It bit a magistrate last summer," said Plautia.

He climbed the last two and caught the edge of her veil, where it hung at her elbow, and held on to it. She kept her hands empty and let him hold it. The porters were at their walnuts again on the step by my feet; one of them won something, and swore happily.

Then Plautia lifted her eyes from her son, and found Valens on the step above me, and looked at him a while.

"Marcus," she said, "this is Valens. He used to carry you. Greet him."
