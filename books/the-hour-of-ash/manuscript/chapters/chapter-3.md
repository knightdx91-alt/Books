# Chapter 3: Parnassus

The thyme grew in low gray cushions up the whole slope, and whenever the wind came over the shoulder of the mountain it pressed the smell out of them and carried it down past me toward the plateau, where the bells of a flock were knocking and clanking without any tune at all and the road lay across the bottom of everything like a dropped bootlace, so far below me that a truck crawling along it made no sound.

I had been climbing for half an hour and had already stopped more often than I liked; the duffel was most of it. In all its years with me I had asked nothing steeper of that canvas bag than the subway stairs at City Hall. Now its strap had found the one place on my right shoulder where there was bone and no padding, and it was working at that place with the patience of a wood rasp. My shoulder hurt and I moved the bag to the other shoulder and then that one hurt. My lungs had spent ten years in a basement lab. The air up here was thin and clean and smelled of resin and hot stone, and I was hauling it in through my mouth.

The taxi had left me at the end of the asphalt a little after nine. Its driver was a young man from Arachova with a string of blue glass eyes swinging from his mirror. He had taken the road out of his village and up across the high plain at a speed that swung the eyes out sideways on their string and kept them there. Where the asphalt gave out and the road went on as two ruts of white gravel he had stopped, and pointed at the slope above us with his whole arm, and then at the bag, and last of all at me, and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes on the back of his wrist. I paid him what he asked without understanding the number. He turned the car around in a spray of gravel and drove off.

I had given up my room in Delphi at seven. It was a room over a shop that sold blue worry beads and plaster copies of the Charioteer, at the top of the town, with a balcony the width of a bookshelf and a view straight down into a gray-green sea of olive trees that ran on to the water at Itea. I had wound the Seiko out there that morning, leaning on the rail, while the swifts went screaming past below my feet. The woman at the desk downstairs had asked whether I was going back to Athens. I had said no, up. Fenn had slept up there. He had climbed to the cave day after day for most of a summer, and I had not flown across an ocean to stand in it for an hour like a woman in a gallery and catch the four o'clock bus.

The plateau lay below me entire. It was a long floor of new grass laid flat between the mountains and walled in by them, green in a way nothing in Philadelphia was ever green in May, a wet, almost violent green with yellow in it. The map called the plain Livadi. The shepherds' summer huts of Kalivia stood about on it in loose clusters, rough gray stone with tin roofs rusted orange at the seams; between them were pens of piled stone and a white church hardly bigger than a garage, with its bell hung in an iron frame beside the door because it had no tower to put it in. Out on the flat there were sheets of standing water where the snow had melted and had nowhere to go. A black plastic bag had blown against one of the pen walls and stayed there, filling and emptying with the wind. The road came across all of it from the Arachova side and climbed the far slope toward the ski runs, where the lifts stood idle for the summer with their chairs hanging in a row. A white van was going along it now with its windshield winking, and whatever noise it made went off down the valley on the wind.

I got the Pentax out of the end pocket of the bag and photographed the plateau from above, the whole green floor of it with the road across the bottom, because my father had always taken that picture first. Every tray of his slides had begun with one. It was the site from a hilltop before a spade had touched it, the house they lived in a white speck and the trenches not yet cut, and he called it the only honest picture of a season. I had sat through a great many of them at the kitchen table in the dark and thought them the dullest photographs in the world. I framed mine the way he framed his, the horizon a third of the way from the top. The shutter went; the mirror slapped up and dropped inside the body with a clack the mountain swallowed at once, and I wound on with my thumb. I had spent a little of the roll in Delphi already, on the stadium at the top of the sanctuary in the last of the light.

The map was in my jacket with Fenn's notebook. I had bought it at a kiosk in Delphi the afternoon I arrived, a walker's map of Parnassus folded into a cardboard cover with a snowy summit on the front, and it had degrees and minutes printed in its margins, which Fenn's chart had never had. That first evening I had sat on the hotel bed and laid the straight edge of the notebook along the grid from 38° 31′ and from 22° 32′; the two lines had met on the mountain a few hundred yards from a small black symbol and the words ΚΩΡΥΚΕΙΟ ΑΝΤΡΟ. A minute of latitude covered better than a mile on the ground, and the cave sat comfortably inside it. He had read the map right in a library in Philadelphia in 1967 without ever having seen the mountain, and then he had come out here and found the hole where his numbers said it was.

The path was marked in red. Every fifty yards there was a square of red paint on a boulder, sometimes with an arrow under it and sometimes with a white bar through the middle, laid on with a brush by a hand that had not worried about drips; the paint had run in places and dried in little beads down the face of the stone. Lower down, a yellow-and-black metal sign on a post had said E4 and pointed along a different line entirely, toward the summits. I kept to the red. Where the path crossed bare rock it was easy to lose. The limestone up here was pale gray and fluted all over by the rain into channels and knife-edged ridges, and the red squares were the only things on it that had been put there by a person.

At one of the squares I stopped to retie a bootlace that had worked loose. Its plastic tip had come off somewhere between Kennedy and here, and the end was fraying out into a little brush. Aglet. A boy at the citywide spelling bee in 1977 had gone out on aglet with two g's and stood at the microphone in his clip-on tie with his mouth open while the bell rang. I pulled the knot tight and went on.

The goats were above the path as I came round the next shoulder of the hill, forty or fifty of them spread across the slope among the thyme and the scrub oak. They were black and brown and a few of a dirty white, with long hair hanging off their flanks in hanks, and they wore bells of every size; the big ones clanked, the small ones went *tink*, and nothing anywhere kept time with anything else. A kid was standing on top of a boulder with all four feet together on a point of rock the size of a saucer. The goatherd sat on a flat stone below it. He was an old man in a flat cap and a gray suit jacket over a sweater, with a crook across his knees, and he was shouting up the slope at one brown nanny who had got onto a ledge above the rest and was eating something there that she preferred. It was the same word every time, her name or his opinion of her, and he said it with more disgust at every return. She did not come down. He broke off for me in the middle of the word.

"Kalimera," he said.

"Kalimera sas."

My kalimera was correct, which my Greek mostly was not. I had learned the language from grammars with paper covers and could get through a page of Thucydides faster than I could buy a stamp in it. My accent did not interest him in the least. Then he finished the word, and the nanny went on eating, and the kid on the boulder watched me go by with its yellow eyes and their flat black bars.

A little higher there was a shepherd's pen beside the path whose wall had fallen in at one corner and been built up again by a man who had not cared for the first man's way of doing it. The old stones were set on edge, leaning into each other in a long herringbone. The new ones had been laid flat in courses with no opinion about the herringbone at all, so that the corner stood out from twenty yards off as plainly as a patch on a sleeve, and in the join, for a gate hinge, he had mortared in a length of bedspring. I photographed the join. I took it again from closer, with the bedspring sharp and the herringbone soft behind it, and I was more pleased with that one than with anything I had taken in Delphi.

Above the pen the path went into the firs. They were tall and dark and very straight, with silver-gray bark, and their cones stood upright along the tops of the branches like unlit candles. The ground under them was brown with needles and soft underfoot, and it was cooler there. The resin smell was so thick I could taste it at the back of my throat, turpentine and the pine-oil cleaner the night men used on the museum corridors. A man came down through the trees toward me with two walking poles going ahead of him like a second pair of legs. He was perhaps sixty-five and tall, with a red face, white knee socks, a red windbreaker and a canvas hat with a cord under the chin, and he stopped above me on the path and planted the poles.

"Good morning!" he said. "English?"

"American."

"Ach so. Good morning all the same." He tapped the bag with the tip of a pole. "You are carrying your whole house."

"Just my clothes."

"For one night?"

"However long it takes."

He laughed, though I had not said it to be funny, and dug in the pocket of his windbreaker and brought out a stone. It was gray and a little smaller than a hen's egg, and he showed it to me on his palm.

"From the top. Liakoura, this morning, at six, with the sun coming. For my daughter. Every mountain I climb, one stone, and she keeps them on the windowsill in her kitchen in Ulm. Olympus she has. Snowdon, which is not a mountain, but it rained the whole day, so it is allowed. Her husband says one day the windowsill falls into the street and kills a man." He tipped the stone to the light with great satisfaction. "This is a good one. Look, it has a little shell inside."

It had; a ribbed fan the breadth of a fingernail, set in the gray.

My father had brought me sherds. There had been one in his shirt pocket every September, wrapped in a page of the *Cyprus Mail*: a scrap of a cooking pot with the soot still on its belly, the rim of a lamp, a strap handle with the potter's thumbprint pressed into its root. I had kept them in a Buster Brown shoebox beneath the bed and taken them out on rainy Saturdays to sort by color. I was twenty-four and halfway through a seminar on the antiquities trade before I understood what each of them was. The box was on the top shelf of my closet now, behind the winter sweaters, and I had not opened it since the seminar and had not thrown it out.

"How far is the cave?" I said.

"For me, forty minutes. For you, with the house, one hour." He swung round and pointed up through the trees with a pole. "Follow the red. Always the red, on the stones. The yellow and black is the E4, it goes over the summit and down the other side and on, many days, to the sea. You do not want the E4."

"I'll skip the sea."

"And at two o'clock comes the cloud, up from the gulf. Every day this week, two o'clock, half past two. Inside it you see nothing, not the path, not your own feet. Be down before."

"I'm staying up."

He leaned on his poles and revised his opinion of the duffel. "You have a good sleeping bag." It was not a question, and I did not tell him that I had a change of clothes, a toothbrush and a box and a half of granola bars. "You are a student? Archaeology? Every second person on this mountain is digging something."

"I fix pots. In a museum."

"Ah! My daughter's husband, the one with the windowsill, he fixes our cups. The handles. He glues them back on with something from a tube that smells of fish, and then you drink your coffee and the handle stays in your hand and the cup goes in your lap." He let go of one pole and crooked a finger through a handle that was not there. "Where in America?"

"Philadelphia."

"Philadelphia! Like the cheese."

"Like the cheese."

"My wife, she eats it on the bread every morning of her life, and me also." Something occurred to him then, and his face changed, and he unzipped the windbreaker and drew a camera up on a cord from inside it, a small silver Olympus whose lens slid out with a whine when he switched it on. He offered it to me in both hands. "Bitte. One photograph, with the mountain behind. My wife is in Arachova with her knee, and she says I take the bus to the top and walk down and tell her stories. Here I am with the mountain. She cannot argue. Only press. It thinks for itself."

The camera weighed nothing; it was all plastic and confidence. When I put my eye to it there was a little bright window with no needle in it and nothing to set, and I pressed the button and it made up its own mind about the light with a buzz and a click. He stood very straight for it with the poles crossed in front of him and the firs at his back, and he did not smile, because this was evidence.

"Now you," he said.

"It's all right, I don't—"

"No, no. For your mother."

"My mother's in Phoenix."

"Phoenix. Good, we send it to Phoenix." He had my father's camera before I could think how to keep it, and then I had to show him everything. He had never held a camera that did not think for itself. I showed him the focusing ring and the needle in the viewfinder that had to be brought into the middle by turning the aperture ring, and he held the Pentax at arm's length and asked where the button was.

"On top. On the right."

"This?"

"That's the lever. The other one."

I stood on the path with the bag on my shoulder and the dark trees behind me, and he took a long time about it, with the tip of his tongue between his teeth. When the shutter went, with its heavy steel clack, he jerked the camera away from his face.

"So loud!" he said. "It is like a gun."

He gave it back strap first, carefully, and went off down the path toward the goats, poles first. The old man on the stone left off with the nanny to greet him. I heard the two voices meet below me and part, and then only the bells.

Above the firs the slope opened out again into broken rock and thyme, and the path climbed it in long zigzags. The wind was stronger up here and came in gusts, pushing at the duffel and flattening the grass, and in the lulls a bird I could not name went over and over a phrase of four notes with the last one missing. On the far side of the plateau the high summits showed now over the nearer ridges. Every gully on them was streaked with old snow, and the sky above was a blue so hard and clean that I kept glancing up at it. I stopped twice more. The second stop I sat on the duffel and drank half my water and ate a granola bar, and the wind snatched the wrapper from my fingers and carried it twenty feet downhill into a thornbush. I went after it, because I could not leave it there, and tore my sleeve on the thorns getting it back.

I saw the cave a good while before I reached it. It was a dark arch in a low cliff of gray rock on the hillside above me, wider than it was high, with a scrub oak growing out of a crack over its lip and leaning out over the drop. I knew the shape. On the chart in my father's study, beside the coordinates, Fenn had drawn a doorway in ballpoint smaller than a lentil, two posts and a curved top with its middle filled in black, and I had taken it in March for the way a child draws any cave at all. It was this cave. He must have copied it out of a photograph in some library book, because it was too exact for a guess; it had the same lean to the left-hand post where the rock had broken away, and the same flattening along the top. I climbed until I had the arch at the angle of the drawing and photographed it from there, with the oak and the lean and the dark inside.

After that the arch hardly got any nearer. Somewhere on the last zigzags the Hanson song from the summer before got into my head, only the chorus, which had no words in it I had ever been able to make out, and it went round and round in time with my boots. I was hungry again, and it was too soon for another granola bar.

The last of the paint was on a boulder at the final turn below the mouth, a gray block as broad as a kitchen table with its face toward the cave. It had been given a red square with an arrow under it pointing at the arch, which by then could hardly have been missed.

At the mouth itself the ground was trodden bare and littered with goat droppings, black and shiny as olive pits. There had been a fire in a ring of blackened stones just under the overhang, and a crushed plastic water bottle and the stub of a white church candle lay among the ashes. To the right of the arch the rock had been smoothed flat for a yard or so at about the height of my shoulder, and there were letters cut into it, Greek capitals, so worn that the lichen had got into most of them. I put my fingers in the strokes and read them like a blind woman. ΝΥΜΦ, and the rest of the word lost; further along, ΠΑΝ, very clear, cut deeper than the rest. I wanted it on film. I balanced the lens cap on the little ledge under the letters for scale, as my father would have done, a black plastic disc with PENTAX across it in white. I took the inscription with the cap in the frame, and then I dropped the cap into my jacket pocket instead of fitting it back on the lens, which he also would have done.

I drank some water standing on the lip. Far down, a red speck I took for the German's windbreaker was crossing the floor of the plateau toward the road; the goats had gone over the shoulder of the hill, and their bells came up to me thinned by the distance. My sleeve was torn at the elbow where the thornbush had had it. A scratch I had not noticed getting ran across my knuckles, beaded red along its length. I capped the water and went in.

The floor sloped down from the lip, loose and stony, and the light came with me a little way, white and flat, and stopped. Beyond where it stopped, the cave opened out so suddenly and so far that I stood still on the slope with the bag dragging at me and let my eyes take their time. Fenn had called it a barn. He had undersold it. It was a single hall running back into the mountain farther than the daylight could follow, with a roof that went up into darkness in the middle and came down at the sides in folds and draperies of stone. Stalactites hung from it in bunches, brown and wet. Under them the stalagmites had risen to meet them, some no higher than my knee and some taller than a man, pale and lumpy and gleaming. One near the middle of the floor had grown into a great slumped mass with a dull shine on its near shoulder where hands had been laid on it for a span of years I could not begin to guess at.

The cold met me from the walls. It was cave cold, the cold of a cellar in August, damp and steady and smelling of wet stone and of earth and, very faintly, of goats. Fenn had known it from the sinkholes in Florida; I knew it from the crawlspace under my father's porch. I zipped my jacket to the chin. Somewhere back in the dark a drip was falling into water with a slow, uneven tap, and another drip answered it from another place, in a different key.

The walls near the mouth were written all over. There were names cut into the rock with knives and nails, hundreds of them, crowded and overlapping, and the light from the mouth came across them low and sideways and made every cut stand up in its own shadow. It was the raking light I used in the lab to find a seam. I went along the wall reading it as I would have read the underside of a pot, for the order things had happened in. A great many of the names were French, cut in capitals with dates in the 1840s and 1850s, and one man had added his regiment. There were Greek names in Greek capitals with little crosses over them, and one long one in a looping Greek script that had taken whoever cut it most of an afternoon. An E. WILKINS of Bristol had been here in 1861 and had cut his letters deep and level and serifed, a man with an idle afternoon and a good knife. The newest cuts were white and sharp-edged and the oldest had weathered back into the stone. In several places a newer name had gone straight across an older one; a 1952 ran through the middle of a French lieutenant. Higher up, out of reach of any knife, names had been written on the roof in candle soot, black smoky strokes laid on by men holding a flame over their heads. One of them was upside down, and its writer must have lain on his back on top of something to do it. Lowest of all, at the height of my hip, was a heart in blue felt-tip with two pairs of initials in it and 1996.

If it had come into my lab I would have needed a month just to decide what to keep.

The French had dug this floor at the beginning of the seventies. Their report was in the university library in two fat volumes with gray paper covers, and I had read them cross-legged in the stacks through most of April, slowly, in French, eating nothing, because I had been caught once already. The plates showed what had come out of this dirt through their sieves: little clay women by the thousand, rings, lamps, coins, a bronze cicada, and knucklebones. There had been more than twenty thousand knucklebones. They were the ankle bones of sheep and goats, carried up to this cave over centuries and left for Pan and the nymphs, the dice and game pieces of every child in Phocis. Some had been dyed or drilled through, and a few filled with lead to make them fall a certain way, and the French had laid them out in the plates in rows, like trays of dried beans. The floor still showed it. The dirt was loose and gray-brown and uneven, humped where the spoil had gone back in and hollow where it had settled, and a goat had slept in one of the hollows not long ago and left the print of itself.

I found it near the slumped stalagmite. A drip from the roof had been falling on one spot until it had washed out a basin in the floor as wide as a soup plate, and something pale lay at the edge of the basin with its top rinsed clean. I crouched, with the duffel sliding off my shoulder into the dirt, and put the flashlight on it.

A knucklebone. From the size it had come off a sheep, maybe a small goat: the ankle bone from the hind leg, about as long as the top joint of my thumb, with its two knobbed ends and its waist and its four long sides, each of the four a different shape, which was why it had been a die before there were dice. It lifted from the wet dirt with a small suck. The weight surprised me; it was heavy for its size and stained tea-brown through and through, not on the surface only. A modern bone off a dead goat would have been chalky and light and would have smelled of something, and this had no smell at all. Two of its long sides had been ground flat. Under the flashlight the flats were crossed with fine parallel scratches, the marks of a stone it had been rubbed on to make it sit right, and along the edges where the flats met the curves the bone had gone glossy, the polish fingers left on a thing after a very long time.

There was a procedure. I had written a page of it myself for the interns, and it was taped above the lab sink, gone wavy from the steam of the kettle. You left it where it lay. If you had already lifted it you returned it to its own hollow, facing the way it had faced, and you photographed it there with a scale, and you wrote down where it was, and then you walked down to Delphi and told the ephorate, who would thank you without warmth and send a man up in a month. I could not photograph it. The camera had no flash; in this light I would have got a frame of brown mud. Every Monday in the lab I unpacked things that had been lifted out of the ground by men who had not written down where, and I had a speech about it that I gave at parties until people found reasons to go and refill their drinks.

I pocketed it.

I stood up too fast and looked out at the mouth, where the daylight lay white across the slope and there was only the arch and the oak and a piece of sky. I would hand it in at the museum in Delphi on my way down. There was a guard at the museum door in a blue uniform who had torn my ticket in half on Wednesday with tremendous ceremony, and I would give it to him, and explain. I kept my hand around it in the pocket. It was cold from the dirt and then it was not.

Past the slumped stone the floor began to climb, and the daylight thinned and failed behind me until the flashlight was the only light there was, a yellow oval that slid over wet rock and showed me a yard of it at a time. I read Fenn's directions again by it, though I had had them by heart since March: beyond the last of the daylight, the roof dropping on the left-hand side, a slot there that a man could get his arm into. The floor here was flowstone, ridged and slick, with a skin of water over it that my boots kept finding, and I went up it with one hand on the wall. The wall was cold and furred with wet. On the left the roof came down. It sloped in above me until I had to stoop, and then it met a great slab that had split off the wall at some time and leaned there, and between the slab and the low roof there was a crack.

The crack ran up and down. At the height of my shoulder it was just wide enough to take my forearm, and above that it pinched shut. Lower down it widened, and along the floor it was a good foot and a half across. The flashlight went into it four or five feet and found a bend. Beyond the bend the beam ran on into black.

I reached into it. It was rock and air, cave cold, the same cold as everywhere else, and when I held my palm flat in the opening I could not feel the air moving at all.

So I waited. Waiting had been the plan all along; it was what the bag was for. The duffel went down against the slab, and I sat on it with my back to the rock, and before long I switched the flashlight off to save the batteries. The dark came down so completely that I raised my hand in front of my face and could not find it. Behind me the drips went on in the hall. Very far off at the mouth the bells reached me when the wind brought them, and then did not. I groped in the bag under me for another granola bar, past the sock with the tape in it, which had been down among my clothes since I laced the boots on at home. I ate the bar in the dark and the oats went down my front, and the Advil bottle chattered in its pocket whenever I moved. Every so often I lit the flashlight to read the watch, whose luminous dots had stopped glowing years ago, and slid my hand into the crack and held it there. At twenty past twelve the rock was still only rock.

The last dark I could remember as complete as this one was in our own cellar, the July I was seven, in a thunderstorm that knocked out the power for the whole block. My father was home that July, which was itself unusual. He had taken me down the cellar steps with a flashlight to look for candles, and at the bottom he had switched it off to show me what dark was like underground, the real thing, the dark his trenches went down into. I had screamed. He had switched it on again at once and been sorry for an hour; in the morning there was a Tastykake on my plate, a butterscotch Krimpet, unwrapped and laid out on a napkin.

At half past twelve I needed to pee. I went back down the flowstone with the light on my boots as far as the edge of the daylight and did it behind the big stalagmite, crouched with my jeans around my knees and one hand on the cold stone, and I begged Pan's pardon in Attic Greek inside my head, with the correct optative, which was more than I could have managed out loud. The light at the mouth was still white and still lying where it had lain at noon. I climbed back up to the bag.

I had not called Gloria. There was a telephone at the kiosk in Delphi where I had bought the map, and I had stood beside it on Wednesday with a phone card in my hand thinking about Ray having his fit. In the end I had bought a postcard of the Charioteer instead, and it was in the bag now, unwritten.

Fenn had sat here. He had spent most of a summer on this floor in the dark with his cigarettes and his notebook and his better flashlight, if he had ever got the better flashlight, going down in the evenings to his beans and his goat and coming back up in the morning. I sat in his place with my bag packed and my father's watch on my wrist and a stolen bone in my pocket, and I would have been hard put to say which of the three of us was the bigger fool. Cave air moved with the pressure outside; the book about limestone had said so. The cold he had felt was the weather on the far side of the mountain, coming through the rock.

It came at twenty to two.

I had my left hand lying on my knee in the dark, and the cold came across the back of it first, from the side where the crack was, a single thread of cold so distinct that I thought a drop of water had fallen on my skin. I thumbed the flashlight on. My breath showed in the beam and drifted toward the crack, and I got up off the bag and pushed my hand into the opening, my palm toward the dark.

This cold came from somewhere behind the rock. It was dry and it had an edge, where the cold off the walls was damp and slow, and it went in through the skin of my palm and down into the bones of my fingers until they ached to the wrist. Under my cuff the steel of the watch took the cold and held it. It was the cold that rolled out when you opened a chest freezer at the Acme and leaned in for the peas. The hairs stood up along my forearm. Behind me the drip went small. It did not stop; it went thin and far away, a drip in some other cave, and the bells at the mouth, which I had stopped hearing an hour before, I heard again only because they were going, and then they were gone. My ears filled, a pressure with no pop at the end of it. The beam of the flashlight shook on the rock.

Fenn had stood here with his arm in a hole for the length of a cigarette, and written it down, and come back on Tuesday. I was not going to stand in a cave with my arm in a crack until the cold quit, and then go down to Delphi and write it up in a notebook. I was going in properly or I was going home.

I drew my hand out of the crack. The flashlight went between my teeth. I got the strap of the bag over my head so that the duffel hung down across my chest, turned my left side to the opening, and put my left foot in.

I sank onto my knees and went in sideways with my left shoulder first and my back flat to the slab and the bag pushed down in front of me in both fists, and the rock came cold against the back of my head and my shoulder blades, and the cold closed over my face and ran down inside my collar and up the sleeves of my jacket to the elbows, and the beam of the flashlight jerked over wet stone a foot from my eyes and showed me stone and nothing else, and the last sound in the world, which was the canvas of the bag scraping along the rock, went smaller and smaller while I listened to it and was gone, and there was nothing at all, and I knelt in the nothing with my mouth open around the flashlight and my breath going out white into its beam.

Then my face went hot.

It rose out of my collar into my cheeks and the tops of my ears, there in the cold, a scalding blush of a kind I had thought I was too old for. It was the blush of a department Christmas party in Mount Airy in 1989. I had gone upstairs looking for the bathroom and opened a door on a bedroom where eight or nine strangers were sitting on the bed and the floor around a single candle, and they had all stopped talking at once and looked up at me. There was no one here. There was rock a foot from my eyes, and the cold, and the quiet. I had the word *sorry* in my mouth behind the flashlight.

I backed out.

The bag caught on the slab. I pulled it free. Then I was in the hall again, upright, and I got the flashlight out of my mouth and wiped my mouth on my sleeve.

The drip was going again behind me. The air on my face was cave air, wet and still and smelling of stone, and when I held my hand to the crack there was nothing coming out of it. I hauled the strap back on and hurried down the flowstone faster than I had gone up it, with the beam on my boots and the bag banging my hip and my free hand in my pocket, closed around the bone. The walls went by unread. The slope leveled, the slumped shape went by on my right as a pale bulk at the edge of the light, and the mouth came up ahead of me, a ragged arch of daylight.

It was the wrong color.

At noon the light had come in white and flat and stopped a few yards inside the lip. Now it reached far into the cave in a long slant, yellow going toward orange, and lit the floor almost to my feet, and every pebble on the slope had a shadow longer than itself.

The watch said ten to two.

I climbed to the lip of the mouth and stopped under the arch. The sun stood over the ridge to the west, low and swollen. It was low enough to look at through my fingers. Half the plateau below lay in shadow, and the half still lit had gone a paper-bag brown that I put down to the light. Beyond it the high summits stood lit to their tops, and there was gray rock all the way up the gullies where the snow had lain that morning. On the slope below me the thyme was over, every flower head dried to a brown husk on its stalk. The air coming in under the arch had a chill on it that had not been there at noon. Woodsmoke rose from somewhere below on the wind. Dung, a great deal of it, close and fresh. The bells were going somewhere under the lip, big and small, out of time.

The boulder stood at the last turn of the path with its face up toward me. There was no red on it. Nothing had been scrubbed off it either; there was no ghost of red down in the pits of the stone. Lichen covered the whole face, gray-green and orange, in rosettes as wide as my hand. Lichen did not grow like that in an afternoon.

I stepped out into October.
