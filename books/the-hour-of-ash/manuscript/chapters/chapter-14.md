I knew the year. It came to me on Theano's doorstep, before her mouth had quite closed on the word, and it came whole. Seventy-nine, late in the summer, in August. Behind it the god's verse lay down flat and pointed one way. The mountain with the vines on it was that mountain, and the sea to the west was the bay under it. The dead of that town left it by its gates, like any town's, and lay along the roads outside. A crow walked over my foot looking for barley that was gone. Theano told it to get off, and it stayed, and she let it.

She had nothing more for me. She had me carry her water instead.

Inside her door, beside the loom, her jar stood empty. The fountain was where the street turned below her house, a lion's head in a wall with moss under its lip. I held the jar under the spout until it was full, and kept on holding it. The water came over the rim and ran down my wrists into my sleeves and on down my ribs, very cold. A woman with a jug of her own waited at my back till her patience gave, and told me the whole street was thirsty, not only Syracuse. I got the jar up onto my head on a rag ring and took it uphill past the open doors. The jar was heavy and I got it up there and she said nothing of it at all. She was indoors already. I tipped it into her big jar; the cat was asleep on the hearth beside it. I left, and the crows were back on the ridge.

Valens was still on the corner, leaning on the same wall. He pushed himself upright when he saw me.

"What did she want?"

"Her water carried."

He took in the crows' house above us, and my wet front, and came along beside me on the house side, half a step behind. From the turn above Kallia's lane the sanctuary showed beneath, the god's roof with the sun going off it. I wanted to throw something at it, and I had nothing in my hands.

At Kallia's the Thessalians had come home with their share of the sacrifice wrapped in fig leaves. They were roasting it in the court and arguing over whose fault the ram had been. Kallia asked me nothing. She put an end of bread in my hand and the scrapings of the pot in a bowl, and I ate on the end of the woodpile while the Thessalians argued. It all went down. I finished the bowl and wiped it round with the bread.

* * *

Valens was asleep across the door of the storeroom.

Kallia had laid his blanket there herself, crosswise on the threshold, between my mat and the court. He had lain down on it in his cloak, facing out. He slept on his side, the bruised arm uppermost. His breathing was slow and even, and very quiet for so large a man. Beyond him the court was crowded with Thessalians. One of them talked in his sleep, a long grievance with a girl's name in the middle of it. Kallia's oil jars stood round my mat, and the barley sack, and the smell of both.

I stretched out on my mat with the lamp unlit and went over what I had.

I had the town. I had it from above, the way a plan in a book had shown it to me. It was a lopsided oval on a spur of old lava, walled. The forum lay long and narrow at the west end, and one straight street ran east from it nearly to the far gate. An Italian in the 1850s had cut the whole place into regions and blocks and doors and numbered every one, to find his way about in it. I had known the best houses by those numbers before I had any other name for them. The rest went by the names the diggers had given them: the House of the Faun, for a bronze faun in its pool; the House of the Tragic Poet, for a mosaic of actors. Nobody who lived in them had ever called them that.

What a single person in that town called a single street was beyond me.

There were the tombs. A famous row stood beyond the gate toward Herculaneum, with stone benches round to sit on; every postcard showed it. More lay along the road toward Nuceria, and odd ones elsewhere. I tried for most of an hour to call up a painted door on any of them, and got only the benches.

* * *

Behind my mat the wall was mud brick under a skin of lime plaster. Whoever plastered it had not troubled to finish it smooth. In the dark my fingertips found the ridges the trowel had left, and a stalk of straw standing out of the surface, and a patch beside the jamb where it had come loose and been pressed back with something softer.

In February of 1988 some of the casts had crossed the Atlantic. I took the train up to see them on a Saturday. I was twenty-three and still a student, with a head cold and a paper on Augustan coinage due on the Monday. They had been laid out in a long, dim gallery on platforms behind a rope. A man lay on his side with his knees up. His tunic had rucked at the hip, and the folds of it stood out in the plaster. One sandal had pressed its laces into his instep. Near me a woman in a fur hat told her husband she had thought they would be bigger. On the wall in a case was a loaf of bread, black, scored into eight on top, like a charcoal briquette somebody had tried to cut into wedges.

I stayed until a guard came through ringing a hand bell. On the train home I bought a pretzel from the man with the cart and ate half of it and carried the other half in its paper as far as Trenton.

Kallia's plaster was cold. I took my fingers from it.

* * *

I had the day, too, or as much of it as one man set down in two letters to Tacitus nearly thirty years afterward. I had put both into English once, for a Latin examination, with a dictionary at my elbow. The mountain opened at midday. Stones fell on the town all that afternoon and all night. Roofs went in under them. In the morning came the thing the letters had no good word for. Their writer had been seventeen that summer, across the bay, reading Livy, by his own account, and making notes. He was a boy of nine now, somewhere in the north of Italy, at his lessons.

It was the year seventy-one, by a count nobody here used, and the spring was only started. From here to that August was eight years and a little over.

The god had not said when. Theano would not say whether he had. The cave had opened for me on one afternoon in May, at one minute of it, and not since. A door that opened when it pleased could open on any day in eight years. This summer would do. For all anybody could tell me, it stood open tonight, on a road of tombs in the dark, with nothing going past it but a dog.

I lay with my face to the roof beams and kept hold of that.

* * *

The bay had a Greek town on it. Neapolis. Halloran had rung me from there in March, sitting on the bare floor of a flat he was leaving. He had offered me a bed in the house the survey rented, a room with a window in it. So that somebody would see the place.

I had said Greece, perhaps.

My father went to the past every summer of my childhood. He came home in September brown to the elbows, with his boots in a grocery bag and his head still full of somebody else's century. Every year there was next year. Next year there was never room. His last permit had been for Campania. I had signed it over to Halloran in April.

And the god of Greece had heard my question out, from a gladiator, word for word, and found me a place in Campania after all. He had not troubled with a permit. He had picked the year.

I laughed.

The sound was loud, the loudest I had made in that house in all my months there. It went straight out past Valens and over the court. A Thessalian stopped snoring. I put my wrist across my mouth and the laugh came round the sides of it anyway, in bits, high, with a squeak in the top of it that I had never heard myself make. My eyes stayed dry. The muscles under my ribs hurt. It ran on well after the funny part had gone out of it, and then it stopped.

* * *

Valens was up on one elbow. I had not heard him move.

The knife from the road was out of his belt and lying back along his forearm, point to the elbow. He was facing the court and the gate and the black beneath the gallery, not me. He spoke under the sound of the Thessalians' breathing.

"What?"

His head came round then, and he found where I lay.

It was there. It had been there all evening, the word, behind my teeth, with everything that came after it. He had carried it out of the temple himself, and risen under Kallia's gallery and said it all to me without a syllable out of place, and he had no more idea what it was than the cat on Theano's hearth.

"A woman on the terrace this morning," I said. "Her husband went in to ask the god where to dig a well. The god said south. She means to dig it where he was told not to, in the dark, and let him think the god thought better of it."

He weighed me a while longer. The knife went back into his belt.

"There's no moon till the new one," he said. "She'll dig it crooked."

He lay down again, facing out, and pulled the cloak up over his ear. His breathing evened. One of the Thessalians said the girl's name again and was quiet.

My hand crossed the dark between us. It hung above his shoulder with the warmth of him coming up into my palm from under the cloak. One word, and he would be awake again. He would ask where it was and how far, and whether the road was good, and how the ground lay round it, and none of it would be the question. I drew the hand in.

I said the word once with no sound in it, toward the jars. To everybody under that roof it was a town in Italy where they made fish sauce.

* * *

Some hours on, I needed the latrine.

There was no way out of the storeroom but over Valens. I gathered the chiton up in one hand and went across him in a single long step, and he slept on, or let me think he did.

The court was cold and smelled of the god's mutton. Overhead the stars were thicker than Philadelphia had ever let me see them. The cliffs blacked out half the sky. Somebody had a lamp lit by the drain in the far corner. It was the father of the family that had pushed the ram up the Way. He was a big man with a black beard. He sat on his heels with his youngest son's head on his thigh, and the boy was being sick into the drain, steadily; he had been at it since dark. Kallia was over them with a cup. She gave me the lamp to hold and went for salt.

The boy drank the salt water and was sick with it again. Kallia filled the cup again.

"It was the ram," the father told me, in a low voice, with great seriousness. "From Larisa it never wanted to come. Every mile, it told us."

"He ate the fat off the god's share. His own and his brothers'," Kallia said.

The boy had nothing left in him at last, and the father carried him back to the cloaks. Kallia emptied the cup into the drain and took the lamp off me.

"What did Theano want with you?"

Kallia had had every other piece of it from me, in its order. The lamp was under her chin. It made two hollows of her eyes.

"She wanted somebody to hear about her stool," I said.

"One of those priests could carry it down for her," Kallia said. "It isn't their knees."

She went into her room. She returned with her own blanket on her arm. It was brown, with a burn at a corner where a coal had caught it. She dropped it over Valens's feet and went back in and shut her door.

I stayed out in the court until the cold was in my soles. I climbed over Valens again and got under my blanket.

* * *

In the middle of the night I made the plan.

The silver off the dead stranger was in my rag. It was more money than I had held in that country. It would not take a woman across a sea and keep her a year at the other end. Euthydikos's bowl would. I had told his slave the bowl could keep another year. It could wait for the god to close his doors for the winter, and I could wait with it, and cut the old lead out of the bronze a hair at a time, and be paid.

Silver was the easy part. In Delphi I had needed a man to stand at a door for me before Apollo would hear a word I said. A ship's master at Kirrha would want the same, and a landlord in Italy, and any magistrate. A husband, a brother or an owner: somebody whose name went down first. The one man I had was asleep across my doorway with a price on him in Corinth. His own freedom was a line in a book in a record office there. All he wanted in the world was a high pasture and nobody coming up it. I left him out of the plan. He kept being in it.

There was a thing the plan did that I circled more than once before I would face it. Whoever took me to that town, the plan left him there. Felix had asked that afternoon whether he could come, if it was Italy. It was Italy. The plan had no boy in it, and I kept it so. Eight years was plenty of time, I told myself. Men went where the work was. A muleteer went wherever mules were wanted, and that was everywhere.

The sailing season closed with the autumn and opened with the spring. The boats going west at this new moon were for people who had both. Next spring, then. Saint Paul had made the crossing ten or eleven years before, in chains. His grain ship was an Alexandrian; it wintered on Malta and came in to Puteoli with the spring. If the grain ships would carry a prisoner, they might carry a woman with a man's name in front of hers. From Puteoli, round the bay by the coast road, to a town I had never stood in and could have drawn blindfold. Out through one of its gates to the tombs, and along them, looking for a door that was only paint. And through it, on whatever day it chose, years ahead of the mountain.

A year to get there. Seven, after that, to watch a door in. It was more time than I had been given for anything in my life. A town that size had lamps that broke and bronze that cracked. I could join a thing so the join never showed, and a mender who could do that would eat while she watched.

It was a good plan. I said it over once more, bowl to door, and it held.

* * *

"Tomorrow," I had said, about the cave.

There was no need of the cave now, if the god meant what Theano had made him mean. I lay and argued it both ways. The last time I had been up there, the crack had given me back my own arm, cold. That was all it had given, and a spring morning was unlikely to have changed its mind. If it had, there was no need of Italy at all. That door went home with no sea in the way, and no town.

I wanted it shut. That was what I found, once the arguing ran dry. Before I crossed a sea away from that cave I wanted to stand in it in daylight and see it shut with my own eyes. After that the one door left in the world would be the door the plan ended at, and I could stop listening for the other.

Either the cave was shut, or it was open. I meant to go up as soon as it was light and find out which.

* * *

Before the gray, the old moon rose above the mountain's eastern shoulder, a paring of it, too thin to give any light. I watched it over the court wall until the sky round it began to pale, and somewhere after that I slept.

I woke to an empty doorway. Kallia's blanket lay on the threshold, folded in four. The fire was lit under the pot. The ashes had been carried out and the hearth brushed, which was my work, and the two water jars stood full beside the wood, sweating. Kallia stood at the gate with the bar in her hands.

"He's gone down for the mules," she said. "You're to wait for him at the bottom of the climb. There's bread in the cloth."

She took the bar down and let me into the lane. Above the end of it the two cliffs were coming clear of the night, gray at their feet first and then, very slowly, a paler gray along the rim, with the path to the plateau scratched up the rock in its long turns and nobody on it yet.
