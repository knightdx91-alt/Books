# Chapter 4: The Goats Saw Me First

The goats saw me first.

They were scattered down the slope below the lip, a few dozen, with burrs in their coats. Every head came up together and the chewing stopped. A nanny with one horn broken off short had a sprig hanging from her mouth and forgot it there. The bells went quiet a few at a time. At the back a kid went on clanking, because it was scratching its ear with a hind hoof and would not be hurried.

* * *

The boy was closer. He had been sitting against the rock round the corner of the arch, out of the wind. He came up off the ground the way a cat leaves a sofa when a door slams, already facing me.

He was nine, perhaps. He wore a brown tunic of some coarse stuff belted with a cord, and his bare feet were gray with dust to the ankle. A sling hung around his neck with its pouch on his chest, and his hair had been cut short with a knife by a person in a hurry. There was a crust under his nose. He had a stick and kept it low.

His eyes came to my face and stayed.

He hooked a finger in the neck of his tunic, pulled it out, and spat down inside it. It was a small, dry, careful spit. Theophrastus's superstitious man did that whenever he passed a lunatic in the street.

"Nümfi?" he said, and then more, low, to himself.

It went up at the end. There was a sound in the middle I had no Greek letter for. The rest was thin and quick and slid straight past me, all but the case endings. I knew those from my grammars, and I had only ever heard them in my own voice.

"Khaire."

I gave it everything, the rasp of the k and both halves of the diphthong, the way the books printed it.

"Khére." He tried it like a coin he suspected was lead.

"Sorry." It was English, and no use to either of us. "I'm sorry, but I don't speak — I'm American. I came up from Delphi this morning. There was a man with poles. He had a stone for his daughter. I won't hurt you. I'm lost."

* * *

"I am a stranger," I said, in Greek this time. "I have come a long way, and I have lost my road. Tell me, boy, who is archon this year?"

It was the sentence for that. It sat at the back of the grammars under *Useful Phrases*, between buying fish and asking the way to the Piraeus.

"Ti?"

"The archon. Who rules here? Is there a king?"

"Pothen? Pothen ílthes? Ek tou antrou?"

He said a good deal more, fast and flat, the vowels pinched and everything I expected to be hard gone soft. I caught *pothen*. I caught *antrou*, which was nearly the word printed on my walker's map beside a little black symbol.

"Yes. I came out of the cave."

He took a step back. "Pothen?" The true answer was still Philadelphia.

"From beyond the Pillars of Herakles, very far to the west. Who is archon?"

"Archon." He frowned at it, and gave me the name. "Kleodamos." He went up on his toes to see past me into the arch. "Móni í?"

"Yes, alone. Which month is this?"

"Boathoos. Ti thélis?"

Kleodamos meant exactly as much to me as Nümfi had.

"Delphoi?" I aimed a finger down the mountain.

He watched my finger and not where it went.

So I crouched and wrote it for him in the dust at the foot of the rock, ΔΕΛΦΟΙ, in square capitals with good serifs. Above my hand the face of the rock was dressed flat and cut with letters. Their edges were sharp, and red paint lay in the bottoms of the strokes: ΝΥΜΦΑΙΣ, and further along ΠΑΝΙ, every letter there. On the ledge under them sat a clay lamp with a sooted nozzle and three knucklebones in a row.

The boy put his bare foot on my word and rubbed it out.

"Mí! Mí gráfe."

"It is only the name of a town."

"Nümfi."

I laid my palm on my chest. "Mara." I waited, and tried his. "Nümfi?"

His mouth fell open. He folded over his stick and laughed in high hiccups, like a much smaller child. I laughed with him without knowing what at.

"Mára," he said, and then to the goats, "Mára!"

* * *

The hunger arrived all at once.

It rose under my ribs and clenched. My mouth ran with water. My knees let go and I sat down on the duffel without deciding to.

"I'm hungry," I said. "God help me. *Peinō*."

The boy had a leather bag at his hip, black with handling. I could smell it from where I sat: sour milk, smoke, something baked. The smell filled my whole head.

"Ti dósis?" He laid a hand flat over the bag.

"Anything you want," I said.

There was a Wawa on Chestnut Street with a hoagie case lit up like an aquarium.

* * *

I got the zipper open. My hands found the box by feel, under the sweater, beside the sock. The pouch was green foil and crackled when I tore it. Two bars were inside, as there always were. I held one up to him and tapped his bag.

"I am a stranger and a beggar," I said, "and strangers and beggars are from Zeus. Have you bread? *Artos*. This, for bread. It is good. Honey. It is food."

He came near, at arm's length, and took it. He sniffed it. "Ti esti?" He bit.

The bar broke in his mouth with a crack and showered oats down his front. His face changed. He spat. Every crumb went onto the path, and he scrubbed his tongue on his forearm.

"*Pheu!* Ti esti touto? *Pheu*."

"I know," I told him, in English. "They're terrible. They're supposed to be good for you."

He gave me back the rest. "*Kopros*."

I ate mine, and then his, standing up, fast, head down, crumbs going down my fleece, and licked the honey off my thumb. I was still hungry. He watched me with his top lip lifted.

He untied the leather bag. Inside, in a gray rag, were a lump of white cheese and the heel of a dark loaf. He broke a piece off the cheese with his thumb and pushed it at me.

"*Phage*."

"Thank you. You are kind. May the gods give you good things for it."

"*Phage*."

It had goat hair on it. I ate it. It squeaked when I bit down, and it was so salty my eyes ran. The bread was half barley and full of grit. I chewed and chewed, and then it was gone.

He wanted the wrapper. He stood close enough to breathe on it, in my hand, where the silver inside was catching the low sun.

"Take it." I put it in his hand. "It is yours."

"*Kalon*." He tilted it and found his own face in it, and went entirely still. Afterward he folded it small and tucked it down the neck of his tunic.

* * *

He whistled the goats together and started down. I followed him because he was going down.

The path was only a goat track now, beaten pale through dead thyme. "Where's the road?" He looked where I was looking and saw grass.

Past the firs a pen stood where the pen had stood that morning. I knew it by the bend of the track and the slab behind it. Its wall ran in herringbone right round, every stone on edge, leaning on its neighbor. The corner kept the same long slant as the rest. A hurdle of woven brush closed the gap, tied to a post with a twist of green withy.

Below us the plain lay in shadow. It was brown from end to end, with two threads of smoke going up. Low huts stood down there, roofed with brush held down by stones. Grass grew where the road had crossed. Across the plain more firs climbed in one dark mass, and the ridge above them had only sky on it.

* * *

"Is there a Kaisar in Rome?" I said to his back, when the pen was behind us.

"Késar," he said over his shoulder. "En Rómi."

"Which Kaisar? His name. Tell me his—"

He was telling a goat to get on.

The hunger had come back. My stomach made a noise the goats turned round to look at.

Halfway down he sat on the track without warning and hauled his left foot into his lap.

A thorn was in his heel.

"Show me the foot," I said. "I will draw the thorn out. I am skilled at small work."

He talked to the thorn instead, and to the bush that had done it.

"Ai! Ai, ai. Káki, káki. Kissa! Kissa, deuro. Deuro, káki."

It was a long grumbling stream, and none of it was meant for me. He broke a fresh thorn off the same bush and dug with it, frowning, his tongue in the corner of his mouth. The sole of that foot was as hard as saddle leather. When the thorn came out he held it up, pinched in his fingers. "*Ide*." He was very pleased with it. He flicked it into the dusk. One of his ears stuck out further than the other.

* * *

At the foot of the slope he stopped, took me by the sleeve, and turned me toward the dark gap between the mountains.

"Delphous. Katō, katō. Delphous."

That one I heard whole. The ending was wrong and the *ph* had gone soft, but I heard it, and the back of my neck loosened.

"Delphoi. I thank you. Down there. Is it far?"

"Makran." And to Kissa, in the same breath, "Sitta! Sitta, íkade."

"Far. Of course it's far." That came out in English. I went back to Greek. "And you. What is your name?"

The goats shoved past our legs. He took his time. He lifted the stick and sent it back uphill, the whole way, to where the cave showed black in the gray cliff with one star over it. He brought it round slowly until its end rested on my fleece, over the breastbone.

"I do not understand. Say it slowly."

He said it a third time, slowly, chin up, loud and patient, for the deaf. I watched his mouth make it. The letters surfaced from the sound one at a time. A nu. The upsilon after it was rounded tight, nothing like mine. A mu, and the phi, the same strokes as the ones cut beside the arch with the red lying in them. At the end his voice came down on an eta that was not the eta of any grammar I owned.

"Nymphē."
