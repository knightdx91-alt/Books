# Chapter 5: The Sanctuary

It was most of a day down the mountain, and I spent the first hour of it walking toward a road that was not there. The boy had left me where the slope ran out and gone off over the plain behind his goats, toward the smoke. When the gap he had pointed at was as dark as the mountains on either side of it, I found a wall with the flashlight, and a dog somewhere out on the plain went frantic at the beam until I put it out. I felt my way to the wall's corner and sat down in what smelled like a sheepfold, and lay with my head on the duffel and my knees pulled up inside the sweater, and all night the dog barked at intervals without much conviction, like a car alarm on Baltimore Avenue at three in the morning. I woke with the canvas under my cheek soaked through with dew and my fingers too stiff to find the notch in the foil, and I opened a pouch from the granola box with my teeth.

The morning before, a taxi had brought me up from Arachova and across this plain on asphalt. At first light I set off back the way it had come, on foot, eastward, over stubble and dung and plowed-up stones. At the lip there was a track, two ruts with a hump of turf between them and a broken cartwheel in the ditch with its spokes weathered silver, and I was so glad of it that I touched the hub as I went by.

Where Arachova had been there was a village, lower and smaller, earth roofs stepping down the hill with smoke leaking through them, and the dogs came out to tell it I was there, and a man splitting wood stopped with his axe in the air until I was well by.

Below the village the road dropped in loops along the side of a long valley. The scrub gave way to terraces of olives walled in dry stone, stepping down toward a river that was only a noise at the bottom of a gorge. Whole families were out in them, the men beating the boughs with long canes and the olives coming down onto spread cloths in a hard brief rattle, and the knocking carried along both sides of the valley at once. The hunger from the mountain had come down with me. Some olives had rolled into the road, and I took one out of the dust, wiped it on my jeans, and bit it.

It was like biting chalk soaked in aspirin, and my whole mouth drew back from it; my cheeks puckered and clung to my gums, and I spat, and went on spitting for most of the next bend while a girl on the terrace above watched me over the rim of her basket and then shouted down, delighted, "You salt them first!" You cured them in brine for weeks; I knew to the decimal what olive oil did to the fabric of a terracotta lamp, and the rest I had learned from the backs of bottles.

The press was in a farmyard where the road made an elbow round a spur. I smelled it before the turn, crushed green fruit sharp as cut grass, over something sour and black that ran out under the yard wall in a channel alive with wasps. Inside the gate a donkey with a sack over its eyes walked round a stone basin turning the millstones, and against the far wall a great dark beam, dragged down by a windlass and by stones the size of stockpots hung off it on ropes, crushed a stack of rush baskets full of paste. A woman about my age was skimming the oil off a sunk jar with a wide shell, her skirts kilted up through her belt and her forearms shining to the elbow.

She saw me in the gate and kept skimming.

"Kirrha?" she said, and something with a no in it, and a great deal more, fast, over the scrape of the shell. She jerked her chin at a boy of ten asleep open-mouthed on a heap of empty baskets. "Two days at Kirrha with the jars," she told him. After that came a word for a husband who was no earthly use, and the boy slept through that too. "And what's that on your legs? Who sent you?"

"I am a foreigner and hungry. I can work."

The verb came out in the form for a man laboring for hire, the only one the book had given me. She stopped skimming.

"Where's your man?"

I understood that one.

"I have no man."

"Lucky." She waved the shell at the baskets of fresh olives stacked inside the gate. "Tip those under the stones for the donkey. You eat when they're done." She was skimming again before I had moved.

So I carried olives for my lunch, in rush baskets that each weighed as much as a sack of wet laundry, and I tipped them one after another into the basin in front of the donkey's slow feet, and the oil found the thornbush scratch on my knuckles and stung. When the stack was gone she tore a round of bread in half, dipped the torn side in the gutter where the new oil ran out, and gave it to me dripping. The bread was gray and crunched and the oil was so bitter it made me cough and I ate it all. "If you're going up to the god, take money," she said as I left. "They charge you for breathing up there." She crossed the yard and kicked the sleeping boy, not hard, on the sole of his foot, and when he sat up she put the shell in his hand.

Below the press the road met another climbing from the gorge, and after that I had company. A family overtook me, a man and his wife and a grown son, with a white kid on a rope that the son carried in his arms whenever it balked.

"Is this the road to Delphi?" I asked the father, because he was nearest.

He answered with his eyes on my trousers. "Where else would it go? Up there's the god and down there's the sea, and this is the road in between."

"Listen to her," said the son behind him. "She sounds like the man who reads out the laws."

"Mind your mouth," said his mother. "You don't know what she is." She put her husband between herself and me and kept him there until the road turned.

The town began with a running track below the road, young men racing its length in the dust, stripped and oiled. Past it the cliffs closed in overhead, two sheer gray faces with a cleft between them, and out of the cleft came the water. In May it had been a dry niche behind a wire fence with a sign in four languages about falling stones. Now bronze lion heads stood out from a wall of dressed stone and poured water into a long basin. Pilgrims stooped at it to wet their hair while women waited with jars on their hips and a man sat at a little table with a ladle and a dish of coins. I wanted to put my whole head under one of those lions. Every face at the basin turned my way; I kept walking, up the last of the hill and into the town.

* * *

The guide found me in the gateway.

Below the gate the town opened into a market square with a colonnade along one side, and every shop in it sold something to give the god: clay women, white wool, laurel, and at one stall strings of little clay discs, each pierced at the center and stamped with something worn too flat to read. They were too light for loom weights, and I could not think what else they might be for. At the gate stood a stone basin on a pillar, and every person who passed it dipped two fingers and touched them to the forehead, the woman with the kid, the son, the kid. My hands were black to the wrist with olive paste, and I kept them at my sides and went by.

He fell into step beside me talking, a lean, bandy-legged man in a brown cloak that had lost the pin of its brooch, so that he had fastened the brooch through the wool with a long thorn instead, neatly, years ago. He walked backward up the paving in front of me; his feet found every broken slab on their own.

"From Italy? From Sicily? No, look at the legs. From up past Thrace, somewhere they wear those. It makes no difference. You'll want the bull first, they all do, and then the treasuries, and then the temple, and you'll pay me at the top what you think it's worth, which will be two obols. And don't buy wool from that woman with the basket, she washes it in the horse trough."

He stopped in front of a bronze bull on a high base.

"This is the bull of the Corcyraeans. You will ask me why a bull. On Corcyra there was a bull that went down to the shore every day by itself and stood bellowing at the sea, and the herdsman followed it down and found the tunny running in the bay so thick a boy could have walked out on their backs. They could not get one fish into their nets until the god here told them to give the bull to Poseidon. So they gave it, and from a tenth of the catch they set up this bull to Apollo."

It was a speech he had given ten thousand times, worn smooth, in a town Greek far nearer my books than the boy's had been on the mountain, and I lost none of it. I would have paid him for that alone, if he had stood still once in his life to be paid.

"Who made the bull?" The folds of its dewlap had been chased so fine that the tracer's marks showed.

He stopped, put out. "Who made it? Nobody asks that. A man from Aegina, and they paid him." He carried on backward up the hill. "They want the fish, the people who come here, not the man from Aegina."

The bull was polished bright, with gilded horns and eyes of white stuff with a dark stone for the pupil. The marble men were worse. Their skin had been tinted a warm brown and their lips red, their eyes painted in with black lashes drawn on one by one, and a girl on a column with a basket on her head had painted earrings and pink nipples showing through a painted dress. I had looked at polychromy through a stereo microscope for most of my working life. For a museum open day I had once painted a plaster cast of a marble youth from the published pigment analysis, and been proud of myself for liking him.

Somewhere above, a goat was going up to its death, protesting, and a flute was playing it up.

"Don't listen to that one," said the guide, without turning his head. Across the Way a short man in a green cloak was talking to a family with his hand on the father's shoulder. "He tells it all wrong, and he's from Amphissa."

"I heard that," the man in green called over, without taking his hand off the father.

"You were meant to," said my guide.

Along the top of the next treasury ran a frieze of gods fighting giants against a hard bright blue. A few grains of that blue off a sherd from Kourion had once glittered under my lens like ground sugar, and here it was by the bucketful behind a giant having his head bitten off by a lion.

"And here stood the boxer from Thasos," the guide said, "whose statue killed a man, which is another story and costs extra. Everyone beats somebody at Delphi, and then they come and put it up where the others have to walk past it."

My feet hurt. The boots had been bought for mountains, and the paving had been worn glassy by so many soles that I climbed it with my toes clenched inside them, and somewhere in the boxer I had stopped listening to anything but them.

"And here," he said, "the runner from Kroton, three times at Olympia."

He had stopped walking backward and was pointing at a base, and he went on with the speech for the man who was not there.

"Look at the calves on him, and the veins standing up on his arms, and his mouth open, you would swear you could hear him breathing. They say he outran a hare on the beach at Kroton, and the sculptor made him run the stadium twenty times while he watched, to get the knees right." He cupped his palm in the empty air above the base, about where a hip would have been. "Bronze. With gilt on the wreath."

It was a block of limestone as high as my waist, with an inscription cut deep and filled with red saying that the people of Kroton had set up this man for the god. There was no man on it. Its top had two sockets cut into it in the shapes of two feet planted for a stride. Around the lip of the nearer socket the stone had sheared off in a curved flake, where something had been driven in under the edge and leaned on, and at the bottom lay a crumb of lead, chopped through.

I dropped the bag in the road and knelt.

The flake had come away clean, its face raw crystal, sparkling, and the rest of the block around it was old weathered limestone, pitted, with a rosette of lichen in one corner as broad as a coin. No dirt had got into the scar, and no lichen. Along the lip of the second socket ran chisel bites, short square cuts where the lead had been hacked out to free the bronze foot, still sharp enough to catch a fingertip. Below where the feet had been, faint green streaks ran down the front of the base, the stain that copper left in stone when rain had run over it for centuries, and the bronze that had fed them was gone. The streaks were the oldest thing on the block; the holes were the newest.

There was another base past this one with two holes in it, and another beyond that with a single heel still in its socket, the rest of the foot gone above the instep and the cut bronze faced green-brown and fine-ridged from the saw.

"The singer," the guide said above me.

He had come close, and his voice had dropped out of the speech into something flatter.

"He came for the games and sang for us, and we gave him the crown. And afterward his men came up the Way with crowbars and ropes, and five hundred of the god's bronzes went down to Kirrha in ox-carts and onto the ships. They took the Thessalians off their horses and left the horses, because the horses were bad bronze. The god's own."

"And the god allowed it?"

He turned his whole attention on me. "The god took his time. He's dead now, isn't he, the singer? Dead in a ditch outside Rome with his own knife in his neck, and the bronzes standing on somebody's doorstep over there, and here I am giving the speech for them still." He regarded the empty sockets the way a landlord looked over a room the tenant had skipped out of. "What is a stranger going to look at? The holes?"

Nero had come to Greece for the games in sixty-six and stayed into sixty-seven. He had died in the June of sixty-eight, in a freedman's villa and not a ditch, with the freedman's hand helping the knife. It was at least that late, then. The lead in the socket had dulled to a skin of gray, but when I scored it with the edge of a coin from my pocket it was soft and bright underneath, and that meant two or three years of weather at the very most.

I unzipped the end of the duffel for the Pentax and spent a frame on the base, close, with the sockets filling the viewfinder and the curved scar sharp in the side light. The shutter fired like a slammed drawer and the guide flinched. "What was that? What did it do?"

He held his hand out at the bend above.

"Two obols."

My jeans pocket had three coins in it from the taxi, and I found the biggest by feel and gave it to him. It was a hundred-drachma piece of the Hellenic Republic, brassy, with a young man's head on it in profile and his hair blowing back, and he read the capitals round its rim where it lay on his palm, with his lips moving. He read them perfectly well. They were his own letters.

"A hundred drachmas! Who strikes a hundred in brass? And this one with the hair, some king from past the Istros? Where do they spend these?"

"Friend," I said, in my best Attic, slowly, with every aspirate and every accent where the grammar had put it, "I come from very far away, and I have no other silver. Take it, and may the god be kind to you."

"Friend!" he called across the Way to the man in green, in my voice exactly, with every aspirate blown and the pitch rising and falling on the accents like a schoolboy reciting for a master with a cane. "Listen to this one. I come from very far away, and I have no other silver!" He put his hand on his heart. "May the god be kind to you!"

"Is it the Pythia's grandmother?" his rival shouted back. "Has she come down to tell the girls they're doing it wrong?"

"She pays in brass, too!"

The woman selling wool took it up and sang the end of it after them, and two pilgrims who could not have known what was funny laughed along because everyone else was.

I had the next sentence ready. It was a good sentence, with an optative in it. I put it away.

"Keep the coin," I said, in English.

He stopped laughing and gave me a look of real interest; then he tucked the coin inside the cloak and set off for the gate to meet the next stranger. I shouldered the duffel and kept climbing.

Above the bend the Way doubled back and climbed past more treasuries crowded shoulder to shoulder with their doors shut. The Athenians' was one of them. In May it had stood white and alone on its terrace, the one on all the postcards; I went by it now without knowing it. Above them ran a great wall of stones fitted together in curving shapes and written over in lines too small to read walking. At the top of the ramp beside it stood an altar of black stone edged with white, with smoke going up from it and a priest's assistant sluicing blood off its step from a bucket. The goat I had heard bleating its way uphill was tied to a ring at the foot of the step, alive, stripping the leaves off somebody's laurel branch; the blood on the step was some other animal's. Behind the altar stood the temple.

It had all its columns.

I had known it would; on Wednesday I had seen it as everybody saw it, six gray columns on a long platform with the cliffs behind them and the light going, two hundred tourists photographing them from the same place on the path, and I had stood among them and wished the light would stay. Here it was entire, and it had been painted. The columns were dressed in a fine creamy stucco, but above them everything had color. The grooved blocks along the frieze were blue so dark it was nearly black and the panels between them red, the cornice was picked out in bands of red and blue and gold, and gilded shields hung all along the front and flashed when the smoke moved. In the pediment the god stood among his family with their skin tinted and their hair gilded, and at the corners of the roof stood gilt figures against the sky.

The Mummers had got at it.

I crossed the terrace to where the path had been on Wednesday, the spot everybody stood on, and turned to face the temple, with the light coming low across it, and I waited for it to turn into my temple. It stayed red, and the shields went on flashing in the smoke. The god in the pediment looked out over all our heads with his gilded hair and his painted mouth, and the word that came to me for all of it was English: ugly. I checked the faces of the pilgrims going up the ramp straight away, in case one of them had heard.

The people who built it had seen it like this and meant it like this, and they had been right about their own temple, and I had been wrong about it for twenty years in a library. I had come the whole way back to see it alive, and I preferred it dead.

* * *

So I went up to give it its due.

The steps of the temple were a mason's steps and not a person's, each one as high as my knee, and I climbed them with a hand on each, like a child getting onto a bus. Between the columns it was suddenly cool, and the noise of the altar dropped behind me. Under my palm the stucco of the nearest column was fine and hard and very slightly gritty, marble dust in the lime, beautifully laid. Along the foot of the porch wall ran a low molding, painted with a key pattern in red and blue. I crouched to look at it, and the blue was the Egyptian blue again, coarse enough to feel under the ball of my thumb. A flake had lifted from it, and under the flake was another blue, older and paler and laid on finer. The new blue had been slopped over the edge of the old border by a painter in a hurry who had missed a whole hand's breadth behind the column, where he thought no one would crouch. The god's house had been touched up. Badly. I had my thumbnail at the edge of the flake, and I was smiling.

A hand took me by the collar of the fleece and hauled.

I came off my heels and round. The man holding me had a white tunic with a purple stripe and a staff in his other hand, and two more men had come up behind him.

"Off the god's step! Who let her up here unwashed?"

"Whose is she? Does anyone know her?"

"See the legs on her? She's run off from some house."

"In the god's house, with her hands on it!"

"Get her down before the priest comes out."

One of them said a word I had never seen written down, and the others laughed. Pilgrims had stopped on the steps to watch.

"Get your hands off me. I wasn't hurting it. Your molding's been retouched and it's been done badly, that's all, the blue's slopped right over the old border, I was only looking at the—"

"What's she saying? Is that Syrian?"

He dropped my collar and took hold of the duffel instead and pulled. I held on to the strap. "The rod!" he shouted behind him. "Bring the rod!"

The slave took the steps two at a time. He was barefoot and thickset, with a cropped head and a peeled rod in his fist as long as my arm.

He hit me across the shoulders.

I was down on one knee on the stone. The bag slid. I held the strap. He hit me again across the back, and the fleece took it, and then across the forearm I had put up, and that one I felt to the bone. The strap tore out of my hand. I was on my feet. "Down." He hit me on the hip. I went backward off the top step and down onto the next, and the next, sitting, my hands on the stone. The bag came down the steps after me and hit my legs. I took it by the handles. I stood. He came down after me and swung and caught my wrist and the watch on it.

I ran.

I ran down the platform past the altar with the bag clutched to me, and he came after me as far as the head of the ramp, hitting what he could reach, and the people on the ramp pressed back to the parapet to let us by. At the head of the ramp he stopped. I kept going. I took the ramp and the bend and the treasuries without looking back, and at the turn where the Way doubled on itself I looked.

He was standing at the top of the ramp with the rod bent open in his two fists where it had split, showing the split to the man in the purple stripe, who was watching the queue on the ramp over his head.

"Look at it," the slave was saying. "Split end to end, and they'll stop it out of my bread."

* * *

I sat on the edge of the colonnade in the market square below the gate with the bag between my feet. There were two hot stripes across my right forearm, and the skin had split on one of them in a short line beaded with blood. When I moved my shoulders the back of the fleece pulled at something underneath that had begun to stick.

The woman selling clay figures from a board at the next pillar had moved her board six feet farther off.

I began to shake. It started in my knees, in the full sun, and when I told it to stop it went on, and it was still going when a man two pillars along bought a string of the clay discs and beat the seller down by an obol.

The herald stepped out of a door at the end of the colonnade a little later, a stout man with a staff wound round with white wool, and a boy behind him carrying a wax tablet. He climbed onto a block outside it and opened his mouth, and the noise of the square sank under his voice.

"Hear, people of Delphi, and you strangers in the god's city! Letters have come up from Corinth with the ship, and the council has received them, and the council bids me cry it."

His was a big slow voice, every word set down by itself and every ending given its full weight; it carried.

"Titus Caesar, son of the Emperor, has taken Jerusalem of the Jews by storm. Their temple is burned and their city is thrown down to the ground, and the war in Judaea is ended by the favor of the gods."

A man near the money-changer's table called out *Kalōs!* The boy put the tablet into the herald's hand. He raised it at the full length of his arm and screwed his eyes at it, brought it closer, and pushed it away again.

"Therefore," he read, "the archon Kleodamos, and the council and the people of the Delphians, have resolved that on the fourth day from now oxen shall be sacrificed to Pythian Apollo, for the safety and the victory of the Emperor Titus Caesar Ouespasianos—"

"Autokratōr," said a thin man in the doorway behind him. "Autokratōr Kaisar Ouespasianos Sebastos. And for Titus Caesar his son."

The herald lowered the tablet and did not turn round.

"For the safety and the victory," he said again, at the same great volume, "of the Emperor Caesar Ouespasianos Sebastos, and of Titus Caesar his son, and of all their house. And the citizens to walk with garlands from the council house to the god's altar. And every householder to set a lamp in his door on the eve, and to keep the day as a feast. And the strangers in the city to keep clear of the altar while the god is served. And the meat to be given out in this square to the citizens."

"Citizens only?" a voice called from the back.

"And the rest of us?"

The herald climbed down.

"The rest of you can smell it cooking," the man by the money-changer's table answered for him, and the back of the square told him what he could do with his nose.

*Ouespasianos.* It took me the length of the whole repetition to get it. It was Greek trying to say a Latin name that began with a sound Greek did not have, and doing it the way the coins did, the coins I had cleaned with a cocktail stick, a thick-necked man with a nose like a doorknocker. *Vespasianus.* Vespasian.

Titus had taken Jerusalem in the late summer of seventy. The temple had burned in August and the upper city had fallen in September; I had read it in Josephus in a seminar in my second year, in a hot room on the third floor with a radiator nobody could turn off. News like that came to Greece in weeks, by sea, and the sea was still open, and the olives were coming down in the valley behind me. It was the autumn of the year seventy.

I did it on my fingers, because it was the kind of sum I made mistakes in. Nineteen ninety-eight, less seventy. One thousand nine hundred and twenty-eight years.

I had told Gloria the ninth of June.

"Is it for the women too, the meat?"

The woman with the clay figures had come back her six feet and was asking me.

"Was it the god's men did that? What did you do to them?"

"No idea," I told her, in English.

She stayed where she was. "Those are a man's boots," she said. "My husband makes these." She tilted the board toward me to show the clay women, each with her hands on her breasts, all pressed from one mold, with the same thumbprint on every hip. "Since the bronzes were carted off there's no buying. Everybody's poorer. You'd think the god would want more of these, with his own gone, but no." She took her board away again, still talking, round behind the pillar.

The herald handed the tablet back to the boy, said something short to the clerk in the doorway, who pretended not to hear, and stumped away along the colonnade with the boy trotting after him. The square came back up to its noise. My hands were lying in my lap; all through the proclamation I had been working the olive paste off them with my thumbs, one finger at a time, and they were clean, the creases still dark and the nails black, but the backs and the palms rubbed sore.

The sun went off the square an hour after that, and the stallholders put up their shutters. Two men at the fountain at the bottom of the square were still talking about the oxen. They were in no hurry, and I could follow most of it.

"Four oxen, he says. For the whole town? Who's going to get a mouthful off four? The council will have it all before it's cooked."

"Last time, for the Nemean thing, it was one ox and a sheep, and my brother's wife got a hoof."

"That's because your brother's wife stood at the back."

"She stood at the back because your mother was at the front with a basket."

"Where are the Jews, anyway?"

"Over past Syria somewhere."

"And is it true they burned their god's house?"

"So he says."

"Then that god will want paying for it."

"Not from us, he won't. It's four oxen."

The herald had said the fourth day, I was nearly sure, and nothing about four oxen. Either they had heard him wrong or I had.

I was waiting for the square to empty so that I could think where to go, and a man came across it in the dusk and planted himself in front of me with his thumbs in his belt. He was younger than I was, with a good cloak and a bad smile.

"On your own? Lost your people? It gets cold in this square after dark." The question was finished before his look was, in a way I understood without any Greek at all. "There's a house down past the stadium road that takes in girls on their own. Clean, and you'd eat. Come on, I'll carry the bag for you."

He put a hand on the strap.

"No. I'll carry it."

It was English, and the words were nothing to him; the voice was enough. He took his hand back. "As you like. You'll think better of it when the dogs come round." He said something sideways to a man I had not seen in the shadow of the colonnade. I slung the bag crosswise and walked out of the square the way I had come in, toward the cleft in the cliffs, and when I looked round at the corner he was still standing by the fountain, watching me go.

Under the cliffs the light was nearly gone. The pilgrims had gone in to their suppers and the table with the ladle and the coins had gone with them, and the water had the place to itself, pouring out of the lions' mouths in five white ropes that fell into the basin with a noise much bigger than they were. Air came down the gorge smelling of moss and cold water.

I knelt on the lip of the basin and put my face in the water.

The cold of it stopped my breath, and I surfaced gasping and went under again, and scrubbed at my face and neck with both hands. I pushed the sleeves of the fleece up and laid my forearms flat in the basin, where the cold bit into the welts, burned, and went numb. I drank out of my cupped hands until my stomach hurt, and leaned on the lip with my sleeves dripping, and made a plan. In the morning I would go back up. The boy's track climbed from the plain, and the pen was by the firs, and the firs were below the cave, and if the cold had leaked from that rock at one-forty on a day in May it might come again on some other day, if I sat there long enough with my hand in the crack. I had the rest of the granola, and now I had water.

A woman appeared on the road with a jar on her head and a lamp in her hand.

She did not see me, but walked the length of the basin to the far end, where the overflow ran out through a channel in the stone, and unloaded herself there in one movement, lamp on the lip and jar on the paving, the way I came in at my own door and got rid of my keys. She leaned over the basin and fished out of it with her bare arm a garland gone limp, white flowers on a ring of twisted wool, shook it once, and flung it out into the dark down the slope as far as it would go.

"Boeotians," she said to the dark.

She kicked off one sandal and put her foot under the overflow and stood on the other leg with her skirt held up. She let the water run over her foot, then changed feet and stood quite still while it ran. Her face in the lamplight was broad and brown and lined across the forehead, a woman of fifty with gray hair dragged back under a dark kerchief, and her eyes were shut. When she opened them she saw me, kneeling on the lip of the god's spring in trousers, with my wet hair in my face.

"Out of the basin," she said. "People drink that. Use the trough at the end."

I got off the lip.

She stepped back into her sandal and filled her jar at the nearest of the lions, holding its mouth up under the stream until the note of the water changed and it ran over, and then she came along the basin to me with the lamp.

"Who beat you?"

"A man with a stick, at the temple. Over a paint job."

She heard the English without any change in her face, and looked at my arm with the stripes on it.

"Hands."

I held them out. The flame was close enough that I could feel its little heat on my fingers.

"Turn them."

I turned them palm up, and she bent over them. They were clean from the basin, and soft, and pink. There was a scar on the side of my left forefinger from a scalpel, and the scratch the thornbush had given me across the knuckles of the right, and the smooth tight shine that years of acetone had left on the pads of my fingers. The only hard place on either of them was a pen callus on the side of my middle finger.

"Open your mouth."

I did not understand her at once. She showed me, drawing her own lips back from her teeth, and I opened my mouth, and she looked in with the flame under my chin.

The last person to look into my mouth with a light had been Dr. Pinsky on Walnut Street, in February, with a paper bib on a beaded chain round my neck and the oldies station on the radio, and he had told me I was brushing too hard and wearing the enamel away at the gumline, and I had gone home and bought a softer brush that same afternoon at the Rite Aid on Chestnut, the one with the angled head, because he was right, and that was worse than the enamel.

She was looking at the upper molars on the left, and I knew what she was looking at: I had a filling back there, an old one, silver gone dull, with a lip on it that frayed the floss.

"What's that? Lead?"

I closed my mouth.

She stood back from me, and I could see her deciding something and disliking it.

"Who saw you come down here?"

"No one." In English it came out all at once and kept coming. "There isn't a person on earth who knows where I am. I work in a museum in Philadelphia, in a basement, I glue lamps back together for a living, I came to Greece for three weeks on a credit card because of a dead man's notebook, and my cousin expects me home in June. I walked out of a cave yesterday. I was in it ten minutes. My father's Seiko says it was ten minutes." The voice went on me there. I stopped and waited for it, and it came back thinner. "I don't know what you're asking me. I don't even know your name."

She let me finish, and pointed at the jar.

"Can you carry water?"

I took the jar by its two handles and lifted it. It was as heavy as a child of four, and it rose off the paving full to the lip and slopped over my wrists and down my front. I got it as far as my chest and could get it no higher; I stood there with it against the fleece and my arms beginning to shake.

"On the head," she said. "Not like that. Like this." She put the flat of her hand on top of her own kerchief and walked three steps with her back straight and her neck long, carrying nothing, and walked back.

"Can you card wool?"

I knew the word. The women in the palace on Ithaca did it all day, and that was the whole of my acquaintance with it.

She saw that without my saying it. "Weave?"

I shook my head.

"Grind? Cook?"

"Eggs. I can do eggs."

She took that for a no, which it more or less was.

"I've a houseful of Thebans," she said, "and the girl ran off at the vintage."

I stood with the jar.

"Whose are you?"

It was the word the shouting men had used on the temple steps. She said it quietly, with her face lit from underneath.

"Mine."

She turned and started up the road with the light.

"Bring the jar," she said.

I carried it on my hip, which was wrong, up the dark road behind her, with the water slopping out at every step and running cold down my leg into my boot.

"Where are we going?"

"The step," she said, and the step was there, and I found it with my shin.

A short way above the spring the road turned off between houses into a lane so narrow that the jar scraped the wall, and halfway along it she stopped at a door and banged on it with her fist until a man opened it a crack with a lamp of his own.

"It's dark, woman."

"My sieve."

"Now?"

"It's been in your house a month. Your wife borrowed my good one to strain her must, and it was to come back next morning."

"She's asleep."

"Wake her."

He shut the door. My arms were on fire to the shoulder. I shifted the jar to the other hip and nearly lost it and caught it on the wall, and she stood at the door waiting, perfectly patient, with her lamp. After a while the door opened again and a hand came out with a sieve in it, a round wooden frame with a mesh of fine horsehair. She went round the mesh with one finger before she said anything.

"There's a hole in it."

"It had a hole when we had it."

"It had no hole. Mend it. Your wife and I fill our jars at the same spring."

The door shut.

She walked on to the end of the lane, where there was a gate in a wall, and behind it a courtyard with a pomegranate tree in the middle and a staircase up the outside wall to a gallery, and from somewhere above a man was singing, not well, a song with endless verses about a girl from Corinth. There was a smell of lentils and smoke. Doors opened off the court on every side; behind two of them lamps were lit and voices were talking, and behind a third a man was already snoring.

"Kallia!" A head leaned out over the gallery rail. "Is there any more of the lentils, or have those Thebans had it all?"

"Pot's on the hearth," she called up. "Scrape it yourself."

"Who's that you've got?"

"A pair of hands."

"Can she sing? We've had that song since sunset."

"She can carry."

Kallia, then.

She led me to a hearth under a lean-to roof, where a pot sat in the embers and a big jar stood by it.

"Water in there," she said. "Ashes out before the light, and the hearth swept. Water again from the spring before they wake, two jars. Then the floors, the court first, and the Thebans' pots from tonight, which they'll have left in the trough for the god to wash."

I tipped my jar into the big one, and it filled the big jar to the shoulder.

"Don't touch the oil. Sleep there."

She showed me a low doorway under the stairs with her lamp, a room with jars and sacks in it and a mat on the floor, and then she took the light away with her, because lamp oil was lamp oil, and crossed toward the singing.

* * *

The storeroom smelled of oil and dust and mice. The flashlight was somewhere in the bottom of the duffel, and it could stay there; Kallia was not going to come back and find a light under her stairs that burned nothing. I found the mat with my feet, and finding it I barked my hip on the shoulder of a storage jar, and something dry hanging from a beam brushed my hair. When I crushed a leaf of it between my fingers it was oregano.

Kallia came back once, in the dark.

"Blanket," she said from the doorway, and something heavy landed across my legs, and she was gone again; I had thanked her in neither language. It was goat hair, as coarse as a doormat, and it smelled of whoever had slept under it last.

I unlaced my boots by feel and stood them against the jar. My right sock was soaked from the water I had slopped down my leg. I worked it off and reached into the bag for a dry one, and my hand closed on the sock and the hard little oblong in its toe. I let it be and drew out the sweater instead, folded it for a pillow, and lay down. The welts on my arm had stiffened; when I lay on my back the fleece came unstuck from the place on my shoulder with a small tearing feeling, and I turned onto my side, facing the door. Upstairs the man was still singing; he had got the Corinthian girl as far as Sikyon. A voice from the gallery told him to shut up, and he sang the last line again, louder.

The watch was loud in the dark an inch from my ear, and I lay and listened to it and worked out how early I would have to have the ashes out and the water in to be on the cart track by full light, with the plain to cross after that, and the long climb to the firs, and the cave at the top of it by noon. Somewhere among the jars a mouse had found its way into the barley, and I lay in Kallia's storeroom on Kallia's mat under the goat-hair blanket with my wrist under my cheek, and listened to the mouse and the watch going about their two small businesses in the dark, one of them in the sack and one of them close beside my head, until I slept.
