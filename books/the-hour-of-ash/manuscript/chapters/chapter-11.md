On the other side of Praxias's wall a mule yard was already at work in the half dark: a shovel went along cobbles in long scrapes, buckets were being set down on stone, a boy was telling some animal at the top of his voice that it was the worst mule between Parnassus and the sea, and once, very low, a man spoke, and the boy answered him with three questions before he had finished. Praxias heard none of it. He sat on the bench outside his door with a quail in the crook of his arm and named me his price.

"Five drachmas," he said. "That's for family. A stranger I'd charge eight, and he'd pay it, because who else has he got?"

He was Kallia's cousin on her mother's side, a soft, heavy, early-rising man who kept his beard clipped to a fringe, and Kallia had sent me to him on the Kirrha road before the lamps were out, because after first light he would be at his olives and not to be found. The quail was a fighting bird; it sat with its feet folded under it and its eyes nearly closed, and he smoothed the feathers of its back with a fingertip, from the neck down, all the while he talked. Somebody in Phocis had bred that bird for courage, and it had the expression of a toll collector at the end of a shift.

"I have three," I said.

"Three is a goat. Or it's the god's cake, if the priest's in a good temper. It isn't a man on his feet in the god's own house with all Phocis behind him, saying a Syracusan girl's question out loud." The finger traveled the bird's back again. "They laugh at any man that carries for a woman. I'd be hearing it at the ring till the vintage."

"Kallia said you would stand for her house."

"For Kallia I'd do it for nothing. Kallia never asks for herself." He looked at me with real kindness, as if he were sorry about my arithmetic. "You're the one asking."

"Two now," I said, "and two more after the harvest."

"By then you'll have sailed for Syracuse, little cousin, and I'll be here with my palm open." He said it without any malice at all.

There were three drachmas in a knot at the corner of my himation, all that the priest had paid me, and everything the god wanted had to come out of them. If Praxias would not do it for three, nobody in Delphi would do it for three. Kallia had one cousin, and I had just spent him.

"There's the Corinthian," Praxias said, and tipped his head at the wall. "He'll carry anything for anybody. Wood, oil, a priest's mother-in-law. He'd put your question in a pannier and bring the answer back up with your change."

"Is he Corinthian?"

"His mules are. Five, little cousin. Think it over while you fill your jars."

It was forty paces along his wall to the gate of the yard, and the boy got louder at each of them.

The gate stood open on a square of cobbles and trodden dung, with a long stable down one side, rubble below and timber above under a roof of reeds, and a shed at the end of it with its door propped on a stone. A trough ran along the wall under a spout. The water spilled over the lip and ran off down a gutter to the road; a yellow dog lay on the dry side of the gutter, watching it go. By the house door at the back a young fig grew in the bottom half of a big jar, sawn off at the shoulder and packed with earth. It had not a leaf on it.

The boy had a shovel taller than he was. He was a thin one of twelve or so in a tunic made for somebody broader, belted up with a length of rope, and he had cut his own hair, with a knife, without a mirror. He had me in his eye before I was well through, and came straight at me with the shovel.

"Are you Kallia's? You're the one he took up to Pan's in the snow. Did you hit Pan? Which hand? Was there much blood?"

"Felix," said a man by the trough. "The black's water."

The boy went off with the shovel, still asking, as he went, which hand.

Valens was sitting on the end of the trough with a knife on his knee and a whetstone in his fist, putting an edge on the blade in small circles. Now and then he spat on the stone; his cloak was thrown back off his shoulders, and the hair at the back of his neck was wet from the spout.

I untied the knot first and put the obol on the stone of the trough beside him.

"For the pitch," I said.

He picked it off the stone and tucked it into his cheek, the way the men did in the market when their hands were full, and returned to the knife.

"I need somebody," I said, "to carry my question in to the god."

"No."

"You have not heard what I would pay."

"Three drachmas." The obol made a small lump in his cheek when he talked. "Praxias has a voice for selling. Half Delphi will have heard him."

"He was not shouting."

"He doesn't have to." The stone went round. "On the god's first day the whole sanctuary's full. Every man in Phocis with a question, all of them watching each other. A priest writes down the name of whoever goes in."

"They will be looking at the god. Not at the man with the goat."

"In a line a man looks at the back of the man in front of him. All morning. He's got nothing else to look at."

"Stand at the back, then."

"There's no back on the god's first day. There's a crowd, and a slave of the god's with a rod, sorting it."

"There is the board in the square, too."

"I heard it read out."

"Who read it out?"

"Whoever stood by it. There's always one." He started on the blade's other face.

From the stable came the boy's voice, telling the black she could drink or die of thirst for all he cared, and a bucket going over.

"Then sell me back the watch," I said.

"No."

"Have you let it stop?"

He loosened the drawstring of his belt pouch with a finger, looked into it, and left the watch where it was.

"The short one's past the nine," he said. "The long one's at the two."

The shadow of the spout was on the trough's lip, and by it the morning was nowhere near so far along. Since the snow he had kept it going, every day, and he had not put a finger to the hands.

"It is fast," I said.

"It hasn't stopped." He pulled the pouch shut.

Close to, his nose had once been broken, and had healed a shade crooked, and a white nick ran through one eyebrow, across the grain of the hair. He tried the knife's edge on the back of his forearm, and it took the hair off clean.

Behind him on the shed wall hung a breeching, the strap that went round under a mule's tail to keep the load from running forward on a descent. Its stitching had gone at the turn, the place the grit worked into most, and the break had been bound up with twine. Beside it on the same peg was a girth with two of its thongs cut half through by their own holes.

"That will break on the first steep road," I said.

He glanced back at it. "It's been breaking all winter."

"I can mend it."

"I never asked for it mended."

"I have not asked to be paid."

He sheathed the knife at his back and crossed to the stable.

The shed held a bench under a window hole, a packsaddle on a trestle, and a shelf with a pot of pitch on it and a coil of flax thread. An awl with a horn handle lay among the dust on the shelf. Its point had been blunted on something harder than leather, and I worked with it as it was. The breeching was oxhide, oak-tanned and gone stiff along the edges, and the old thread had not broken so much as been sawn through, a little at every downhill step, by the grit in the stitch holes. I picked the old thread out with the awl, pitched new thread on the pot's rim, and sewed the turn through twice, with a needle at each end of the thread, so that every hole was filled from both sides.

Felix fetched me bread at noon and stood first on one foot and then on the other while I ate it.

"What's he got in his pouch that ticks? Yours, is it? Each day before he's had his bread he does something to it, with his back to me, and if I ask he sends me for water. Does it live? He won't let me near it, and I held a fowler's hawk down at the harbor, bare-armed, and it never put a claw in me."

"It is mine," I said. "He is keeping it for me."

"He doesn't keep things for people," said Felix, and was gone.

At dusk I hung the breeching back on its peg.

Valens came in for it before I had the thread put away. He looked at the turn without touching the stitches and took the strap to the stable door, where a beam ran across under the reeds; over the beam it went, and with an end in each fist he hung his whole weight from it, his feet off the cobbles. The beam creaked, and the strap held.

"Who sewed it last?" I said.

"I did. At Kirrha, by a fire."

"It lasted you to the olives."

He brought the breeching back and hung it on its peg himself. "There's a heap more under that bench."

"I know. I have had my feet on it all day."

He went to see to the gate, and I made my way up to Kallia's by starlight.

In the morning the awl lay on the bench beside the girth, and somebody had put a point on it that would have gone through a sole.

* * *

For a week I mended his harness.

I had Kallia's water in before the sky was gray and was on the Kirrha road by full light, going down while the pilgrims came up. There were more of them every day, all of them early, on foot and on donkeys, a family from Boeotia with a grandmother in a basket chair slung between two poles. Most mornings Valens had a string out on the road already, to Kirrha for oil or salt fish and back up, and the yard was Felix's, which meant that it was loud. Felix did nothing at a walk. He climbed the rubble of the stable to the reeds to fetch down a sack and dropped back down it, though the door was beside him; he ate standing; he sang, and broke off singing to ask me something, and was gone before he had the answer.

"Where'd you learn leather? Off your father? Had he mules? There was a sailor told me in Syracuse they eat octopus raw. Raw!"

"Cooked," I said, to the octopus, but he was already on the roof.

On the third day the string came back before noon with nothing on it but empty jars, and Valens took the shed step to eat. He had bread and a string of dried figs, threaded on a rush through their stalks with their necks all lying one way. He broke one off and passed it to me without looking round.

It took some chewing, and it was very sweet.

"These have been too long in the sun," I said.

"They're Caunian. They're meant to be like that."

"A fig ought to give when you press it."

"A fig that gives has gone to the wasps." He bit one in half and examined the half in his fingers. "You pick when the skin splits by itself. A day in the shade first, on the tree's own leaves. After that the sun, whole, on a rush, turned every morning, and in at nightfall, ahead of the dew."

"In my country they split them and press them flat, in rounds, with a leaf on top."

"Where's that?"

"Syracuse."

"They don't, in Syracuse."

His voice did not change for it at all.

"Split, the dust gets in," he said. "And the flies. They go hard at the cut and stay wet in the middle, and come the spring you've a jar of mold."

"Pressed, they keep a year."

"Pressed, they're a cake. If I want a cake I'll buy a cake."

"The wasps go for the whole ones as well."

"Not if they're turned."

"What has turning them to do with wasps?"

"A wasp likes a thing that keeps still."

"Chian are bigger," said Felix, going past with a bucket.

"Chian are for men who've never had a Caunian."

"I never had a Caunian till this winter," Felix said, and was round the end of the stable before anybody could answer him.

"That one's Caunian." Valens pointed with the half fig at the tree in its jar. Its twigs had come green and hard at the tips, like the ends of kitchen matches. "From a cutting. In a pot, it goes where I go."

"When does it fruit?"

"When the summer's done, if it's any good. You dry them your way, and I'll dry mine, and we'll open the two jars at midwinter and see."

I put the awl through the girth and drew the thread after it. By then, if there was any justice anywhere, I would be standing in a kitchen in Philadelphia with a tap in it.

"Somebody else would have to open mine," I said.

"Then I'm right." Valens ate the rest of it.

It rained a bit the next morning and then it stopped. While it rained the string stayed in, and Valens sat on an upturned bucket in the shed doorway with the mules' bells in his lap, a dozen of them, stuffing the throat of each one with a twist of wool.

"Why stop them?" I said.

"Road's full of strangers on their way to the god. I'd sooner hear them than have them hear me."

He did three bells without saying anything else. Then he said, "The thing in my pouch. Has everybody got one, in your country?"

"Most people."

"Women?"

"Women too. Children, once they are old enough to be late."

"Late for what?"

"For whatever they are going to. Lessons. Work. Each other."

He pushed the wool into a bell with his thumb and shook it, and it gave a dull clank with no ring left in it. "Why does anybody need the hour that close? The day's got a middle. Everybody knows where it is."

"So that two people can be in the same place at the same time, and neither of them stands there waiting."

He considered that, with the next bell in his fingers.

"They could meet at dawn."

"Not everybody is awake at dawn."

"Who isn't?"

"The rich," I said. "And people who work all night, and sleep in the day."

He watched the rain on the cobbles. "Who keeps the door, while they sleep?"

"In my country—" I began, and he was on his feet, and the bells were in my lap, the lot of them. He crossed to the stable through the last of the rain.

The girth went back on its peg, and after it a headstall with a cheekpiece sewn on by somebody who had never seen one sewn, and a packsaddle pad whose straw had gone to powder; I emptied it on the dung heap and Felix stuffed it again, elbow-deep, from the barley straw in the loft, complaining throughout.

On the morning of the fifth day Valens was taking the string to Kirrha without Felix, and Felix took it hard. He stationed himself in the shed door to take it hard in front of me.

"There's boxing at the gymnasium before the god sits. A Thessalian, and a man from Elateia who killed somebody at Thebes, they say, with his fists, with one blow. Everybody's going. Kallia's lodgers are going. Can I go? I'll have the stalls done before noon."

Valens had come into the shed for a pad off the trestle.

"No."

"It costs you nothing. I'll walk there and back, I'll be here before the string is. I'd go a round with the Thessalian myself, if they'd let me in the ring. Have you ever seen a man killed with one blow?"

"No."

"You've never seen anything," Felix said, with great bitterness, and took himself off to fork out the stalls, and forked them so hard that the dog left the yard.

Valens left for the string with the pad under his arm. I put my next stitch in the headstall and stayed on it until I heard the gate.

The black came back lame on the fifth evening, at the tail of the string, nodding at every other step, her head going up hard each time the near forefoot came down. Valens tied the others and had Felix trot her the length of the yard and back. He watched, and said, "Again," and the boy ran her up and back once more.

"Near fore," he said.

He laid his hand flat on her shoulder and ran it down the leg, slowly, to the fetlock, kept it there a while, and then took it on to the hoof. The hoof was hot; even from the shed I knew it, because she snatched the foot away from him and stamped, and threw her head up so that the whites of her eyes showed.

"Do you want the twitch?" Felix was already halfway to the shed. "I'll get the twitch. I'll hold her ear; nobody holds an ear better than me. Or the rope on her hind leg?"

"No."

Felix stopped short.

Valens left the foot alone. He stood at the black's shoulder, a hand on her neck under the mane, and talked to her. It was Latin, and low, and so little of it reached me that it might have been the same few words over and over. It went on until Felix gave it up and perched on the trough. I had the headstall over my knees, and I put no stitch in it. At last the black shifted her weight off the sore foot of her own accord, to rest it, and as it left the cobbles his hand was under it, and he had it against his thigh, sole up, and she let him keep it.

"Lamp," he said.

Felix had it lit and there before the word was well out. Valens cleaned the sole with the point of his knife and searched it under the light, and found a black spot near the toe, a lentil's worth. He pared round it in slivers, and stopped whenever she shook, and went on when she stood. Once she brought her nose round to the back of his neck and blew there, and he let her. When the knife went in far enough a thread of gray matter welled from the sole, with a smell I could taste, and the mule held still on her three good legs and let it come.

"That's the bad in her, coming out?" Felix had the lamp up so close to the hoof that Valens put his wrist against it and moved it back. "All of it? She'll go to Kirrha tomorrow, won't she?"

"Not tomorrow."

The board in the square had advised whoever went to fetch him to bring friends.

"Bran," he said. "Hot."

Felix brought it in a bucket with a sack. Valens packed the hot bran into the sack, stood the foot in it, and bound the sack round the fetlock with a strip of rag, and the black waited with her foot in it and her head low, and he stood at her head.

"Why not the twitch?" I said, when Felix had gone for more bran.

"She'd remember it."

"She is a mule."

"That's why."

It was full night at Kallia's gate. She stood over the pot with the ladle, and gave me only the side of her face as I came past her.

"That yard's on your himation," she said. "Whose water washes it?"

"Mine. I will carry it."

"Is he paying you?"

"No."

The ladle made its circuit of the pot. "There's women in this lane twenty years married who'd not do that much for a man for nothing."

By morning a fresh sack was on the foot, and Felix was waiting in the gateway.

"He was up all night with her. Three times he was up to change it, and I never heard a thing. Why won't he let me? I know poultices. Did he say anything to you about me doing it?"

* * *

Praxias came down to the yard on the sixth morning with his quail in his arm. He stood in the gateway until he had priced the place.

"Four," he said. "For family, four, and the standing thrown in."

Felix reached him before Praxias had the last word out.

"That's the one? From behind the stadium, that blinded the Amphissan's bird in one eye? The left, I heard. Did it fight again after, the Amphissan's?"

"It did not," said Praxias, who liked him at once.

"I have less than three now, Praxias," I said. "And the god's share comes out of that first."

"The god sits every month now till the winter. Go to him next month, when you've four." He shifted the bird to his other arm. "Three and a half, then. I'm robbing my own children."

I told him no. Going out, he stopped at the trough.

"Mule-man," he said. "That Syracusan of yours is a hard woman."

Valens gave him no answer, and he went off uphill, telling the quail about it.

Valens had been at the trough with the black's foot in a fresh bran sack, and he had not looked round once while Praxias was in the gate.

"What did he want?" he said.

"To carry my question for four drachmas. And then for three and a half."

"He looked at the string. He looked at the house twice."

"You had your back to him the whole time he was in the gate."

"He turned uphill. Not to his own door."

"Perhaps his olives are uphill."

"Felix."

Before his name had finished, the boy was through the gate. Valens put the bran sack in the bucket, and walked to the back of the yard, where the wall gave onto the olive terraces and the goats had pulled a gap in it, and he looked at the gap for some time. Then he set two stones back into it that would not have kept out a hen.

Felix was back before the shadow of the spout had left the trough, out of breath and full of it.

"He went to the wine-seller under the plane tree, and he had a cup, and he told the wine-seller the Syracusan was tighter than a priest's purse, and then he went to the ring back of the stadium, and he put two obols on the Amphissan's new bird against the baker's, and the baker's won, and he called the baker a thief, and the baker said—"

"Did he go up to the square?"

"The square? There's no quails in the square." Felix took a breath. "The baker feeds his bird on wine, everybody says so. Wine, to make it brave. Would it work on a mule?"

"He wanted my three drachmas," I said. "Only that."

"He's got a wall on my yard." Valens returned to the black's foot.

That evening he had two mules of split wood to take up to the priest's back gate, and we climbed to the town together with the mules in front. The pilgrims were all in by then. The ones who had found no roof had made fires on the terraces under the olives, and their suppers were cooking, and a child was crying in Thessalian, which sounded much like crying in anything else.

"What happens on the god's birthday?" I said.

"Everybody comes."

"I meant after they have come."

"The priests go down to the spring to wash, in the dark. After the goat, the doors. Delphians in first, and the rest as the priests like."

"How do the priests like it?"

"Paid."

"Where would you stand, in all that?"

"Thessalians at the front. They push. Boeotians at the back, drunk by noon. Men off the islands selling figs nobody ought to buy. And men with nothing to sell, watching the line."

"Watching it for what?"

"Whatever's in it."

"You have been in yourself, surely."

"No."

"Not even a question, sent in by somebody else?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"I don't want anything he's got."

"Suppose he has nothing for me."

"You've lost a goat." The mules had stopped to look at a fire, and he whistled them on. "What are you asking him?"

"That is between me and the god."

He let it be. At the edge of the town he took the priest's lane with his wood, without anything more, and I went on to Kallia's alone.

On the seventh morning the yard gate was shut and barred, and I had to call over it. Felix let me in and dropped the bar back into its sockets, and did not like it.

"Who's coming? The Amphissan, about his bird? We've never had it barred in the day, not since I came. Is it you?"

"Leave it barred," Valens said from the stable.

"The Amphissan, though?"

Valens took the string out an hour late that morning, by the back way over the goats' gap and the olive terraces, and was back by the same road at noon.

"You came in by the olives," I said, when he brought the string into the yard.

"The road's dusty."

The olive path was twice the distance and too narrow for one loaded mule to pass another, and all week he had used the road.

Felix spent the afternoon climbing the gate to look over it, and calling down what he saw. "Nobody. A cart of oil. Some Thessalians, lost. Nobody. I can see clear to the bend from up here, and past it if I stand."

I had been writing my question on a sherd. In the evenings under Kallia's stair I scratched it into a piece of broken jar with a nail, and at noon in the shed I had it out of my belt and turned the words round, and scraped half of them off with the edge of the awl, and scratched them in again. Whatever I put to the god had to be said under the god's roof by somebody else, in front of a priest who wrote things down, and I wanted it in Greek that nobody could mistake. Kallia's obol for the papyrus was knotted in with the remains of the priest's money; I had never gone up to the priest's house to buy the sheet, and her son's line was still unwritten, and every evening I scratched at my own instead. *The door that let me in is shut. Where is another?* It was the best I had, and on the seventh day I left it on the bench, face up, while I took the mended girth across the yard.

Valens was in the shed on my return, hanging the black's halter on its peg. The sherd was as I had left it.

"Take out the door," he said.

In the yard and on the mountain both, I had kept doors out of all I told him.

"What?"

"The priests will ask what door. Whose door, and where it is, and what you were doing going in by it, and they'll write that down too." His eyes stayed off the sherd. "Ask for a road. A road they'll answer."

"You read it."

"It was on the bench."

"You said you heard the board read out."

"I did hear it."

He went to the trough. On the back of the sherd I scratched it again, slowly, with the nail: *Where is my road home?* It was shorter, and it said less, and a priest would have nothing to ask me about it except where home was.

Felix put his head in at the window hole.

"What's that, writing? A curse? Who's it on? Can you write me? Big?"

On the shelf lay another piece of the same jar, no wider than my hand. I scratched his name on it in capitals, the way they cut them on the stones in the sanctuary: ΦΗΛΙΞ.

He was in at the door to look, and carried it to the window two-handed, and stuck his head straight back out of the window hole.

"Valens! She's written me! Come and see what I look like!"

Valens came as far as the shed door with the bran bucket.

"Read me," Felix said, and pushed the sherd at him. "Go on, then. Say me out loud."

"How would I know what it says?" said Valens. He let Felix have it back and returned to the trough.

I kept quiet, and Felix brought his questions to me instead.

"He can't. Nobody in this yard can but you. Which bit's me? Where does it start? That one, with its arms on its hips, that's the F. Will it wash off, if it rains?" He tipped it toward the door. "Nobody at Kirrha's got his name written down. The Cilician called me Boy from Side to Kirrha. Has he told you about the Cilician?"

"No."

"He bought me off him on the quay at Kirrha. He paid more for me than he paid for the black, and she's four years old and sound, or she was. The Cilician laughed and said nobody had ever paid him so much for a boy with a cough. You'd think he hit the Cilician. He never. He just looked at him, and the Cilician gave him a drachma back. You've seen how he looks at people." He returned the sherd to me, and took it back again to look. "The name's his. Felix. It's lucky, in Latin. He talks to the mules in Latin, did you know that?"

"I knew that."

"He said I could go wherever I liked, and I said where, and he said wherever I liked, and I came here." He put the sherd face up on the bench with great care, his name to the light. "Can I keep it? The dog's never seen a name."

"Did he take you to the priests," I said, "and have it cut on the wall?"

"What wall?" he said, and went out with his sherd to show the dog.

* * *

On the last evening of the week I finished the breastband, in the shed, by the lamp. It was a band of ox leather that went across a mule's chest to keep the load from sliding back on a climb, lined with felt where it bore, and the felt had matted to the hardness of a hat brim and rubbed a bare place on the gray's chest that I had found and the gray had not forgiven me for finding. I had cut the old lining away and sewn in a new one of fleece, wool side in. Valens had come in to hang up the black's halter for the night, and he stayed, and found the knot in the end of a lead rope to work at, which I had watched him tie that morning.

"Is that the last of it?" he said.

"The last."

"That's the heap gone, then."

"Felix says you freed him on the quay at Kirrha," I said.

"Yes."

"Before whom?"

"The Cilician. Somebody selling eels."

"There is no paper."

"No."

"Who is to say he is free, then?"

"He can say it."

"And if somebody takes him? If somebody says he is a runaway?"

The knot came loose in his fingers. He was a while silent, and I let the question stand.

"Paper gets taken off you," he said.

"Who took yours?"

He hung the rope up with the knot still in it.

I had the last two stitches to put in, where the breastband joined the saddle strap. I put one in and drew it up, and the second wanted pulling hard against something.

"Hold this end," I said. "Hard."

His fist closed on the end of the strap. I drew the last stitch through and pulled both threads tight against his grip and knotted them, and cut them off with his knife, which was lying on the bench, and lifted my head from the knot, and his face was a hand's width from mine.

The lamp was on its nail behind me; it lit him and left me in the dark. The nick in his eyebrow showed, and the healed break in his nose, and the rest of the shed was gone. Neither of us moved, and then we both did, his head coming down and my face lifting to meet it.

Short of it by the width of a finger, he stopped, and stepped back. The shed was small, and the one step put his shoulders in among the girths hanging on the wall behind him.

I sat down. There was the packsaddle on its trestle behind me, and my knees put me onto it, with the cut ends of the thread still pinched in my hand. Everything in me that had been held up went down at once, the way a body went down at the end of a long walk once the shoes were off, and I was hungry. I wanted bread, and whatever Kallia had in the pot, and I wanted it now.

He took the lamp down from its nail and looked at the wick, and pinched off the char with his fingers, and hung it back up. Neither of us said anything. He was out in the yard next, and out there, where he must have taken himself to be unwatched, he stretched, both arms straight up over his head, until something in his back went with a crack I heard from the bench.

I went out after him.

The yard was blue with dusk, and the string was in its row, eating. Felix was in the end stall with the black. He had sat down in the straw to do something, by the look of him, and the something had not got done: he was asleep, with his back to the stall's wall and his head fallen sideways against her belly, mouth ajar, and the sack he had been sent to change still in his fist. The black stood over him. All week she had swung her rump at him, and kicked his bucket over every evening, and stood on his foot whenever he gave her the chance. Now she was standing square on all four feet, the bad one as well, which she had not done since it went lame, because to take her weight off it she would have had to shift, and he was asleep against her.

Valens stood and looked at the two of them. He went in beside the boy and eased the sack out of his fist without hurry, and the black let him, and he came out again without waking either of them.

"He's been up since before it was light," he said, very low.

"Will you wake him?"

"No."

"Who's your Delphian?" he said.

"Nobody. I cannot meet Praxias's price."

"Praxias lost two obols on the Amphissan's bird. He'll stand at the door for a drachma, if his name's said out loud."

"Who told you what Praxias lost?"

"Felix."

"At the door. Inside, though?"

"I'll take it in. The day he first sits this year. His birthday, the priests say."

"The priest will write your name down. You told me so yourself."

He let that go by him. I waited for him to tell me why, and he went on looking at the boy.

"Why?" I said.

"What name does the priest write?"

"The clerk wrote Maira."

"And you?"

"Mara."

That got no answer. He had it, and he kept it.

"Which one does the priest get?"

"The clerk's."

"The goat," he said.

"The goat-seller in the square wants more for his she-goat than I have."

"He'd want more than you have if you had twice as much. At Kirrha there's a man brings them over from Aigion on the boats, at half his price, and none of his have ever been near a fire."

"How does a goat get from Kirrha up to Delphi?"

"On its feet. Felix goes in front with a fist of barley, and it follows the barley."

He went to the gate to look at the road, as he did every night before he put the bar across. The dog got up off its side of the gutter and went with him.

"We take the string to Kirrha for oil the day after tomorrow, at daybreak," he said. "Bring your drachmas, and wear something you can walk back up in behind a goat."
