The crowd roared for the wrong man.

They knew him. He had fought on that sand twice already, according to the woman on my left, and twice he had walked off unhurt, and they called him Rusticus because he had a field somewhere up the Nola road and returned to it afterward with his purse to weed. When the herald cried the pair, the benches answered Rusticus from every side. Felix's name went up after it into the noon, and nobody caught it.

"His wife's my husband's niece," the woman said, to the bench at large. "She sells eggs in the forum. She won't come and watch him. She says she can't watch a man she has to cook for." She kept a hand on a bag in her lap. "Who's the bird?"

I could have told her. I watched the sand instead.

He had walked out with a bird on his head. I had known for a week that he would be a thraex, and I had seen thraeces on that sand before, but nothing had warned me that the helmet would take his face away entirely: a broad bronze brim, a visor of punched holes where his eyes ought to be, and on the crown a griffin's head with its beak open, aimed wherever he aimed. Below the helmet he was all wrappings. Quilted cloth wound both legs to the groin. A greave was strapped over each from mid-thigh to instep, so high and stiff that he crossed the sand lifting his feet like a wading bird. They were new, and the sun found them; I knew by then what they had cost him. His right arm was quilted from fist almost to the shoulder, and in the fist was the short curved sword, bent at its middle the way a vinedresser's knife is bent. The other arm carried a shield the size of a bread board.

I knew him by his feet. Inside all that bronze and padding he could not keep still. He rose onto his toes and sank again while the herald finished, as he had after every errand I ever gave him, impatient to be let go.

Valens sat beside me with Lucius on his knee.

We were up in the last ring of benches, beneath the masts that held the awnings, among the wives and grandmothers and the men in dark tunics who sat at the top because the good seats were spoken for. Under our feet the seats dropped away band after band to the painted parapet, and down past it all spread the oval of sand, raked in long lines that the morning's pairs had scuffed out at the middle.

Lucius sat facing outward, his spine to his father's belly and both legs stuck straight over his father's knee. His father had told him to watch Felix, and he was watching Felix. When the thraex first stepped out he had leaned hard on Valens's arm and said, "Lix," loud enough to turn the two women on the bench in front.

Rusticus walked to his mark behind a shield as tall as his chin, curved about him like a stave of a barrel, painted red; his helmet wore a fish for a crest. He planted the shield's foot and settled in behind it. Of him there showed a helmet, one shin and a sword.

The man with the long rod stepped back from between them. The horns stopped.

Felix went left. He went far left, wide round the red shield in a curve, high-stepping in his greaves, his small shield carried out in front at belt height and his sword arm drawn back, and Rusticus turned behind his board to keep it facing him and otherwise did nothing whatever. A man below us laughed. Felix went right instead. The board turned. Somewhere in the lower benches a man put two fingers in his mouth and whistled the two notes you whistle at a dog.

Valens's hand lay spread on the front of Lucius's tunic.

My eyes kept leaving the fight. They went to the strap that held Felix's left greave below the knee, where a new hole had been punched for his thin calf, too near its neighbor, so that the leather between them had begun to tear. Each time that foot took his weight I expected it to part. It held, and I waited again.

Felix stopped circling. He went in. The sword drew back and came over, and the curved blade struck the top of the red shield, high, where the rim was bound in bronze; the knock came to us after the sight of it, small and flat. The red board shoved forward. Felix hopped clear. Out of the bowl came a long sliding groan, and the griffin tipped upward at the benches, hunting for whoever had made it.

Lucius drummed his heels once on his father's shin.

Felix set his feet and went in again.

* * *

Nine days before, we had returned from the Puteoli loads at the end of a long day on the bay road, with the four mules in a string behind and Lucius sleeping in a pannier with his cheek on a sack of meal. Aelia had slept since Herculaneum with her mouth on my collarbone. The street had gone blue with evening and its shutters were all up. Grata was round her marble and out to us as the dun came to a stop.

"He's gone to the gladiators. He went the very next day after you, before the bakers had their fires lit, and I'd have sent after you if I'd had a soul to send, but my husband's no use on a road. He swore himself in the forum, the whole oath, and the fuller's girl was the first he told. He left your stable mucked out, in the dark. His blanket's gone. That bowl of his is still on your shelf. He's to fight at the duumvir's games, nine days from now, the painter's had it up on the bath-house wall since yesterday, twenty pairs and the awnings, and the fuller's girl says he's to be a Thracian, whatever that means to her." She lifted Aelia out of the sling and settled her on her own shoulder. "Give her here. You're dropping where you stand, the pair of you, and the boy's got meal up his nose."

Valens slipped the dun's lead.

"Which troupe?"

"The troupe that sleeps at the theaters, with the bald one." She was rocking Aelia, awake now and staring at her earrings. "You'll never get him out of there, and I'll not charge you for knowing it. A cousin of mine had a boy swore himself at Nuceria the year of the riot, and his mother went to the lanista with her wedding money in a cloth, and the lanista counted every coin and gave the cloth back to her full."

"Did he leave any word?" Valens said.

"For you? He said to tell you the dun's off her feed, and it's the new hay and not her teeth, so you're not to go poking about in her mouth. That was all the word he left. I said, have you nothing for him that bought you off a quay, and he said no, that was it, the hay, and off he went."

Valens walked the mules through to the stalls. I heard him go from stall to stall in the dark, the dun, the black, the two others, stopping at each, and the knock of a bucket lifted to judge how much was left in it.

Felix's corner of the shop was only floor. Where his blanket used to lie, along the shelving the cobbler left us, a patch of floor showed a shade cleaner than its surroundings. The hare bowl sat high up, upside down, so that no dust could settle where the hare ran. The sherd with his name on it was gone. He kept it in a crack behind the shelving, which he believed was his secret, and the crack was empty.

Lucius had woken when his pannier was lifted off. He wandered in with sleep on him and meal in his hair, made straight for the corner, and stood on the clean patch with his head bent. He squatted and patted it. He rose, faced me, and waited.

At midwinter there had been a split eyebrow, and Felix said the dun had thrown her head.

Valens sat out on the shop step with a pannier over his knees, unpicking the stitches of a strap that was sound, and stitching it again. I laid Lucius on his pallet, still blinking, and fed Aelia when Grata brought her across, and he was out there yet when I drew our curtain.

He came to bed late. Past the curtain the shop was so quiet that the lamp was the loudest thing in it, a small tick of oil.

"You knew about the eyebrow," I said.

"He went there afternoons. After the loads."

"From when?"

"Since the must was in the vats." He lay flat. "Nicanor had him sweeping the yard first. Holding the posts for the others. On the posts himself by the new year."

"And you let him."

"He's free. I said it at Kirrha, with the Cilician listening. You don't say that and then come round after and haul him home by the ear."

"He took his name with him," I said. "The sherd."

Valens rolled toward the curtain and the dark shop. If he had an answer, he kept it. I slept some and not much and then it was morning.

First I walked to the baths, to see the painter's work. He had taken the whole of a white panel beside the bath door. The duumvir's name headed it in red, each letter taller than Lucius, with his father's name after it. Below, smaller, came the day, twenty pairs, a hunt, and awnings. No name was on it except the duumvir's. A boy on the curb with a pear asked me what it said, and I told him.

"Is there a bear?"

"A hunt. Nobody wrote what kind."

"There was a bear before," he said. He went at his pear again with the face of a boy cheated once already.

The fullery on our corner did its treading at the back, in a yard with no roof, where four stone basins stood in a row at knee height, and the smell of what filled them sat on the yard like heat on a summer road. A girl was treading in the end basin with her skirt knotted up between her legs. She gripped a rail in her two fists and rose and trod, rose and trod, on a heap of cloth in gray water above her ankles. From the knees down her legs had been bleached so pale by it that they might have been borrowed off someone else.

She saw me at the doorway.

"You're his. The mender."

"Mara."

"Prima." In the dry basin next to hers sat one of her aunt's twins, wedged in with a fleece, chewing the end of a leather strap. "He told me first. He'll have said I told everybody. I never told everybody. I told my aunt, and my aunt told the fountain."

"Saying what?"

"That he'd sworn. He stood just where you're standing, with his blanket rolled on his back, and said me the lot. The burning and the chains and the rods and dying by the iron, whatever they like to do to him. Same words he gave the magistrate's man. Twice, so I'd have it right. I called him an idiot. He said I'd said I liked the look of them." She stopped treading and stood in the gray water. "I said they had arms. At the fountain, in the autumn. They go by every morning to the baths in a string, with their arms out like that, and the whole line says so. I never said it to him." She trod again, harder. "Will they burn him? Is that in it?"

"Nobody has told me."

"He made it sound like nothing." She bent and hauled up an end of the cloth under her feet, dripping, and showed me a long brown stain down its length. "This is the duumvir's wife's. It went in with wine on it and it'll come out with wine on it and they'll say it was me." She let it drop. "He left me this. He said to keep it."

She fished inside her neckline for a flat piece of red pot with scratched capitals across it, its edges rubbed soft. ΦΗΛΙΞ. I had scratched those capitals myself, in Valens's shed below Delphi, the winter we met.

"It says Felix," I told her.

"I know it does." She tucked it away against her skin. "He told me it, letter by letter."

The twin had dropped the strap over the basin's edge and set up a wail about it. Prima leaned across, hooked the strap out of the dirt by one end, wiped it on her hip and returned it.

"I'm to marry the baker's nephew, from the Nola Gate bakery," she said. "When his uncle gives him the second oven. My aunt's settled it. Two years, she says, or three." She considered her white legs in the water. "Felix said bakers are fat by thirty. He said no crowd ever cried a baker's name, only people wanting bread."

"What was your answer?"

"That fat's better than burned." She stepped over the basin's rim, and her wet feet printed the flagstones gray. "Give him that, if you see him. I've said it twice now."

Her aunt was calling for her out front, wanting the duumvir's wife's cloth wrung before the steward arrived, and asking was that the mender out there, and if so would the mender look at a lamp. Prima dragged the stained cloth clear of the basin and twisted it, and gray drips coursed to her elbows.

"Free, she wants it," Prima said. "She wants everything free. Slip out by the back, or you'll be here till noon."

* * *

The lanista kept his accounts in a room behind a wine shop on the Stabiae road, a short walk from our door. Valens had not wanted me along. I went because the money was mine: the jar from under our bed, my mending money and his carting money together, which we had been putting by since Lucius was born against a day nobody had named. I carried it in a cloth knotted at the corners, the way that boy's mother at Nuceria must have carried hers.

Albucius was eating eggs. He was a broad, soft, clean-shaven man in a good tunic, not as old as I had pictured, at a table heaped with tablets, with the shells picked off into a small white pile at his elbow, and he stayed in his chair.

"Lupus," Albucius said. "Nicanor said you'd come. I'd have bet on today." He glanced at the cloth in my hands, and off it. "Sit. There's the bench. You'll not mind if I eat. I've had a bear off its food since the Ides and a slave who swears it's sulking, and I was up with it till the cocks."

We both stayed standing. "I'm here about Felix," Valens said.

"Naturally." Albucius drew the tablet he wanted from the pile without hunting, opened it on the table, and turned it round. "There. Felix, free, of no town he could name, eighteen, he tells me. Sworn to me for three years. The duumvir's freedman heard it, and my trainer stood witness. Four hundred paid down. Nicanor wrote the name in for him."

From my side of the table the letters stood on their heads, but they could be read. The hand was a clerk's, round and patient, and under the four hundred, smaller, ran a second line: less forty, greaves. The wax looked honest to me. Valens gave the writing no glance at all.

"How much for him?"

"Today? Nothing." Albucius ate half an egg. "The duumvir's bought twenty pairs from me. The list's made. Your boy's in one with a man of mine called Rusticus, who's no killer, before you ask; he's a farmer. If I pull your boy off the list I owe the duumvir a pair, and I've no spare thraex in the yard who can stay on his feet, and the duumvir doesn't forget. Come back once the games are done, and bring your cloth. A tiro who's been out once and lived, the price comes down on him, and I'll sell him back cheaper than what's in it."

"And if he doesn't come back," said Valens.

"Then the duumvir pays me for him, and I'm sorry, and your cloth stays yours." A slave looked in at the door, and Albucius lifted a hand to him without turning. "Is it eating?"

"It's had a sniff."

"A sniff." He closed the tablet. "A sniff, he tells me. Two hundred sesterces of bear. Lupus, I'll tell you this because Nicanor says you're no fool. The lad will fight better if you keep away from my gate. They watch for who comes asking after them."

Valens turned and left between the wine jars. I followed with the cloth.

He walked fast toward the crossing, weaving among the carts. At our own corner he stopped.

"He wants more. He's looked the boy over and he knows what he's got. He'll take my money once the games are over and find a reason to keep him. Or he'll put him in with someone who can kill him and sell the duumvir the blood. You heard him. The duumvir pays him for the dead."

"He said Rusticus is a farmer."

"He'd say so."

He went to the stable for the string. The cloth went into its jar again, beneath our bed, and I mended a lamp all afternoon, and by supper could not name one thing about it.

Nicanor brought Felix home at dusk. I heard Felix first, talking to somebody from the fountain onward, and then he was in our doorway with Nicanor's bald head showing past his shoulder. He wore a new tunic, coarse and undyed; his hair had been cropped close to his skull, and along his jaw grew the beard Nicanor had made the price of trying him, a dark down he had been nursing, I guessed, all winter.

"Nicanor's making me tell you myself, so this is me telling you. I've sworn. Three years to Albucius, in front of the magistrate's man in the forum, with Nicanor listening, and I never tripped on a word of it. There. It's told."

"Grata told us," Valens said. "Before we'd unloaded."

"Course she did."

Lucius had toddled round my plank at the sound of him, at his own pace, hands held out from his sides. Felix put a hand down to him blind, and Lucius gripped two of its fingers and leaned on his leg.

Nicanor stayed outside. He went across to Grata's, and I heard her start on him: he was the trainer, she could tell a trainer, they had the legs for it, and he was to keep his rod off her marble, and he'd have the chickpeas because they were hot and he'd pay for them because she wasn't the duumvir and she didn't feed fighters for the love of it.

"How much did he give you?" Valens asked.

"Four hundred down, and the same each time I fight, and more if I win. Less the greaves. Bronze ones, past the knee. The smith measured me for them. Forty, they were. He had it out of the four hundred."

"He's charging you for his own kit."

"They're mine. They're made to my legs. Nobody else can wear them."

"Who's your pair?"

"Rusticus. A murmillo. He's fought twice and owns a field out toward Nola, and he only fights to buy the next field along." His knees had started to go, a small bounce, and Lucius bounced with them, holding on. "I asked for the fish. I told Nicanor I'd go out the way you did, with the big board and the fish on my head, and he laughed, with the whole yard watching. He said I'd be a thraex or I'd go back to the mules."

"He's right."

"Because my arms won't hold the board up." Felix's chin lifted. "Say so, then. Say I can't."

"You're quick. A thraex wants quick."

"A murmillo," Valens said, "looks out over his board. He can't see your feet. He watches your shield. Wherever it goes, he thinks you're going."

"I had all that my first day."

"Keep it up, then."

"I keep it where I like."

The lamp on its hook between them had started to smoke. I unhooked it and trimmed it, because my hands wanted something, and the shop went dim and came bright again.

"The girl at the fullery," I said. "Prima. She says to tell you fat is better than burned. She says she has told you twice now."

Felix went red to the collar.

"She'll come to the games," he said. "The fuller's shutting the vats for it, they're all going, the aunt and the twins and everybody. She'll be there, up with the women, and she'll see. She's got the sherd. Did she show you? She'd show nobody that unless she meant to keep it."

"Albucius robbed you," Valens said.

"Robbed me? He gave me what he promised. He never even asked me for a paper. He said, your name, and I said, and Nicanor put it in the wax, Felix, and I watched every letter go in." He stopped. "You've not been to the sand since the bear, Nicanor says. Two summers and more. Not even to look. Why's that?"

"Albucius wants me kept off his gate. He says they watch who comes asking."

"Albucius." Felix snorted. "You could come to the yard. They'd let you in. You could stand at the posts some morning and see me go, and say what I do wrong, you'd find something, you always find something. Everybody else's people come."

"I'll not come to the yard."

"You needn't," said Felix. "I'd sooner you stayed away. If you sat near the tribunal they'd be chanting Lupus before I was halfway to my mark, and the whole place would know who'd taught me, and I'd spend the bout hunting the benches for you while he took my ear off." He freed his fingers from Lucius and took the hare bowl off the shelf, and tipped it, and puffed into it. He crouched and gave it to Lucius. "That's yours. Carry it in two hands, like that. Keep your fingers off where the hare is."

Lucius clutched it to his front.

Felix left. He paused at Grata's marble to speak to Nicanor, and then he was walking away to the fountain. Nicanor rose and came to stand in our doorway.

"Twelve years I've worked for Albucius," Nicanor said. "Forty he's had off him for a pair of greaves that cost the smith thirty-five. There's your robbery." He scratched his cheek. "And he's told you he'll sell him back after, cheaper than today. Go round every school this side of Capua asking for that. You'll wear your boots through."

"Who'll kill him?" Valens said.

"Nobody, if he does what he's bid. He won't. He'll go at Rusticus the way a pup goes at a cart wheel, and Rusticus will sit behind that board and let him. Third pass, your lad's flat, and Rusticus's foot on his shield." He squinted at the lamp I had trimmed. "After that it's the duumvir. The duumvir's mean. Dead men cost him."

"The day of the bear, your middle one went home on his feet."

"Twice this year." Nicanor touched his rod to the lintel, a small tap, and went.

Valens took my stool behind the plank, which he never did. Lucius came to his father's knee and tipped the bowl up for him to see the hare. Valens stooped to it. Painted in brown slip, the hare ran round its bowl after its own tail with its ears laid flat, as it had run since Felix bought it, and it never gained an inch.

* * *

For the rest of the week Lucius went nowhere without the bowl. Mornings, he bore it out to the stable and stood with it while his father roped up the string; afternoons, he sat with it on Grata's step. Once he tripped on our threshold and went down flat, the bowl kept off the stone by his two arms stretched up as far as they reached, until somebody came.

Valens carted each day and came in each dusk, and ate, and slept. He went nowhere near the barracks or the lanista. I was certain he would do something; nothing had ever seemed surer. At night I lay awake and planned it for him. He would put on his good tunic and go to the duumvir and offer himself for the games in Felix's place, Lupus for a nobody, a man with nine wins at Corinth for a tiro with no fights at all. I had heard the benches roar that name, and I could guess what a duumvir would pay to hear it roared again. Or on the day itself he would vault the parapet to the sand, and that would be the whole plan. Over supper I studied his face for it. After dark I listened for him. Once, when he left our bed for the stable in the night, I had my clothes on before he got back.

"The dun's cracked a hoof," he said. "Lie down."

On the fifth morning Prima stood ahead of me in the fountain line with two jars and a twin, and she turned and spoke to me over the twin's head.

"He sent an answer. The porter's boy brought it to the vats, for a copper." She swung a jar up onto her hip. "I'm to know he's not burned yet. He says Nicanor has him on the posts from first light to lamplight, and he holds his shield up all day, and he's quicker on his feet than Rusticus, and a baker can't lift anything but bread." The jar slopped, and she let it. "I sent the porter's boy back to tell him bread's heavy when there's enough of it."

We went home from the fountain side by side, her jars and my one, the twin riding her hip and reaching for my hair.

"Your husband was Lupus," she said. "They talk of it at the vats. Nine times, and the emperor's own sword."

"Yes."

"Did he ever lose?"

"He is alive," I said.

She chewed on that the length of a house front, hitching her load higher, and turned in at the fullery.

On the last night, Valens came back across from Grata's with a cup of her wine, still full, and left it on our shelf.

"I'll go," he said. "Up top. With the women."

"With the women?"

"If I sit down below with the men, a man along the bench knows me soon enough, and the herald has it ahead of the thraeces, and it's Lupus's boy on the sand." He sat on the bed and started on his boot laces. "They'll want Lupus's boy to kill a man. At the top I'm a carter with a child."

"The women will not know you."

"The women will know me. They don't call heralds. Bring Lucius."

"He is not two yet. They will be shouting."

"He'll sit on me."

I would have argued it.

"If it goes badly," I said.

He worked the second boot off and stood it with its fellow, toes to the wall, and lay back.

"I can't go down there. I'd be one more pair of feet on that sand. I'll watch."

I pinched out the lamp and lay beside him. Through our party wall Grata's husband sang one line of something, and the same line, and then quit.

"Your first time at Corinth."

He let so long go by that I thought him asleep.

"I couldn't feel my feet," he said. "Walking out to my mark. I looked down to see were they still on me. Nicanor yelled at me to stamp."

"Did you?"

"The crowd laughed." He shifted his head on the bolster. "After, I had them back."

Soon he slept. Lucius lay open-mouthed on his pallet, his bowl at his ear, where he had insisted it stay, and whenever a cart rolled by outside the bowl answered it with a faint dry hum on the floor, louder to me than all the rest of the room. Toward cockcrow I rose and nudged it onto the blanket's edge, a fingernail's breadth. The humming stopped. He slept on.

In the morning Grata crossed over while our shutters were still up and took Aelia off me in silence, for once, and made up for it on the doorstep: we were to leave the girl with her, the noise in that place would turn a baby simple, and she'd seen grown men leave a show with their ears singing and their purses gone, and she'd keep the girl on her counter in the basket where every customer on the street could admire her, and we'd tell her who won when we got home, though she knew already, because the trainer had eaten a bowl of her chickpeas and said the lad would be down inside three passes, and if that was what the trainers thought of him she'd hate to hear the lanistas.

We took the lane past the exercise ground and the vines, Lucius riding on Valens's shoulders and gripping him by both ears. The seats were half full when we climbed to the top, and the awnings were being hauled up their masts with a long creaking of ropes, sailcloth unrolling across the sky in tawny strips, and from below came the sweet sharp smell of the saffron water they sprinkled on the sand between bouts. I thought of my cousin Gloria's yellow rice, out of a box on Sundays, and Ray salting it before he had tasted it.

First the fighters came round. They walked the oval behind the horns in a long loose file, bareheaded, cloaked, slaves carrying their helmets after them on poles so the crowd had the crests to cheer, and the benches called out to the ones they knew. Felix walked among the last of them, the thinnest, his griffin bobbing on its pole at his back. He paid no mind to the shouting. He went with his chin lifted toward the good benches low beside the tribunal, searching along them as he walked, one bench after the next, right round to the gate where the file had begun.

Valens's hand did not leave the boy.

We sat through the hunt and two pairs. Valens gave the ropes on the masts more of his attention than the sand. Lucius gnawed a crust and showered crumbs on the woman in front, who brushed her shoulders off and never looked back. During the hunt he wet himself, and Valens shifted him to the dry knee.

Then the herald cried Rusticus, and the benches answered.

* * *

Felix's second rush hit the red board square, and the board took it. He went in once more, lower, and Rusticus turned the blow aside with the board's edge without any trouble at all, and Felix backed off out of reach with his shield sagging to his belt.

The third pass came and went, and Felix was upright yet.

They circled. They circled for so long that my neighbor opened the bag in her lap, which held salted beans, and passed them along the bench. A boy with a water skin worked along the ring behind us, a copper a cup, and the man at the end bought two and poured the second down his neck. Felix kept his distance now. He had his shield up, high on the arm, level with his chin, the griffin's beak jutting over it. Wherever the small shield went, the fish crest turned after it. Felix's feet meanwhile went elsewhere, quick and short in the tall greaves. Then he did nothing with any of it.

A man below us jumped up and bawled at them to fight. The cry spread.

His greave strap was holding. I had forgotten to watch it.

Halfway round the oval, Rusticus stopped. He grounded his shield, stood his sword in the sand point down, and bent sideways to his left shin, where the lacing of his single greave had come undone, and began to tie it. Every bench in the place could see him, bent double, helmet tipped to his work, the board propped on his hip. Felix stood three strides off with his sword raised.

The bowl went mad for him to do it. The noise came from all sides together, men up on their benches, the woman beside me up with her beans spilling, a roar that I felt in the stone under me. Felix stood fast. Sword up, shield up, the griffin pointed at the back of Rusticus's neck, he waited. Rusticus finished his knot. He straightened, lifted his board, and drew his sword.

The jeering started then. Two slaves with whips appeared at the door below the editor's box and took their places where the sand met the wall. The man with the long rod stepped in between the pair and cracked it on the backs of Felix's thighs, then on Rusticus's, and pushed at them with it.

All around us the top ring was on its feet. They were shouting for the whips, kill him, whip him, burn him, those three words, beginning again each time they ended. My neighbor shouted it with the rest, at her husband's niece's husband. Ten places along, Prima was standing on her bench with her palm pressed to her tunic where the sherd lay and her mouth closed. I was standing too. Along the whole long curve of the top ring, every soul but one was up.

Valens sat. Lucius sat on his knee.

Valens had an arm round the boy's middle. The other rested open on the stone at his side. His eyes were on the sand.

Felix tried again under the rod, shield high, and the fish crest swung after the shield. He came round low the far way with the sword, at the bare place behind Rusticus's knee. Rusticus chopped down and across with the board. Its bronze rim caught him on the upper arm, above the quilting. He gave two steps. Rusticus gave two steps. Blood showed at the border of the cloth on Felix's arm, ran a dark finger's length down the quilting, and went no farther.

They stood and breathed, two strides apart.

The man with the rod looked to the tribunal. The duumvir sat there in the shade of the canvas, gray-haired, a slave at his side holding a cup. He considered the benches nearest him, and bent toward the man at his elbow.

He would make them go on. There was no doubt in me.

The herald walked out. He cried that the giver of the games sent them both off standing.

For a breath the sound meant nothing to me. It started with the men below, a deep hooting with whistles riding on it. Behind the noise came whatever they had in their hands: nut shells, crusts, a sandal that tumbled end over end and dropped on the sand between them. Rusticus made for the door below the editor's box with his board on his arm, looking at no one. Felix stood alone at the middle with the sandal at his feet, the griffin raised and turning slowly to face each bank of seats in turn, until the slaves with the whips came and walked him off.

Before anybody had cleared the sandal away, the horns were sounding for the next pair. Beside me the bean woman sat and began gathering up her beans.

"You know that bird?" she said.

"My boy," said Valens.

When I unclenched my hands, the bench's grit had printed itself into both palms.

Valens rose then, Lucius on his arm.

He took the stair outside to the ground and made his way to the long tunnel at the north end. The porter there knew him from the sanding and let us through with a nod. Inside it was cool, with a smell of oil and sand dust. Two fighters of the next pair but one sat waiting, helmets on their knees, a big Gaul with a net bundled at his feet and a boy no older than Felix, and the boy was eating bread so slowly that it scarcely got smaller. The Gaul made a face at Lucius, crossing his eyes. Lucius craned back over his father's arm to keep the Gaul in sight until the tunnel bent.

A bench stood along the passage for the pairs to sit and wait their turn, and above it, at a sitting man's shoulder, the plaster was covered with scratched names and helmets and rows of strokes.

Among them was a palm. It was new; the scratches were white and the dust from them still lay in a little drift along the bench's back. It had been drawn with something with a point, quickly, a spine from bottom to top with fronds laid back along the left of it, and on the right two fronds, and then nothing.

Felix walked in with Nicanor at his back. He had the helmet hooked on two fingers. His hair was black with sweat and plastered flat, and the beard on his jaw looked darker. The blood had dried on the quilting. He saw who was waiting, and stopped.

"You saw him? Bent right over in front of me, neck out, and all of them up there begging me to take it! His ear was mine for the taking. Nicanor says I'm a fool, and I'll fight out the rest of my three years in sheep towns where they can't tell one fighter from another, and no one will cry my name in this town again." He was talking very fast. "Somebody threw his sandal at us."

Valens passed Lucius to me. He caught Felix's arm over the elbow and turned it to the light at the tunnel's end to look at the cut at the edge of the quilting, and Felix let him. "Wash that."

Felix studied the hand on his arm. He looked past Valens's shoulder at the scratched wall.

"When they were all up shouting, I looked for you," he said. "You were sitting down."
