"And a bear, a live one, in a cage on a cart. They had him in at the barracks gate with me right there by it, and he put his paw out through the bars at me in passing, and I never moved, the porter saw me not move. Did you ever see a bear close to? Have they got bears in Syracuse? His claws were longer than my fingers."

Felix filled the shop opening with the morning behind him and a mule's nosebag in each fist. I had vomited into the night pot at cockcrow, quietly, while he slept on, and I sat on the cobbler's old last sipping water, waiting for the shop to hold still.

"Where is Valens?"

"Loading at the yard. He sent me back for these. We cut behind the big theater to save the hill, and the painter's up a ladder on the barracks wall putting the show up on it, all of it, so I stopped." He swung the nosebags wide to show me the size of the wall. "A hunt in the morning with the bear, and four pairs after, and the man giving it is a Fabius who wants to be aedile, and there's awnings if the sun's out. For the Saturnalia. One of the four is a Thracian that's never been beaten. Will he take us? Will you ask him for me? He says no to anything I ask before I've got to the end of asking it."

"It is a month to the Saturnalia."

"Twenty-six days. The painter told me." He leaned in and peered at me. "You're green again. It's that fish of Grata's. It's gone off, I've said so. My nose finds it out in the stable."

"Go on to Valens. He is standing in the yard waiting on his nosebags."

"You'll ask him, though."

"You have a mouth."

He was gone, and back for the second nosebag, which he had put down to show me the claws, and ran again.

I finished with the shutters. On the second plank, among the things waiting their turn, lay a spice-seller's scales: a bronze beam the length of my forearm, a pan on three chains, and the hook from which the whole of it hung, with the pin gone that ought to have held beam to hook. The pan still smelled of his cumin. For a breath I stood again at my father's stove, where a dented tin of it had stood all through my childhood and gone into everything he cooked, eggs included. I filed down a nail of bronze to fit the hole and peened its ends over, and hung the scales from my lintel to see whether they swung true.

Before noon the carter who ate Grata's fish on his bread came by with his cart empty and a friend up on the board beside him. The two of them looked into my shop as they passed. The friend grinned at me and patted his own belly, and the carter cuffed him for it, not hard, and they both laughed and rattled away gateward.

Grata crossed to me soon after with the end of a loaf and a piece of cheese on it, and left it on my plank, clear of the scales.

"I've told nobody, so you can take that look off your face. That one's told the whole road to the Sarno Gate, he'd sell his mother's secrets to a mule for a carrot. Get that down you. Your man doesn't know yet. I'd know it if you had, he'd have been at my counter for a jug by first light, and then they lie on their backs all the next day and leave the work to you." She lifted the scales' pan on one finger and let it fall back on its chains. "Tell him before the carter tells him, that's all I'll say, and the cheese is sheep's, it'll sit."

"He is not home till dusk."

"The carter's got the whole afternoon, then, and he never talks faster than when he's chewing."

I ate the cheese. It sat.

All afternoon I mended what came in and listened to the street. Each time a man laughed outside I lifted my head. A woman brought a lamp whose nozzle had snapped off. A boy brought a horn comb in two halves, and stayed to watch.

"It's my sister's," he said. "She's to be married at the start of next month."

"She will want it for the wedding, then."

"She'll want it tonight. It lives in her box, and she counts what's in her box every night before she sleeps, and I took it to comb the dog." He leaned his chin on my plank. "The dog was sitting on it. You can't blame the dog."

I could not. I had it whole for him before the shade had crossed the street, and he ran. The sun left the paving and climbed the fronts of the houses opposite, and the street began to smell of supper cooking behind every shutter.

They came in as the light failed. Felix led the string round to the stalls, whistling. Valens stood in the opening with the last light on his back. He had sand in the hairs of his forearms and something in his hand.

"Did anyone speak to you on the way home?" I said.

"The lime carter. At the crossing." In his hand were two leaves of wood bound face to face with a cord, and wax in the knot. "He'd been to Puteoli. He looks at the board for me."

"Valens. I am going to have a child."

He stayed on the sill.

"Who knows?"

"Grata. The carter who eats at her bench. By now, the carter's friends." It was not the question I had pictured him asking, and I heard how my answer sounded. "I wanted you to have it from me, ahead of the street."

"Since when?"

"Last night. Grata knew before I did."

"Grata." He left the tablet on the plank unopened. "When?"

"With the heat. Summer, she thinks."

He lifted the lamp from its hook and took it to the back. His feet climbed the roof stair, and stopped, and after a silence he called down for the big knife.

He was crouched at the stair's head when I reached him with it, with the lamp beside him, pressing his thumb into the middle of the tread. Under it the board gave, very slightly, and rose again.

"This is where Felix slipped."

"In the summer. With the bowl."

"It's worn through to the soft. A foot goes off it sideways." He took the knife and set its point under the tread's end, where the peg met the stringer, and began to work. "There's oak behind the inn yard. I'll have a new one in by morning."

"Will it take my weight?"

"It'll take the both of you."

I left him to it. For an hour I heard him overhead, the knife biting and the old tread groaning off its pegs, and a quiet while he went for the oak, and then the knife again. Felix let himself in from the stalls and went to his blanket. When Valens came down at last he was carrying the old tread. In the middle it was hollowed deep, and polished there like horn by every foot that had used it since the house was built.

It went on the hearth for burning. That was when I began to laugh, and he waited with oak shavings in his hair and looked at me until I was done.

"It's a stair," he said.

"So I see."

"Are you sick with it?"

"In the mornings."

"Grata's fish."

"Everything is Grata's fish."

We read the tablet with our curtain drawn, the lamp between us on the bed, because of Felix. Valens broke the wax and read it first, as he read everything, slowly and without a sound, start to end. Then he turned the leaves to face me.

It was from Plautia's brother, in a scribe's hand, round and even.

*To Gaius Iulius Valens, greeting. The praetor has given us our day and our judge. The matter of my sister's dowry will be heard at Rome, in the Basilica Julia, in April. My freedman will wait at the wine-seller's by the harbor at Puteoli from the Kalends of March to the Ides, with money for the road for you and yours. Any witness not with him when the Ides is out we will take to have stayed at home. Farewell.*

Under it, in another hand, smaller and pressed harder into the wax, the letters crowding toward the edge:

*Four lines since Corinth. Is she well? Bring her, and I will see for myself.*

"She wants you to come," I said.

"She wants you. Her brother wants me." He shut the leaves on it.

"Four lines?"

"There was the dun's hoof to tell her about."

"It says you and yours."

"You. And whatever you're carrying."

"You told her you would stand up."

"I told her in Corinth." He stood the tablet on the shelf above the bed, by the oil jar, and stretched out. "The Ides is far enough."

He pinched the wick. The smell of cut oak reached us through the dark.

I lay in the dark, my palm on the blanket over my stomach, with nothing yet to find there. All my palm found was supper. I thought of the maternity floor at Pennsylvania Hospital, where I had once carried a pot of tulips up to a colleague; of its smell of floor wax and lilies.

"There's no step on that stair now will turn your foot," Valens said, beside me in the black.

Felix heard it from the baker's boy along with the loaves. He ran in through the shop while Valens was at the door tying the lead rope to the dun's headstall, and brought up short against my plank, loaf in fist.

"Is it true? The baker's boy says the carter says Grata says. When does it come? Is it a boy? Where's it going to sleep? Not in the shop, I hope, with me, I'll never get a night's rest again." He wheeled round on Valens. "You knew. You knew it last night, and you let me go to bed."

"I knew it last night."

"And I went to bed like a fool, and slept." He glared at me too, for good measure. "It'll want its own bowl. I'll get it one next market, with a murmillo on, with the fish on his helmet. The potter by the Herculaneum Gate does them in red with the shield in black, and he wants a whole copper for one, and I'll pay it."

"It'll want teeth before it wants a bowl," said Valens.

"I'll keep the bowl till it's got them."

He went out after the string telling the dun about it. Valens lingered in the doorway. Opposite, Grata was taking her shutters down board by board and calling something to her cat.

"She'll want more for the rooms," he said. "With a child through her wall."

"She gave me cheese."

"Cheese is cheap."

"She told me to tell you before the carter could."

"You did." He went after Felix and the mules.

* * *

Fabius had hired every mule string in the town to sand his arena. The contractor took the money for it and paid his own muleteers in bone, a little disc each with a seat number scratched on it, and Felix had all three of ours strung at his throat before the bear was dead.

I missed the bear. I came out at midday with bread for Valens, by the lane that ran between the palaestra wall and the vineyards, through a crowd already pressing toward the stairs, and found him where Felix had said, in the long passage that ran under the seats into the arena at its north end. The last of the string waited by the tunnel's opening with their baskets empty. Valens was raking the sand smooth where the bear had been dragged out over it, a long dark smear running away across the oval, and the rake brought the smell up with it.

He took the bread and ate it with the rake leaning on his shoulder.

"The women sit at the top."

"I know which rows are ours."

"Sit at the end of a row. By the stair." He tore the last of the bread in two and gave the dun half. "When it lets out they all go for the stair together."

Out on the oval the sand lay pale under the sun. The walls round it were painted with things I had no time to look at, beasts running, a man with a whip, and above the walls the seats went up in their bands to the top, filling. Somebody up there was already singing.

A short bald man walked up the tunnel from the arena end with three young fighters behind him. He was bandy-legged, in a good cloak, with a rod of polished wood in his fist, and he walked straight past us onto the sand and stood on it. The fighters hung back in the shade. He took two steps, and stopped, and pressed his heel into the sand before he put his weight on it, and then the other heel.

"Who laid this?" he said, with his back to us.

"The contractor's strings," said Valens.

"Who raked it?"

"I did."

"It's soft yonder, under the tribunal. They'll go down there." He returned to us off the sand and stood looking down at Valens's feet. "Do that again."

"Do what?"

"What you did just now, when you stepped down to the sand. With your heel."

Valens had gone no nearer him than the sand's edge, to find what was soft, and before his weight went onto it he had tried it with his heel. I had watched him do the same on ice, and in snow, and on the deck of Pacuvius's ship when it was wet.

"I taught you that," the bald man said. "At Corinth, in the old school under the hill, with the Syrian that had no wind and the boy from Argos that cried. I taught you that before I ever put a shield on you. Heel, and then the weight." The pleasure in him stayed round his eyes. "Lupus."

Felix had been perched on the passage wall with his seat tokens, swinging his legs. His legs stopped.

"Nicanor," said Valens.

"You're bigger in the arms." Nicanor tipped his rod at the empty string. "I heard you'd been sold on and died in Asia. Somebody told me you'd drowned off Kenchreai. I said, that one doesn't drown, he walks home on the bottom." He spoke over his shoulder to the young fighters. "This is him. The murmillo Nero freed. Nine times at Corinth, and he never lifted his finger to the editor in any of them."

The three young men looked at Valens with the rake in his arm. One of them, the youngest, with a fresh scar still pink through his eyebrow, looked at him longest.

Felix slid down, wordless. He stood by the dun with the string of bone tokens in his fist.

"I rake sand," Valens said.

"So you do. Fabius is up in the tribunal. He'll hear of this before the Thracian's out."

"He'll hear it from you."

"He'll hear it from these three, at the oil, before he's sat down." He jerked his chin at them. "Go on, you. Up and get your oil." They went, the youngest looking back till the dark took him. Nicanor watched them go. "The middle one's going to die today. He drops his shield when he's tired, and the Thracian knows it." He followed them into the tunnel's shade.

"Is he right?" I said.

"He's been wrong." Valens wiped the rake's teeth clean on the tunnel wall. "Not often."

I climbed the outer stair with the women. It was steep, and I climbed it in a press of wives and slave girls and grandmothers, one with a basket of eggs, until we came out at the top under the open sky. The women sat up here, the whole top ring of the bowl; the law said so, and the women seemed glad of it, because from here they could see who sat beside whom on every bench below. I took the end of a row next to the steps, beside a girl with a baby asleep on her shoulder.

The whole oval lay under me. On the near side, low down, was the tribunal with its awning, and a youth whose toga showed very white against the shade, sitting under it with his friends. Lower, in the men's rows, I found Valens because Felix was beside him, standing up, until somebody pulled him down.

Horns. A water organ in the depths of the place that wheezed and caught. The first pair walked out and round the sand together and saluted the tribunal, and the crowd shouted their names at them, and they fought. I watched the first exchange and then I watched the girl's baby instead, who slept through the horns and the shouting with his lips apart, and only woke when the crowd went quiet. The quiet meant one of them was down. When I looked, the one on the sand had his finger up toward the tribunal, and the young man under the awning looked round at his friends and lifted his hand. The crowd breathed out. A slave trotted onto the sand with a palm branch and gave it to the one standing, who held it up and turned with it to every side of the arena in turn, and somebody threw him a purse.

The second pair was the Thracian and Nicanor's middle one. I did not watch that. I watched the girl beside me unwrap a sausage, and when the crowd let out a noise it had not given the first pair, low and drawn out, I went on watching her eat it. Afterward I saw the middle one walked away between two slaves, on his own feet, with his shield arm held across his chest in the other hand.

Then, with the arena empty, a man in a red cloak walked out to the middle of the sand, a boy at his heels with a board, and lifted his arms until the benches quieted.

"Thanks to the gods, and to Gaius Fabius Rufinus, who gives you this day," he cried, and his voice rose through the bowl to us whole, every word, "there is among you on these benches one who took his freedom at Corinth from the hand of Caesar himself! Lupus, murmillo, nine times victor! Lupus, who sits among you!"

The whole bowl swung on itself to look.

Felix was up again, pointing down at the crown of Valens's head, his arm straight out. The nearest men turned, then those behind them, and then a whole block of benches together, and the shouting started from that place and spread. *Lupus.* It ran the oval and ran it again. A man two rows below Valens reached up and slapped him on the shoulder, and another stood on his bench to see.

Valens's right hand came up off his knee. It rose as high as his ear, with the palm open toward the tribunal, the way the pair on the sand had held their hands up when they first walked out. It hung there while the shouting went once more round the bowl.

Then he gripped the back of Felix's tunic with that hand and sat him down.

"That's the muleteer," said somebody behind me. "Him that lodges at Grata's."

"With the Greek wife."

"That mends."

I felt them find me, face after face down the row. The grandmother with the eggs leaned across the girl and her baby and pressed an egg on me. It was boiled, and still warm from being sat on in her basket.

"For the child," she said. "Grata says the heat. Eat it now, you'll want it later and it'll be cold."

I had not told anybody in that whole bowl of people anything. I sat there holding the egg.

"Is that yours?" the girl with the baby asked. "The one with the boy that won't sit?"

"Yes."

"Mine's on the stair selling cushions." She shifted the baby to her other shoulder. "He charged me for this one. His own wife. A copper, and that's the price, he says, and it's flat as a griddle cake, look, there's no wool in it at all, he's sold the wool out of it to somebody at the top."

"To whom?"

"To a woman with a cushion, I should think." She pressed the flat one with her free hand, gloomily. "He'll have sold her mine and charged me for the cover."

Below, Felix had swung about on his bench and was telling the men behind him something with his hands, a shield on one arm and a blade in the other fist, and the men were listening to him.

The herald waited, and when no one stood to be cheered, he spread his arms at the crowd as if to say he had tried, and the third pair came out.

Nicanor and a smooth young freedman, in a clean tunic and with a tablet open in his hand, were standing in the tunnel when the show let out, and the crowd parted round them. Valens had the string roped together and was leading the dun out; he stopped because the freedman stepped into the dun's path, and the dun stopped because Valens had.

"Gaius Fabius Rufinus sends his greeting," the freedman said. "He gives another show at the Parilia. He asks that Lupus open it. An exhibition between the pairs, with the practice sword, against the best of his own murmillos. No blood. Two thousand sesterces for the afternoon, half of it now, and a seat at his dinner after."

"No," said Valens.

The freedman's stylus waited above the wax and wrote nothing. Behind him Nicanor was looking at the dun's feet.

"My master asks what Lupus would take," the freedman said.

"Lupus died in Asia," said Valens. "Or drowned off Kenchreai. Ask Nicanor." He clicked to the dun. She went on, and the freedman had to step out of her way or have his foot trodden on.

Nicanor took a groom's place at the black's head.

"Your lad never shut his mouth through the whole of the third pair. He's built for it."

"He's built for a shovel."

"I've seen worse start." He glanced back for Felix, who was behind us leading the last mule and looking at nothing. "Who does he belong to?"

"His own. I freed him."

"Then I'll not need to ask you." Nicanor halted where the sand ended and let the string go by him, and lifted his rod to me as I went past, a courtesy no one had paid me before.

At the bottom of the outer stair, where the crowd thinned onto the road, somebody had already worked on the pier with a nail. Among the old names and tallies, low down, fresh scratches showed white through the dirt on the plaster: a helmet with a fish along its crest, drawn by someone who knew one close, and under it, in big uneven capitals, LVPVS. The final S ran off the stone, where the boy had been jostled.

Valens walked past it with the string. Three paces on, he stopped.

"The fish faces the wrong way," he said.

On the road home his back was as straight as a post. He kept catching himself at it, letting his shoulders down, and a dozen paces on they were up again.

Felix kept beside the dun the length of the road, and through the gate, and as far as our door, and he asked not one thing.

* * *

That night Valens washed at our basin well past what the dust of one day needed. He scrubbed his face and his nape and his forearms to the elbow, and then the hand that had lifted to the benches, alone, the palm and between the fingers. He dried it and looked at it, and plunged it again.

The street had it by morning. The coppersmith shouted it to Valens from his doorway as the mules passed, and a baker's boy ran after him calling it.

In the afternoon Fabius's freedman came to my plank. In daylight he was younger, and his nails were very clean. He read my board on the pier from the paving, every word, before he came in.

"The wife of Lupus," he said.

"Valens's wife. He is at work."

"My master raises it to three thousand." He laid a purse by a glass cup that was waiting its turn on my plank, and the cup chimed. "For an afternoon, at the Parilia, with a stick of wood and no blood. And his name painted on the walls as far as Nuceria, under my master's own. Fifteen hundred there, for earnest. He'll not ask for it if the answer stays the same."

It was a fat purse. It was more than the contractor would pay Valens between one vintage and the next, and the child was due in the summer, and the purse sat on my plank and did not go away.

"Take it back to him."

"He'll only send me again. I saw a Lupus once, at Nuceria, when I was little. Not him. Another one, a Lupus from Capua, there's always a Lupus. My father had me on his shoulders. I told my father I would be a murmillo, and he laughed so hard he had to put me down, and the next year he sold me to the Fabii."

"What became of him?"

"He keeps a cookshop at Puteoli now, by the fish market. He's fat. I went in once." He looked up at my scales. "Your husband never asked for the finger? Not once, in nine?"

"You would have to ask him."

"He'd not tell me." He tucked the purse away.

Going, he stopped at Grata's and drank a cup standing, and paid for it. When he was gone she came across and stood in my opening with her arms crossed under her bosom.

"He'll be back. They come back when it's money. You sent it off, I saw you, and quite right, it's not the sand he's for, a man built like that, he's for sitting on a bench in the shade where the street can see him and pouring for. If I'd one like that I'd not have him carting rubble for a contractor that shorts him, I'd sit him on my bench in a clean tunic and let the custom come to look. My husband's never lifted anything heavier than a wine cup." She looked over at her own counter, where her husband was not. "You'll tell him I said so. You'll not, I know you. I'll tell him myself."

She had it ready for Valens when he got in at dusk, with her forearms on the counter and a jug already poured.

"Sit down on that bench, you'll sit down, I've had men stop at my counter all day asking which door is the wolf's, and me with no answer for them but the door I let to a muleteer. Twenty times on the sand, the baths have it, and a sword off Caesar, and you've drunk my thin wine on that bench these two springs without a word, and paid, and that's better than the aediles do. That's the Falernian. You needn't eye it, it's paid for, it's paid for by the whole street. The fuller's woman sent it." She was round the end of the counter in a moment. "Look at the state of you." She brushed the sand off the shoulder of his tunic with the side of her hand, and the far shoulder, and let her hand stay on it after the sand was gone. "Fabius's man's been in twice. He's a poor sort of freedman, he's got clean nails, he'll be in again tomorrow, so you'll sit on that bench where I've an eye on you and let him find you there, and I'll pour."

She had her earrings in, the gold ones with the little pearl that she kept for the baths. They were new to her counter.

Valens drank the Falernian standing.

"That's good wine," he told her.

"It's the best on this street, and you'll not have it free twice, so you'll sit next time."

He thanked her for it and crossed to our door, and she was left with her hand in the air.

"She wants us out," he said, once our curtain was drawn.

I had unhooked the scales from the lintel, for the spice-seller was to fetch them early, and I nearly let the pan go.

"She brushed your shoulder."

"She was looking for the place to start." He let himself onto the bed and began to unwind the rag from his wrist, where a rope had burned him that morning. "She's got a fighter under her roof now, and Fabius's men in and out at her counter, and a baby to keep her awake by summer. She'll have the rent raised before the month's out, and if I won't pay it she'll have the excuse she wants to put us in the road."

"She gave you Falernian."

"She gave me the fuller's wife's Falernian, in front of her bench. So the street sees she's no quarrel with me, when she puts us out. She's never liked me. She watches me across that counter all day long."

"She had her earrings in."

"For Fabius's freedman. So he'd see she has gold to lose, if he brings his trouble to her door."

I lowered the scales onto the plank, very carefully. My face was doing something I could not stop. I turned it to the lamp until it behaved.

"Fabius has gone up to three thousand," I told him. "His freedman put the earnest down on my plank so that a customer's cup rang."

He was still. "Where is it?"

"I sent it home with him."

"And Rome?" I asked.

"Fabius isn't Rome."

"Fabius is why you will not go."

He raised his eyes from the burn.

"At the show," I said. "You thought your hand went up unseen."

"I saw you see it. You were the only woman on the rim not eating. My hand went up to them. I hadn't told it to."

"Nobody at the Basilica," I said, "will want you to lift your hand. They will want you to say what happened. That is all."

"They want my name. Said aloud, before a judge, and Celer's advocate, and every idler in the basilica that morning. And then a clerk has it in ink. Here it's scratched on one pier, low down, in a boy's letters. After Rome, Celer knows where I sleep. His freedmen know. So does any man on those benches with a mind to sell it him: a Lupus with a Greek wife, in a cobbler's shop in Pompeii, beside a fish stall."

"Celer knows already. You told him you were coming to Rome. He heard you."

"I said that because he'd offered me a farm."

"And when it is over he will have paid back a dowry and lost. Men who have lost do not come looking. They go home and dine."

He did not raise his voice; he never did. "A child's coming. Since the ship I've been nobody here, and I've slept all those nights through."

"You sat me by the stair at the show."

"That was the crowd."

"There is always a crowd. Every place we have been, you have looked for the way out of it before you looked at anything else. Our first night under this roof you asked me which gate was nearest, and then you tried that door twice before you had so much as unrolled a blanket."

"The door was sticking. There's always a way out. I find it first."

"I know you do."

I took the bed's foot.

"You can be nobody in Pompeii for the rest of your life," I said. "You can cart stone and pay Grata's rent and wait, morning after morning, for a man in the street to look at you twice. And he will. Nicanor knew you by your heel. Felix will have told every boy in the barracks before the Kalends." I heard my voice go up, and could not bring it down. "Or you can go to Rome and say it once, aloud, and have it written, and be believed, and come home. And then if somebody looks twice, let him look."

"Have you got the other blanket in there?" It was Felix, from the shop. "I'm not listening. Have you, though? It's gone cold enough out here to crack the water jar."

I pulled the second blanket off our feet and pushed it round the curtain's edge to him. It was snatched, and the shop creaked with him rolling himself up in it, and then was quiet.

"Be believed," Valens said.

"Yes."

"By who? The judge? The judge has never seen me. He's seen Celer, at dinner."

"Plautia believed you, and her brother is paying your road on the strength of it. I believe you."

"You weren't there."

"No. I was not there. I believe you anyway."

"And after it I come home and cart stone, the same as now."

"You cart stone. I beat the dust of it out of your tunic every week."

"And they'll ask about you. Celer's advocate will. Who is the witness's wife. Who her father was. Which house in Syracuse. He'll have a man at Syracuse before the heat, knocking on doors."

I had a name in a clerk's book at Delphi, and a kinswoman whom everybody there believed had sailed, and nobody at all in Syracuse. I told him none of it, and he let it lie.

He lay back and closed his eyes. Next door Grata was laughing at something her husband had said, from low down in her chest.

* * *

Felix missed the string two mornings running in the last month of the winter. On the third Valens went out with the string alone. At nightfall he mentioned that he had not had to give his name once all day; the other muleteers said it for him now. He ate leaning on the doorpost. Then he left, lamp and all, and I threw my cloak on and caught him up at the fountain.

We went on past it in the dark, and turned left for the theaters, with every shutter closed along the way and the lamp throwing our two shadows ahead on the paving stones. I had had a burning under my breastbone since the Saturnalia and no Latin word for it. I called it, to Grata, the fire under the bone. Grata had laughed at me for it and given me a lump of chalk to suck, and it had worked.

"Where will he be?"

"Where Nicanor is."

"Is he old enough for them?"

"The slaver said twelve, at Kirrha. Slavers say what sells."

"Fifteen, then. Sixteen."

"Of an age to swear himself to a lanista, if one will have him. There's no paper on him. I never had one made. Nobody can stop him but Nicanor."

"Can you?"

"I gave him that much. I can't unsay it to keep him off the sand."

"You could tell him what it was. The sand."

"He was there. He saw a man walked off it between two slaves holding his own arm on."

"He saw a palm," I said. "And a purse thrown."

The street fell away, and the high back wall of the big theater rose dark ahead.

The barracks were at the theaters' back, in what had been a great walk of columns round an open yard where people strolled between the plays, before the earthquake. Now the columns were walled up, with a gate and a porter, and from the yard behind it came the knock of wooden swords on wooden posts. Over the gate a lamp burned on a bracket. The plaster on the gateposts was scratched over with names and fighters and tallies, up past where a man's arm would go.

Felix was sitting under the lamp with his back to the gatepost, his knees drawn up. He had a nail in his hand. He was working at the plaster beside his shoulder and did not hear us until Valens's shadow went over him.

He had scratched a palm branch. It was not much bigger than my hand: a stem, with short strokes slanting upward off it to left and right, close together. Each cut showed fresh and white. He had cut the stem twice over, so that it sank deeper.

"Nicanor says I've the shoulders for it, or will have," Felix said, before we had a word out. He kept his seat against the post. "He had me in the yard this afternoon with a stick. Too old to start and too thin to finish, and I'm to try him again when I've a beard. You were worse, he says, your first month, that you couldn't keep a shield up while a man said his own name, and you were bigger than me." He drew the nail down the last of the palm's strokes, slowly, deepening it. "He says you never told me because you're ashamed. Are you? Why would you be? They shouted it round the whole bowl. I heard them."

"Grata's kept you some supper," said Valens.

"Will you speak to him? He'd have me tomorrow if you said."

Valens reached for the nail. Felix rose, and kept it.

"I asked him today. I'll ask him tomorrow, and the day after that." He was taller than me now, I saw, and had been for some time. "He'll get tired of saying it before I get tired of hearing it."

Off he stalked homeward, nail in hand and shoulders up, and never turned his head to see if we were following.

"Go after him," I said.

"He'll go home. He's hungry."

The porter had gone in at the sound of Felix's voice. Now the gate opened a crack and Nicanor stood in it, in an old tunic, with chalk on his hands from the posts. Behind him the young men went on at the posts without him.

"Three days he's sat on my step," he said. "He eats my porter's bread. I send him off and he comes back with a nail."

"Send him off again."

"I have. He comes back. He'll outlast me at it, that one. Fabius's man was at my gate today. I said you'd lost your wind."

"I have."

"You've lost nothing. You tried my sand with your heel." He looked the way Felix had gone. "If he comes to my gate with a beard on him, and nobody's said no to him by then that he'll listen to, I'll take him. You know I will."

"You take them all."

"I took you." He looked at the cloak I held shut across my belly. "Go home. Both of you. It's cold for her out here."

He shut the gate. The bar went across on the inside. Somewhere in the yard a wooden sword clattered on the paving, and a young voice swore at it, and someone laughed.

Valens held the lamp to the palm. Inside, the wooden swords went on knocking, slower now, with Nicanor's voice between the blows, too far off for words. I fitted a fingertip into the topmost stroke. The plaster crumbled at the edge of it, a little, and the white came away on my skin.

"He cannot write his name."

"A palm. I've eyes."

"You could teach him to write his name under it."

"Felix thinks I can't read."

"Felix believes whatever suits him."

"Then he'd know I'd let him think it four years."

Over the palm, high on the gatepost where a tall boy could reach on his toes, LVPVS showed again, smaller this time and neater, with a little wreath round it. Valens slid his knife in where the wreath's two ends met and lifted the wreath off whole.

"Rome," I said. "Somebody has to give Plautia's brother's freedman an answer. You have until the Ides."

"I can count to the Ides." He lowered the lamp. The palm went into shadow, and the name. He stood with the light hanging at his knee, looking at the dark plaster where they were, and I wanted him to say Rome, or say no, and he did neither of those things. He turned to me instead. What was he to tell the child, he asked me, when it was grown and came to that gate and read the wall, and asked him what he had been?
