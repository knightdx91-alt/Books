I was kneading when the floor moved.

The board went first. It slid toward me a little and slid back, and the dough rode with it under my knuckles, and for half a breath I blamed my own weight. Then the water jar beside the hearth began to talk. Its water slapped the clay, this side, that side. A spoon jumped from the shelf's lip into the ash.

Under my feet the trodden floor heaved, set me down, and heaved again. My hands went still in the dough. In the stalls all four mules were kicking their boards, and the street's dogs started up, each setting off the next. Somewhere a tile let go from a roof and burst on the paving.

Over the hearth the wall gave a dry little ticking. A line opened in the plaster under the beam. It ran downward in jerks, the way ink runs down a wet page, and quit short of the fire. All along it a thin white dust sifted out and hung, turning, where the light-well sent its slant down. It settled on the hearthstone. It settled on the board, and on the dough.

The floor lay still.

Aelia had been sitting on the earth by Lucius's pallet, drumming a lid on her knees. When the ground rose under her she shrieked with laughter and slapped the floor with both palms, hard, to make it heave again. It did nothing for her.

I had her in my arms with no memory of crossing the room. She was warm and whole and she wanted down; she shoved at my chin with her fists until I returned her to the floor.

Lucius had bolted in to me; his father was out front mending a headstall. I checked him all round, as I would a dropped pot. He frowned up at the wall.

"Wall broke," he said.

* * *

"Castor," said the wall.

It was Grata, on her own side of it. The crack brought her through to us whole.

"Castor and his brother and their mother besides, there goes my good wall, there goes a plasterer's day and his boy's day after it, and lime at the price they're asking since the forum swallowed every cartload in Campania, and it's the landlady's purse for it, same as always. And you'll say it was nothing. The man I married says it was nothing, and he never woke for it."

High against the beam, where the crack gaped widest, a stripe of her room showed. A brown eye appeared in the stripe and glared through at our hearth.

"Grata," said Lucius, pointing.

"You keep off that hearth, my lad, there's live coals under that ash."

Valens had followed him in, the headstall dangling from his fist. First he tried the beam over Aelia's head, shoving up at it, and the beam held. He dropped the headstall beside my board and worked one finger into the split, chin-high, to learn how deep it went.

"Get your finger out of my wall," said Grata.

Valens laughed. Lucius laughed because his father had, eyes squeezed shut, both hands clapped over his mouth, and Aelia laughed at the pair of them at the top of her lungs, for no reason she needed.

"It goes clean through," Valens told the crack.

"I know it goes through, I've your hearth in my eye as I stand here and it wants sweeping."

The eye withdrew. On her side we heard Grata starting her husband on the price of lime once more. Valens wanted me in on it. He saw I was not laughing.

I stood at the board, my eyes on the crack.

The crack had traveled down another man's work. After the big shake fifteen years back a plasterer had mended that wall, and mended it well; the crack had found his repair under the limewash and run the length of it, beam to hearth, the way a zipper parts. The edges of his patch showed now on both sides of it, a shade grayer than the wall round them.

A fine skin of the dust had settled over the dough.

In Puteoli Fenn had told me the doors went with the ground. When it moved hard enough, they could open. On the night of the big shake a family eating late on the tomb road had felt cold come off Eros's door till nearly dawn, and Fenn heard of it from a carter, too late to go.

That one had been hard. It had broken a mend that had held for fifteen years.

I watched the crack and wished it wider.

I wiped my hands down my skirt and left by the shop, past my plank, to the paving. Behind me Valens said my name, once.

* * *

The street was full of people standing in it.

The shake had emptied the doorways, and now people were drifting back in, slowly, talking. A roof tile lay smashed across the paving outside the dyer's. A woman with a hen clamped under one arm was pointing up at her own roof with the other. A pair of old men on the fuller's bench were already quarreling about the year of the shake before.

"It was the year my mule died," one said.

"You were riding that mule to Nola all the spring after," said the other.

I ran.

The paving was gritty with fallen plaster under my sandals. Skirt gathered in my hand, I went east past them. A dog ran with me a while and gave it up. The women queued at the fountain swung round, jars and all, to watch me by. Aelia had last fed at dawn, and the running jarred my breasts, which were full. At the crossing my sandal strap came loose, and from there on it slapped my ankle at each stride. A man in a cookshop door shouted after me that it was over, Greek, nothing to run from. I turned south toward the Nuceria Gate.

Each Kalends I passed under that arch at a walk, with a few violets in a cloth: a woman going out to her dead. A woman with violets got no second look on that road. I had not run it before.

In the gate passage a boy was gathering up the pieces of a dropped jar. He was fitting them together on the paving. Another day I would have stopped and shown him how the handle sat. I went round him.

Outside, the tombs stood in full sun. Not one had fallen. A goat tied to somebody's railing was eating the violets off a tomb step.

My breath was all in the top of my chest, and my side hurt. Ahead, low and white among its neighbors, there it was.

* * *

Eros's door had had the sun on it since morning.

My palm met it near the foot of the left leaf, and I pressed.

The paint was warm. My palm was hot from the run, and at first I felt only the two warmths together, mine and the sun's. I stepped in close, so that my shadow covered the place, and waited for the sun's heat to fade from it.

It left slowly. Under it, when it rose, the coolness was the one I had found there when we were new in the town. Faint. A little less warm than sunlit paint ought to be, and nothing past that.

I pressed harder. Nothing in it altered.

I leaned my forehead against the leaf. The paint smelled of dust and lime, and it was warm against my brow from the sun.

The ground shivered under my feet. It was a small one, the tail of the first, there and gone; grit trickled off the cornice of the big tomb next door and pattered on its step. I bore down with palm and fingers together, and waited for the door to answer the ground. Eros's bench was against my shin, where Fenn had sat his nights out.

No breath moved out of it. The dust at its foot lay undisturbed. Sweat was going cold along my spine, and the door was a door on a tomb, faintly cool, the same as on the day I found it.

No colder.

* * *

Behind me I heard a man's breath, hard and fast.

I turned. Valens was standing in the road.

He had followed me out without his cloak. There was plaster dust in his hair, from our ceiling. His chest was heaving. He had pulled up on the road's crown, a rut on either hand, ten paces off, and came no closer.

The door was in front of him, and my hand on it. He saw where I had run to. I had no story ready.

He had known for years that there was a door. Its place I had told nobody.

He read the name over it. I watched him read it. His lips moved on it once, without sound. His hands hung empty at his sides; the headstall lay by my board at home.

He put no question to me.

We went back with a gap between us. In the arch he turned for one look back at the tombs, the look he gave a ford once the mules were over it. He entered the passage ahead of me.

* * *

Under the gate he spoke. "Grata has them."

I had forgotten them since the kneading. Now I wanted to run home as hard as I had run out, and had no run left in me. A girl of fourteen months and a boy not yet three, left with a hearth and a cracked wall, and I had run a mile.

Valens matched his pace to my loose sandal. I stopped at last and tied it, badly, and let it be.

In the town they were already at their repairs. A plasterer on a trestle outside a wine shop was trowelling wet lime into a split above the doorway, his loaded board held aloft. A boy below him mixed more in a trough. The wine seller stood under his sign, arms crossed on his belly.

"Yours as well?" the plasterer called down to Valens, trowel going.

"The landlady's."

"Tell her she's behind the cooper and the widow and this fellow here. Tomorrow, if the lime comes."

Farther on a woman was sweeping up her broken tiles with a broom of twigs, while a man on the roof handed down more and named what each had cost him. Boys ran past with a basket of lime between them, slopping it. At the crossing shrine a woman had set a honey cake on the altar under the painted snakes and was telling the snakes about her roof. It was a mess, and people were clearing it. On the fuller's bench the two old men had settled the year between them and gone on to roof tiles.

Passing the fountain I had an answer ready for the question, a poor one, about my dead and what a Greek owed them out of season. I did not need it.

Grata had Aelia on her hip at the counter and Lucius sitting on the marble with his legs hanging down. She saw us coming from forty paces and started before we were in earshot.

"He hands me your two over my own counter, the big ox, and off after you like a hound off its rope, and never a word, and the boy wanting his mother, and the girl chewing my sleeve, and my wall open to your fire so I'll smell your onions in my bed till the Kalends. And the plasterer's promised to the cooper. You've run yourself scarlet, and that girl's wanted the breast this hour past. They're yours again, my pot's catching."

I lifted Lucius down and took Aelia, who had a fistful of Grata's sleeve and had to be unpicked. Valens looked in on the mules first, all four, and then set his shoulder to our wall beside the crack and leaned on it, hard. The wall bore it.

"It'll hold," he said.

The dough was on the board as the shake had left it, under its skin of dust. I shaped it into flat cakes, dust and all, and baked them under the clay bell in the hot ash, and at midday we ate them with oil and the hard end of a cheese.

The bread crunched. Everybody ate theirs anyway, me as well, it was bread.

Lucius bit, and spat the bite into his palm to hunt for whatever had crunched, came up empty, and finished it.

"The wall," said Valens.

Lucius climbed off his stool to inspect the crack, close up, hands clasped behind him. Aelia had a whole cake to herself and wore most of it.

Once during the meal I thought Valens would ask. He asked Lucius for the oil instead, and Lucius carried it to him at a solemn crawl, the jar gripped to his chest.

Before we had finished, a wad of rag came worming through the top of the split from Grata's side and hung there. Lucius stretched for it, toes and fingertips. At its foot, over the hearth, Valens had already stuffed a twist of straw into the bottom of the split against the draught, and the wall was plugged from both sides, hers at the top and ours below.

"Leave my rag be," said Grata, through it.

She talked on till the light changed, and the rag let most of it through.

Later that day the shake brought me trade. The dyer's wife came with a lamp that had jumped off its bracket and lost its spout. A girl of nine or so arrived with her grandmother's bowl, broken in three, carried in the lap of her tunic with the corners gathered up in her fists. Last came my adviser from the cookshop door, with a wine jar split from lip to shoulder, and stood it on my plank, grinning.

"The small shake did that. I'd just told you it was over."

I promised it him for market day. The plank was full by lamplighting, and I took the pieces in turn with the glue pot warming on the coals beside me, its smell of pine and hoof filling the shop. The jar I kept for last. Its split ran clean, and two lead clamps would hold it.

* * *

That night, when the others were down, I lifted the satchel off its nail. Lucius had taken a flake of the fallen plaster to his pallet, and his hand was shut on it yet, the thumb laid over it. Aelia slept crossways at the foot of our bed with her heels against her father's shins.

I trimmed the lamp and drew the notebook from under Fenn's letters. The back leaves were mine. They had been blank when the notebook crossed with me; now my writing ran across them, entry after entry, each dated and short, the ink gone brown on the oldest. Eros's name was in none of them. Nearly five years of Kalends I had walked to his door, and written none of it down.

The last before it was from the vintage. I wet the reed and wrote the day under it.

*Mid-morning. Hearth wall split down the old repair.*

That was the shake. One thing more wanted writing, and it was shorter.

*Eros: the same.*

Valens in the road I left out.

I let it dry, closed the book on it and slid it in again below the bundle. No light showed in the crack; Grata's lamp was out. Valens was a long ridge of wool on the wall side. Whether he slept I had no way to tell.

I did not wake him.
