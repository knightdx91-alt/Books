*A bronze pot has walked off from this shop, the big one, with the handle I had riveted on new at the Saturnalia. Whoever brings it back gets sixty-five sesterces. The thief knows who he is, and so do I.*

It was up on the pier of a cookshop above the bull's-head fountain. Under it, in charcoal and a smaller hand, a reader had added that the thief was enjoying his soup. I had passed it every day for half a month with our water jar riding my hip before I stopped to read it. While I read, a woman with a basket of leeks halted beside me and read it aloud for the old husband on her arm.

"Sixty-five," he said. "For a pot. He could have two new ones and a lid."

"Two new, and not a handle between them worth the name." She hitched the leeks round to her other side. "Riveted, he says. He paid good money for that handle."

"He should have chained it to the wall."

"He should have chained you." She steered him off downhill, and over the leeks, without my asking, she called back to me. "It was his father's pot, that, and it lived through the earthquake with a dent in its side, and he'll tell you the dent's the shape of Nuceria if you stand still for it."

The cook was in his doorway in a stained apron, digging at a splinter in his thumb with a pin. He stared after me, in case the jar was his pot.

At home I put it to Grata. "Where does one buy paint, in this quarter?"

"Nobody buys paint here, they're robbed for it," Grata said. Her forearms were on the counter; the steam off her pot went up past her face. "Them that paint the candidates live behind the theaters, and they'll sell you a cup of red at the price of a jar of wine, and you'll hand it over, you've the face of a payer. Soot's free. There's soot on my brazier every night of the year, and the glue-boiler three doors down will give you size for an egg, and soot in size is black, and black reads best on lime. You're putting up a board. I told you so the week you came, and here's the spring half gone." She caught my upper arm and squeezed it, as a wife at market would squeeze a hen to feel for the meat on it. "Spell it right. There's people on this street can read."

The pier beside our shop opening had been limewashed in the cobbler's time; the lime still took a mark. I stood on the cobbler's broken last to reach the top line. I lettered it with a brush of pig's bristle bound to a stick, dipping into a cracked cup of Grata's soot stirred into size.

*Broken things mended here. Bronze, clay, glass.*

Felix came in from the string at dusk, with lime in his eyebrows, and faced it with his fists on his hips.

"What's written? Which bit's the fish? Did you put me in it anywhere? Who's going to read it, anyway, when half this street can't tell an A from a fishhook?"

"There is no fish, and you are not in it," I said, and read it to him.

"That wants shouting." He dug lime out of one eyebrow with a fingernail. "Market day, I'll stand out on the curb and give it them. There's no voice on the Stabian road to touch mine. The contractor puts me at the head of the string so the carts coming up know to pull over."

Valens read it that night by lamplight on his way in from the mules, when Felix had already gone in to his blanket in the shop.

"Glass," he said. "Who breaks glass, in this street?"

"Everybody who owns any."

He went over it again, the lamp lifted to the pier. "Grata will want her name on it somewhere."

Felix did shout it on market day, from the curb, with his hands round his mouth.

"Cracked your pot? Dropped your lamp? Bring it to the dead cobbler's, next door to Grata's, and she'll have it whole for you by next market! Nobody on this road mends like her!"

Grata let him go round three times before she came out.

"Take that voice off to the forum, you've emptied my bench twice since the shutters were off, and there goes the third man with my cup in his fist and not a copper paid for it. Shout at the fullers."

By noon a woman was at my plank out of the glare, with a stewpot wrapped in her apron.

It was coarse gray ware, black with old fires to the shoulder, and cracked from the lip halfway down its belly. Lower down, my fingers found an older break with lead run through it in two places and beaten flat, standing proud of the clay like scabs.

"That's my mother's," the woman said. "She had a tinker put his dogs in it the winter I was born. Do it the same."

"Which are the dogs?" The word belonged to tinkers.

She set a fingernail on each clamp in turn. "These. Lead. They've not shifted since."

"I can close it so that you will never find the crack again."

"I'd only break it in the same spot, looking for it." She folded her arms over the apron. "Make it tight. My oil lives in it."

I warmed the pot through beside Grata's coals till the clay was hot in my palms. Then I ran resin into the new crack from inside, coaxing it deep into the break with the tip of a heated nail, and pared it flush while still soft. When it had cooled, my own eye could barely find the line. She paid me in bronze and carried the pot off under her arm.

The cook arrived on the last day of that month with his pot on his shoulder. It was a big round-bellied thing of bronze gone brown, and under its lip two rivet holes stood empty where the handle had been.

"Found it in the ditch outside the Sarno Gate," he said, "handle gone and a frog in it. Nobody gets the sixty-five. A fellow told me where, wanting it, and I went and fetched it home myself, so he gets nothing. I want a handle on it."

"I cannot rivet bronze. The coppersmith in the next street could."

"He's the one I'd have said took it." He left the pot on my plank all the same, mouth to the wall. "You've your board. You'll know somebody."

"I will ask him for you."

"Ask him where he was the night it went."

I carried it round to the coppersmith that afternoon, a big soft-spoken man with burns all up his forearms. He knew the pot on sight.

"Riveted that handle on myself," he said. "This winter gone. Good rivets."

"He would like a new handle, and the same rivets."

"He'd like to know where I was the night it walked." He fingered the empty holes. "Tell him I was at my sister's in Stabiae, and her husband will swear to it, and so will her husband's goat. Tell him a copper pot gets no handle off me twice for the one price." He swung the pot round so the dent faced him. "Shape of Nuceria. He tells everybody that."

"It looks like nothing."

"Like nothing on earth," the coppersmith agreed, pleased. He had it fitted with a new handle inside four days. The cook stopped at my plank with it on his way home.

"A handle you could lift a mule by, and he's charged me more than he first asked, like I was the one took the pot. Never again. Next time it walks, it can stay walked."

Valens, meanwhile, had pegged a second plank along the shop wall, for the things that waited their turn.

* * *

Felix broke our bowl on the roof stair in the first hot week of the summer.

It was red and glossy, from the potteries up the coast, with the potter's name stamped inside a little sole of a foot on its floor. We had paid too much for it at the market in our first days in the town. Every night since, we had eaten our supper from it on the tiles. Felix appeared on the stair holding up two pieces for me to see, his face ready to be blamed.

"It slipped on the top tread, where the wood's worn hollow, and the beans went everywhere, and Grata's cat's in them now. I had these two in the air before they ever reached the bottom. That's a drop of two of me."

The other pieces lay among the beans at the stair's foot, and I fished those out myself.

"I'll buy another," Felix said. "I've coppers put by from the contractor, more than you'd think. I'll get a finer one at the market tomorrow noon, with a picture on it."

"I will mend this one."

"What do we eat out of tonight?"

"We have the pot."

I mended it that night at the table in the back room, with the lamp pulled close. The resin was lentisk. The drug-seller under the forum colonnade sold its clear tears to women for sweetening their breath. I melted a little pine pitch in with it, in a mussel shell propped over the flame, until the two ran together. I warmed each piece near the lamp first, so the resin would not chill and go stringy on a cold edge. With a splinter of cane I drew a bead of it along one broken face and brought the faces to meet.

The break was clean; its faces went home into each other grain by grain, with a small dry seating I felt in my fingers rather than heard. The resin squeezed out along the join in a soft brown welt. I held the pieces until they cooled. I wound thread round the bowl to keep them so, and started on the next.

Valens lay on the bed behind me with his hands under his head, watching the lamp, or me.

"The foreman shorted us a load again today," he said.

"Did you let him?"

"The black bit him. He found the load after that."

"Was he hurt?"

"In his pride. And in the meat of his arm, a little." He shifted, and the new cord creaked. "He'll want her sold. He said so to the yard at large."

"Will you sell her?"

"I'll stand her further off from him."

The second piece seated. I wound the thread on.

"Will it hold soup?"

"It will hold anything you put in it."

"Beans will do," he said, and slept.

When the rim piece went in, the mussel shell was cold beside me and the lamp was nearly dry. With the point of Plautia's little knife I shaved the welts down while they were still soft enough to cut like cheese, and left the bowl to stand the night out in its thread.

When it was light I cut the threads and carried it out to the sill.

In daylight the bowl was whole and red. The joins crossed the red in three fine brown lines; at the rim the last piece I had set sat lower than its neighbor by the thickness of a hair. My lower lip found that before my eye did.

A rubble cart was rolling downhill out of the crossroads with a load of broken roof tiles, an old carter walking at the mule's head. When it drew level I reached up and put our bowl on the heap of tiles.

He stopped his mule and came round to the cart's tail, lifted the bowl off the load, and blew the tile dust out of it.

"My wife's been at me for a bowl since the Floralia," he said. "Have you got any more you don't want?"

I gave him no answer. He stowed it in the breast of his tunic and walked on at the mule's head, with the cart creaking behind him and our bowl riding against his belly.

Past noon a girl of six or so came in, towing her mother by the hand, with a clay pig in her fist. The pig was a rattle the size of a plum, with a slit along its back, and it had cracked down one flank, so that the pellets inside dropped out at every shake.

"He won't talk," she told me, and shook him to prove it. Two pellets fell out onto my plank.

"He talked all winter," her mother said. "She took him to the baths. She takes him everywhere."

"He likes the hot room," said the girl.

I fed the pellets back in through the crack with the tip of the knife, and closed the flank over them with a bead of resin, and pinched it shut until it cooled, with the girl standing so close her nose was nearly on my plank. Once it was hard she put the pig to her ear and shook him, and he talked.

"He's better," she told her mother, and left shaking him at every step. Her mother paid me in a hard little cheese.

When Valens got home in the dusk Felix met him at the sill.

"She mended it all night and it was whole, I saw it, you could hardly tell where, and this morning she gave it away to the tile carter, a gray old fellow, and he took it off to his wife. Why would she give it away? Was there a fault in it? Can a mended thing go bad in a night?"

Over Felix's head, Valens met my eye.

"We'll manage with the cookpot," he said.

* * *

In the hot months the carting stopped at noon. Before that, from first gray, Valens worked the string to and fro, Felix with him, between the forum and a tip among the fields by the amphitheater. The town's broken stone left it a basketful at a time on mules' backs.

One morning I carried their bread up to them. The forum lay under a white haze of its own making. At its far end the big temple stood in a cage of poles roped together, with men climbing about in it. On the paving beneath, two quarrymen were breaking up a column drum that had split in the earthquake and lain where it fell ever since. They swung their sledges turn and turn about. At every blow the drum rang and gave up another chip of itself. Felix shoveled the pieces into the mules' baskets, and Valens led each mule off as it filled, with a rag knotted over his nose and mouth and his eyes red above it.

He pulled the rag down to eat. Under it was the one part of his face the dust had not reached.

"What's in it?"

"Grata's olives."

"That's yesterday's bread."

"Grata says it is today's."

"Grata would." He ate it standing, while the sledges went on behind him, and gave the end of it to the dun.

Felix had left his shovel to stand by the quarrymen.

"You're swinging it too high, the pair of you, you're wasting half your arm on the air. Give it here and I'll have that drum in bits before the bread's eaten."

"Show us," said the older quarryman, and handed him the sledge and stood back. Felix got it up level with his collarbone, and it took him round in half a circle and struck the stones a hand's breadth from his own foot. The drum never felt it.

"It's an old drum," said Felix, handing back the sledge. "It's gone rotten in the middle. A blind man could tell that."

By the hot hour Valens was home; by mid-afternoon he was asleep on the roof.

I climbed up with the water jug and found him under the fig. The tiles were too hot for a bare foot. Under us the town lay shut behind its shutters, with nothing in the lanes but a dog going from one shade to the next. The fig had grown since the ship; its leaves were wider now than my spread hand. He had stretched himself along under the parapet with his head and chest in its shade and the rest of him in the full glare, his bare soles gray with the forum. The shadow of a leaf lay over his open lips and stirred when the leaf stirred. By his hip on the tiles lay a small heap of the summer's figs, hard little green things he had pinched off that morning before the mules went out.

I went below for the Pentax and climbed back with it.

He slept on. Through the lens I had the jar on the left and his gray soles on the right, and the leaves above his face; the heat rising from the tiles made all of it waver, so I held still for it to settle and it never did. I took it, and wound on, and he slept through both.

He woke when the shade had slid off his face, and found me sitting up by the parapet with the camera in my lap.

"Did you spend one on me?"

"One."

"On a man asleep."

"And a tree."

He lay gazing into the fig overhead, at the undersides of its leaves with the sun showing through them, till it seemed he might sleep again.

"She wants water," he said. He fetched it himself, from our jar below.

Supper was beans again and they were cold and we ate them standing at the pot. Felix came upon the little heap of green figs by the parapet and pitched one at the baker's roof opposite, and missed.

"Pulling them off, are you? Are they bad? Grata's cousin sells figs at the gate a copper the double handful, and his are no bigger. Will you pull the next lot too?"

"These would never ripen." Valens edged the jar west along the parapet by its rim, into the sun's last edge. "Last year she carried them all summer and dropped every one in the autumn. Without them she makes wood."

"What's she for?"

"Next year."

Grata was hollering at the parapet from her counter.

"Your tree's dropping its leaves in my wine, I've had three out of the jars today, and I'll charge him for them by the leaf, you tell him."

Valens glanced over at the fig, which had not dropped a leaf since it budded.

"It hasn't," he said.

"He says it has not," I called over.

"It's the wind does it, and I'll charge the wind."

Once Felix was off to see to the dun and the black, Valens brought a tablet out of his pouch and wrote in it with the lamp by his elbow, slowly, the stylus held low. He closed the leaves and tied them shut.

"For Plautia," he said. "Where we are."

"Will the wine-woman send it on from the board?"

"Most months a ship goes over to Lechaion. She'll put it aboard one for a copper, and keep the copper if there's no ship."

He sent it to Puteoli with a carter bound there for lime on market day.

When the lamp was out and the tiles had given back most of the day's heat, he spoke again.

"Do you still walk out to your gate?"

"On the Kalends."

"And it's the same."

"It is the same."

Near the theater a dog barked, and another answered it. His hand found my hip in the dark and lay there.

* * *

That autumn grapes rolled into town on carts that dripped; the streets smelled of must and wasps.

One night of the vintage the bed walked an inch on its legs and the lamp hook rattled on the wall, and next door Grata's cups chimed together on their shelf. I lit the wick, dug Fenn's notebook from beneath my spare tunic in the bag, and on the first of his blank leaves at the back I wrote the date and *small shake, lamps, nobody woke*, and put the light out.

A bone-turner who lived one street over brought me his money box in the week the must was put up in jars. It was a squat clay pot the shape of a little beehive, with a slot in its top for coins, and it lay in four pieces in the turner's cap.

"I broke it to get at what was in it," the turner said. "They're made for that. Only now the money's gone, and I've a mind to start again in the same one. There's no luck in a new box. This one's been full twice."

He was a small man with shavings of bone in the hair of his arms. Through all our talk his cap stayed cupped under the pieces, for fear one of them should fall again.

"I can make it keep coins," I said. "Whether it fills is your affair."

"Twice," he repeated. "Once for my wife's mother's funeral. Once for a horse that died."

I joined it for him next day and left the slot as it was. When he came for it he ran a fingertip round the slot and was satisfied.

"I've no money. That's the trouble with mending a money box." He grinned at me. "But I've this." He felt in his belt and produced a finger-long rod of bone, polished, with a point to it, and carved at its other end into a round spoon smaller than half a pea. "Teeth this end. Ears the other. Don't mix them."

The point went straight into my mouth.

Two years, near enough, I had cleaned my teeth with a chewed twig of lentisk and my own tongue. Every night I fell asleep running that tongue over the places behind, where the stick never got, and they were waiting for my tongue at dawn. The bone went into every place there was. It found the gap at the two molars that had held a filling since sixth grade, and slid along behind the bottom front teeth, where Dr. Pinsky's hooked pick had scraped every spring and fall. I stood in the shop doorway with my back to the street and worked my way round my whole mouth, tooth by tooth, while the carts went by behind me.

Felix found me at it when he came in for the string's waterskins.

"Bone, is it? Did he turn it himself? Can I have a go after you?"

"When you have one of your own."

That night, on the tiles, I worked at my back teeth until dark, and then passed the little rod across to Valens.

"Which end is for the ears?"

"The spoon."

"Then I'll keep to the other." He worked at one of his back teeth with the point, frowning at the town, and returned it.

Grata caught me at it from her counter the following morning.

"You'll have the street thinking it's a religion, you at your teeth in the door every morning with all Pompeii passing. The fuller's wife asked me was it a vow, and I'd no cause to tell her different. I told her you'd promised your teeth to some god of the Greeks, and you've to scrape them for him every morning until he comes for them." She slid a cup over to me. "She's at her own with a pin now, I saw her at it on her step. That's my husband's cousin's, off his vines on the Nola road, it's thin, but it's this year's."

"It is not a vow."

"It is now. I've told her so. You'll do me a board too, while you're at the soot. You've the hand for it, everybody's seen, and the painters want wine by the letter and more for the ladder. Put that Grata says. Put that a copper buys you a drink here, and two buys you better, and four buys you Falernian, and put Falernian big, because it is, or next door to it, it comes from that side of the bay."

I lettered it for her that afternoon on the pier at the end of her counter, standing on her upturned water bucket.

*Grata says: a copper here and you drink. Give two and you'll drink better. Four, and it's Falernian.*

She had a carter read it back to her before she would pay me.

"Four, and it's Falernian," the carter finished, and squinted along her jar mouths. "Which of these is that, then?"

"The one you'll pay four for. The price tells you which."

She paid me for the soot, which was her own, in wine.

On the doorpost of the fullery at the corner, among the scratched tallies of who owed for what, a better hand than most had put one line.

*I sing of fullers and their owl, not of arms and the man.*

It was the first line of the *Aeneid* with the hero taken out and the laundry put in. I needed two readings to see it, and grinned at the paving all the road back.

The fuller's wife at the corner came in the week after with a scent bottle of blue glass wrapped in a tuft of wool, in three pieces and a scatter of chips.

"He threw it at me," she said, "so I threw it back, and he ducked, which he never does. It was his mother's. He'll be sorry within the week and be looking for it on its shelf. Make it so he can't tell. I want him looking at that shelf for a year thinking it never broke and wondering what it is he's sorry for."

"Glass is hard to hide. I can come close."

"Come close, then. And not a word to Grata, or she'll be telling it at every counter by noon."

The glass would not have stood the heat, so I mended it cold, with the resin thinned until it ran like oil; the bottle's own blue did half my work. I laid the chips into their places with a wetted fingertip, singly, and when I had done none of it could be seen unless the sun came in sideways and found a line. She came for it while Grata was at the baths, and stood in the opening holding it to the daylight, looking for the break and not finding it.

"There's his mother's bottle," she said, "that nobody ever threw at anybody." She paid me, and wrapped it up again in the same wool.

A month later she was ahead of me at the fountain, telling the line.

"Twice he's had it from the shelf to look at, and back it goes. Yesterday he brought me home a comb, and couldn't say what for."

Grata was in the line.

Felix bought us a bowl at last at the vintage fair, with his own coppers, a brown one with a hare painted chasing itself round the inside.

"Break that, anybody, and see what you get. Nobody touches it but me. I carry it up and I carry it down. I've never dropped a thing in my life I meant to hold."

The turner's box came to me once more before the year turned cold, in four pieces again.

"Full again in under a month," the turner said. "Don't tell my wife it was full." He paid that time in dice.

When the rains set in there was no carting on the bad days, and Valens spent them in the shop with me out of the weather, mending the string's tack on my plank beside me. Water ran down our street round its stepping stones. Customers came in dripping and stood about after their business was done. One fellow had fetched his lamp at noon. "I'll wait till it eases," he told us, and at dusk he was still in the opening. Felix came in, and left, and came in, too wet to be any use and too restless to stay dry.

"The fig," I said, one afternoon of that. "Up there in this."

"She's wrapped." Valens drew a stitch tight through a girth with the thread's tail in his teeth. "Sacking round the jar, and round her stem to the first fork. She doesn't mind the wet. It's the cold that creeps over at night she minds."

"You did that in the dark."

"She can't climb down to me."

Felix, at the opening, watching the rain go past: "He went up twice. Once to wrap her and once to see had he wrapped her right. I heard him on the stair. I hear everything in this house; I could tell you what the both of you eat in your sleep."

That night the rain found a cracked tile over the bed and came through onto Valens's foot. He moved the foot, and the drip moved after it, or seemed to. He rose in the dark and dragged the whole bed toward the wall, scraping, and stretched out again in the dry. The drip went on landing where his foot had been.

* * *

*Gaius Iulius Polybius for aedile, all of you. He brings good bread.*

It appeared on the red house past the temple crossing in a single night at the end of the winter, when every house front was breaking out in candidates. Felix had me out of bed for it.

"They're painting the red house," he whispered at the door to our room, so loudly Grata could have heard. "Three of them, with a ladder and a light. Come and tell me what it says. Grata reckons it's Polybius paying for every wall from here to the gate."

I followed him out with my cloak over my sleeping tunic. A boy waited at a ladder's foot, holding a lamp up on a pole. On the ladder a man with a pail of lime and a broad brush was laying a panel of fresh white over the house, from the eaves down to the height of a tall man's chin. He took the cousin's pink stripe in two strokes of the brush. It went under the white; in the lamplight the whole wall looked paler for it, like a face just shaved of its beard.

On the curb a thin fellow with red to the wrists sat with his pots in a row, eating bread and waiting for the lime to dry enough for lettering.

"Who are you painting?" said Felix. "What does he pay you? Will you paint a fighter's name, if I bring you one?"

"Polybius," said the painter, chewing. "Enough. No."

He dried his fingers on his tunic and climbed, while the whitewasher came off. "You, lantern, hold that light still or I'll paint *you* on the wall."

"Four more walls before the cock," the whitewasher grumbled, wringing his brush out on the stones. "Four, and my wife thinks I'm at the bakery kneading, and Polybius pays at the month's end, and last month he paid us in loaves."

"Good loaves," the painter called down.

"Chalk," said the whitewasher. "Ask anybody."

Felix sat among the painter's pots and tipped his face up to the ladder. The lantern boy's light sagged by degrees until he was told about it, and then it rose. The painter worked downward, filling his brush from a pot hooked on the rung beside him. I stayed in the road in my cloak till my soles were numb, reading it out to Felix as the words arrived.

"His bread comes last," I told him, when the painter got down to it. "It says it is good."

"All that red, and a ladder, and three of them out of their beds, and me out of mine half the night. For bread."

With the last letter on, the painter climbed off and wiped his brush and scratched something small on the lime under the panel with the point of a nail, for the light to show him.

"What's he putting?" Felix asked me. "Is he putting the fighter? He said he wouldn't. He's putting it, isn't he, he's putting a fighter after all."

"Come and read it in the morning," the painter said, and shouldered the ladder, and the three of them carried their pots and their light off to the next wall.

At daybreak I found this under the panel, small and scratched rather than painted:

*Infantio wrote it. Lantern-boy, hold the ladder.*

By daylight the panel glared white and its letters glared red. Where the cousin's stripe had run down the house there stood a faint ridge under the lime. The painter had found it in the dark. He had set the upright of the I in Iulius straight down it, so that the letter stood exactly on the crack from the eaves to the lowest stroke.

"Which one's the crack?" Felix wanted to be told, when I had read it him again.

I showed him.

"Polybius is standing on it," Felix said, delighted. "Right on top of it, the whole height of him, and he doesn't know."

Valens came by us with the string on his way to the yard, and Felix caught at his arm.

"Can you read the new one? You can't, can you, only she can, in this house."

Valens studied the panel with the dun's halter in his fist, down to the last red letter.

"It's red," he said, and led the dun on.

Grata had the panel read to her by her son on his way to bathe, before her shutters were down.

"Polybius," she told me. "His loaves are half chalk, and his grandfather swept out somebody's kitchen in Rome, and he'll get it, they always get it who paint first and pay the painters' wine. He'll have every wall from this corner to the basilica by the Ides, and the plasterers will be at their wits' end." She put her hand on my forearm and gave it a little push, the way she moved customers along her counter. "Go on with the boy, he's waiting on you, he's been on that curb since the cock."

From there Felix took me to the fountain, to read him the pier at its back, which was scratched over as high as an arm could stretch.

"No candidates on this pier. It's people wrote all this."

*Successus the weaver loves the innkeeper's slave girl, Iris, and she does not care for him at all.*

Under it, in a different hand, deeper:

*Jealous, are you? Don't come pushing in where there's a better-looking man.*

"Which one's the better-looking man? Which inn? Is Iris the one with the bucket by the Sarno Gate?"

"The wall stops there."

"Successus can keep his weaving." Felix ran his eye up the pier, over all the scratched things he could not read, much pleased with them. "I'd have her off the pair of them by the Ides, if I put my mind to it, and I'd never touch a loom."

Highest of all on the pier, older than everything round it, its letters gone soft at their edges, there was one more.

*I'm amazed, wall, that you haven't come down in a heap, with all the drivel you've had to carry.*

"Who wrote that? What's drivel? Does it mean us?"

"It means everybody."

"That's wrong." Felix slapped the pier with his open hand. "It's been standing since before any of us was born, Grata told me, and it's the straightest pier on the street."

* * *

The fountain at our corner was a stone trough fed through the bull's mouth on its back slab. The bull ran water day and night, over the trough's lower lip and away along the gutter. At the spout end, where people leaned a hand to drink, the rim had been worn to a smooth dark dip. The bull's left horn had lost a chip, years before; the broken place had gone as dark and smooth as the rest of him. The water struck the trough with one unchanging slap; a week in that street had taught my ears to leave it out. A dog stood in the gutter below with the overflow running round its feet, drinking.

One morning in the summer I was there with our jar, in the line of women and slaves, waiting my turn. The line was talking about a wedding.

"Fourteen," said a woman with a red jar, "and him forty."

"Forty-two, he says. He's been forty for three years."

"With the dowry in figs."

"In fig trees. That's different. You can't eat a tree."

"You can sell one."

"Who'd buy a fig tree off a man of forty-two?"

"Another man of forty-two."

"You can sell anything, if you've a face for it," said a slave girl at the spout.

"Fill and get on," the woman with the red jar told her, "and leave the talk to them that's got dowries."

A child crawled round the trough's corner through the overflow. He was a year old or not much over, naked but for a crescent of leather hung on a cord at his throat, and wet to the chest. He grabbed the worn place in the rim and pulled himself up on it and stood, rocking. In his fist, raised to me, he had a pebble.

I put out my hand. He laid the pebble in my palm, small and black and round, wet, and cold from the trough's bottom. Then he kept two of his fingers on it, after it was in my palm, until he was sure it would stay.

I was crying.

The jar was on my hip and the stone was in my hand. The crying heaved up through me. My mouth was open. I could not shut it. Water from the jar's lip ran down my side and my leg and into my sandal, and I could not right the jar, because my hands were full. The next woman in line reached round me and pushed her jar in under the bull's mouth.

His father came after him round the trough, a man whose hands were stained blue from somebody's vats.

"I never touched her," he told the line. "You saw. I never came near her." He hoisted the boy onto his hip. "He gives them to everybody, lady. He gave one to a mule this morning. It's only a pebble, that's all he does, he gives pebbles."

From his father's hip the boy watched me. I could not stop.

"I was at the bakery door all along," his father told a woman with a basket, and took the boy off with him. "Everybody saw where I was."

It ended when it ended. The jar was half empty by then, and I filled it again at the spout and carried it home.

Grata was at her counter with a customer, a man buying oil and disputing the measure. She looked once at my face and reached down a cup, filled it and set it before me on the counter, and went on telling the man about his measure. The stone in my other hand had dried by then. It was gray, and not black at all.

"You'll get it no cheaper in Nuceria," she was telling him. "Suppose you do, you'll have walked to Nuceria for it, and walked home again, and that's two days' wages gone on your feet, and your sandal's through at the toe already, I've been looking at it all this while."

"Grata tells me you cried at the fountain," said Valens that night on the roof.

"A child gave me a stone."

He waited, in case more came. When none did he held out his hand, and I put the stone into it. He looked at it a while by the lamp, small and dry and gray, a stone like any other in any gutter in the town, and passed it back to me, and took up his supper.

"That's a good stone," he said.

*The Greek woman at the cobbler's mended my jug, and it holds. Take her yours.*

It turned up scratched into the lime under my black letters in the month they picked the olives, small and slanting, overnight. Felix had me read it to him daily for a week.

"Read me the one about you," he would say at the pier, with the waterskins slung on him, and stand mouthing it after me.

"Who wrote it? The jug woman? I'll find her out." He rubbed a smut off the lime beside it with his sleeve. "There's a name scratched on every wall in this town but mine."

The stoker from the baths at the crossing came in that same month, black to the elbows and smelling of the olive stones they burned under the floors. He had a lamp in three pieces in a fold of his tunic, and he laid them out on my plank with one black finger, as carefully as a gambler laying out dice.

"The furnace stair did it," he said. "Third step up. It's always the third. The stone's gone hollow in the middle, and it's dark down there as a cow's belly."

It was a plain round lamp with FORTIS stamped in tall capitals on its foot. It had broken across the shoulder in a crooked line, and a wedge of the rim had broken loose beside it.

"The potters' row," I said, "sells this lamp by the basket."

"I could buy forty." He jerked his head toward the baths. "There's a shelf of them down there, all off the one mold, all with that same name on their bottoms, and whichever of the boys gets down there first in the morning takes the nearest. Hermes takes mine. He swears he doesn't. This one's mine. I've had it three summers."

"I could put it together so the break would not show."

"And then how do I know it from the other thirty-nine?" He scratched in his beard with a black finger. "Leave me the crack. Leave it where the boys can see it's spoken for. It's dark on that stair, and I'll want to find it with my thumb."

"What name goes on the tag?"

"Zosimus. Of the crossing baths. Down at the furnace, not up with the bathers." He was gone again, telling a man with a handcart that he'd have Hermes's ear for him yet.

Round the filling hole the clay was dark and glossy from his thumb, pressed there each dawn for years to tip the oil in. The broken edges were the only clean clay on it. They were pale and dry and sharp, and they smelled of nothing. When I brought the two big pieces together they met at once, the break so new that nothing had yet got into it.

I warmed the pieces and the resin, drew the bead along the broken shoulder with the cane and brought the faces home, and the brown welt swelled from the break. Then I let it stand. Along the shoulder I trimmed it to a thread's thickness proud of the clay. I warmed the knife and stroked the top of the welt smooth so it would not catch his skin. The wedge from the rim went in after. When all of it had set I took a pinch of Grata's brazier soot and rubbed it into the join with my fingertip, until the break ran black across the pale clay from the oil hole to the rim and on across the wedge.

Valens got in from the mules at dusk, while the lamp was still on my plank with the soot fresh in its line. He halted before it. He set his thumb on the black line at its start and ran it along the welt to the rim, slowly, and back.

"Who's this for?"

"The stoker from the baths at the crossing."

"Left like that."

"He wants to find it in the dark."

He left the lamp standing and went to wash.

Zosimus came for it two mornings later, on his way down to light the furnaces. It was still dim in the shop. He cradled the lamp in his palm and shut his eyes, and his thumb travelled the shoulder and stopped on the welt. It went back over it, and stopped again.

"That's it," he said, with his eyes still shut.

"Keep it off the furnace mouth," I said. "The join will soften in a great heat."

"It's never been near the furnace mouth." He opened his eyes, offended for the lamp. "It lights the stair and it lives on the shelf. Hermes's goes in the fire. He dropped it in the fire twice, the fool, and raked it out, and now it's black, and he thinks it's mine."

He paid me, and put the lamp back in the fold of his tunic, and was away to the baths with his palm over it.

The fig above us had kept its fruit that year, and it ripened late, in that same month of the olives, when every other fig in the town had been eaten or dried.

Valens knew the day. Each morning he climbed up and took one in his fingers and weighed it and left it, and on the morning it came away into his hand by its own weight, with its neck gone soft and a bead of something sweet standing at the eye of it, he brought it down to the shop with two more and put them on my plank among the broken things.

"Today," he said. "Not tomorrow. Tomorrow the wasps have them."

I ate all three. They were warm from the roof, and I wanted nothing else that whole day.

Grata had opened a new jar of fish sauce that month. It stood at her counter's brazier end with a ladle on its lip, and every morning, once it was open, the smell rolled over the paving, salt and rot and something under both. I had taken to going round the long block to the fountain so as not to pass it. The jar had turned, I decided. All that week Grata watched me take our jar off the roundabout way, and never spoke of it to me. To whoever was at her counter she talked about oil, or about the scent bottle at the fullery.

"Blue glass, his mother's, and he's looking at that shelf yet and can't think why, and she reckons I had it from her at the fountain. I had it the hour it broke. The street heard it go, every door of it."

When the week was out I sat one evening on the bench before her counter, over a cup of her hot wine I had not drunk, and she lifted the lid to ladle some for a carter, and my hand went over my mouth.

"That jar has gone off," I said, behind my hand.

The carter sniffed toward the jar over his cup. "That fish of yours would turn a mule," he told Grata, and put his cup out for more of it.

Grata clapped the lid on. She put her arm over the counter and rested two fingertips on the inside of my wrist, beside Plautia's gold, and kept them there while she talked.

"My fish is the same fish it was at the Kalends, and you ate it then and wiped the bowl out after with your bread. Three mornings you've gone as green as a leek at the smell of it, and you've the look about the eyes my sister had with each of hers, and you've not drunk my wine tonight, and you've not told him either, or he'd be standing in my door grinning at nothing the way husbands do. It'll come with the heat. You'll curse that stair every day of the last month, and him along with it, and he'll be no use to you, none of them are. It's not my fish. It's a child you've got."
