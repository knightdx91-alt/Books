# Chapter 2: Fenn's Notebook

*Thanksgiving, 1967, and Margaret has decided that a scholiast is some kind of foot doctor. I explained it to her twice over the turkey. A scholiast is a monk with a pen and too much time on his hands, and when he's copying out some old Greek book and knows a thing the book doesn't, he writes it in the margin, and eight hundred years later a German prints it in little type at the bottom of the page, and then nobody cuts the pages of the German's book until August Fenn walks into the big new library at the university and does it with his thumb. The girl at the desk looked at me like I came about the radiators. Father Kostas at St. George's put the Greek into English for me for the price of a roast pork sandwich at the Terminal, and here it is the way he gave it: "The books of the Ptolemies were carried out of Alexandria by ship and taken up to the cave of the nymphs on Parnassus, and they are there." Are there. Present tense. The Library of Alexandria, Margaret, the whole works, sitting in a cave on a mountain. She asked if they'd be wet.*

*The chart says the cave is right here, and those Venetians knew their business: 38° 31′ N, 22° 32′ E. I put it on in ballpoint so I'd have it. Margaret said if I was going to write on something that old I had to at least put a question mark, to be honest about it, so I put one, to keep the peace on a holiday. All I need now is about two thousand bucks and a better flashlight.*

I read that in my father's kitchen the night I brought it home, under the overhead light, with the answering machine blinking on the counter over the empty well where his greeting had been. Fenn wrote in block capitals nearly a quarter of an inch high and retraced every stroke. He bore down so hard that every page carried a second copy of itself on the back, raised and backward, like the dots beside an elevator button; in the bowls of his R's the ballpoint had furrowed the paper and left a black bead of ink at the bottom of each furrow. It was the hand of a man who did not expect to be argued with, or who expected it and liked his chances.

I had read a good many scholia in graduate school. Most of them were a cold monk telling the page it had got Homer wrong; the one I remembered best was a complaint about the ink. A note that put the Ptolemies' books in a cave on Parnassus was exactly what a monk wrote on a slow afternoon to feel nearer to something, and I did not believe it. The coordinates were another matter. They sat in the middle of the page as plainly as a street address. When I slid the chart out of its garbage bag and lifted the top board, the blue ballpoint above *Castri* said it too, to the minute.

I put the notebook back on its bed of tobacco crumbs and shut the lid. After that I read it in rations, an entry a night and two when the day had been bad, though nobody had told me to make it last.

* * *

On Monday I was back at work. The lamp was where I had left it the Friday before the memorial, face down in a tray of sand under the magnifier, with its nozzle beside it in a nest of tissue. It was a mold-made lamp from North Africa, second century, the clay slipped a shade darker than its body; on its discus a hare sat up on its haunches eating a bunch of grapes. Somebody in the museum's storerooms had knocked the nozzle off in about 1935 and stuck it back with shellac, in a hurry and a degree out of true; the shellac had gone orange and crazed like varnish on an old door. I had used up most of February taking that man's work off with acetone poultices and a sharpened stick. What was left was a clean break, two faces of fired clay that had last been one piece under a Roman roof. On the tip of the nozzle was a crust of soot from the last time somebody filled the thing with oil and lit it, and I had not let anybody near the soot.

The lab was cold, it was always cold, it was a basement. I brushed a bead of acrylic resin along one face of the break with a brush that had about three hairs left in it. Then I set the nozzle home and held it under the hood with my elbows on the bench, while the solvent went off into the fan and my fingers began to ache from the wrist down.

When it had set I switched off the overhead light and put the raking lamp on it, low and from the side, the light that found everything. There was a step at the underside of the join no thicker than a fingernail paring. Under the raking light it threw a shadow you could have parked a car in.

So I took it apart. The resin let go under acetone in about an hour, which was the whole virtue of the stuff, and I cleaned both faces again and started over. The second time I held the nozzle longer and did not look at the clock. When the raking lamp went on, the seam had gone. The hare sat on its discus eating, the nozzle ran out from the body in one unbroken line, and when I drew my thumbnail across the place where the break had been it did not catch. I tried it again with my eyes shut. Then I sat back on the stool and looked at that lamp for longer than I needed to, which in my trade was most of what they paid you for. Nobody came in to see it.

At lunch I took half a sandwich up to the stacks of the university library, where food was not allowed. The German was on a bottom shelf in a run of tall volumes in marbled boards, Leipzig, 1838, the spines flaking like pastry. I sat on the floor and opened it across my knees. Two rows over, a boy in a hooded sweatshirt was eating sunflower seeds and spitting the shells into a Wawa cup. The line was at the foot of a page about Phocis, in small Greek type under a rule, and it said what Father Kostas had said it said. Somebody had slit the leaves with something blunt, the edges furred and torn, from the front of the volume up to that page. The leaf after it was still joined at the top; so was every leaf to the end of the book. He had found his line and stopped reading.

I did not cut the rest. I put the volume back where it lived and went down to my lamp with the crust of the sandwich in my pocket.

That night's ration was February 1968, and the capitals were still a quarter of an inch high.

*Sold the Plymouth to a mailman in Jenkintown for six hundred, and he threw in a sack of oranges, which I took. Athens on the second of April, TWA. Margaret won't tell the girls at church I've gone to Greece to dig up a library, she says they'll all pray for me and she'd never live it down, so I'm going to Mexico instead. Never been to Mexico. Maybe after.*

* * *

The watch slid down over my knuckle every time I lifted my hand, and on Thursday after work I took it to a jeweler on Sansom Street. He had a telephone clamped between his ear and his shoulder when I came in and did not put it down for the whole of my visit. He took the watch without a word, screwed a loupe into his eye, and began pushing pins out of the band with a little steel punch while he told the telephone that a ring was not a ring until somebody paid for it. He measured my wrist with a strip of paper. Three links went into a manila envelope the size of a stamp, which he sealed with a lick and slid over the counter.

"Eight dollars," he said, to me or to the telephone.

I had my mouth open to say fine. Nothing came out of it. My throat had shut on the word, and the harder I pushed at it the less there was, so that I stood at his counter with my wallet open and my eyes going hot over eight dollars. I put a ten on the glass. He made change one-handed out of a drawer without looking and pushed it toward me, still talking about the ring. I picked up the envelope, left the change, and went out onto Sansom Street with the watch sitting snug against my wristbone for the first time.

*Delphi, at last, and it isn't even the real Delphi, they picked up the whole village and moved it down the road so the French could dig. Room over a taverna. The owner's mother feeds me beans, and there's a goat that eats whatever I leave on the sill, including half a postcard. Walked up to the plateau and the cave today, four hours up and every one of them uphill, and it's a big wet barn of a place with names scratched all over the walls, a lot of them French. No books. Nobody finds the books on the first day.*

The capitals stood upright through April. In May they began to come apart. First the letters shrank. Then they started to join, the R's running into the E's along the line, and by the middle of the month he was writing in a small lowercase hand that leaned to the right and did not trouble to close its loops. Since the first night I had turned every page over to read the copy his pen had pressed through from the front. When I turned these over, the backs were smooth.

*Way in past where the good light gives out, the rock comes down low on the left and there's a kind of slot you could get your arm into. I put my hand in at the back and the air was cold in a way air isn't. I know cave cold, I was down in the sinkholes in Florida with the Navy, and this was something else, this was the icebox at midnight with the light off. It came for a minute or so and then it quit, like somebody shut a door in another part of the house.*

*Cold again Tuesday morning. Nothing Wednesday, nothing since. The boy with the goats brought me bread and cheese and stood there and watched every bite go down, and then took the cloth home to his mother.*

*Slept up there. Didn't help.*

Caves breathed. I had read it somewhere, in a book about limestone: the air inside went in and out with the weather, slow as a sleeper. I said so to the kitchen, and turned the page.

* * *

He rang on the last Saturday in March, a little after ten in the morning, while I was standing at my father's sink eating cereal out of the box because the bowls were all in the dish rack and I had not decided whose they were. The wall phone had a coiled cord that years of walking off with it had pulled straight in places.

"Ms. Reyes? Good. A real person. It's Halloran, in Naples. I've rung four times this week, and each time your machine gives me its little tone and then nothing at all, not so much as a hello. I'd begun to think I had the wrong house."

"It's the right house."

"You'll have to forgive the echo. We're leaving the flat, my wife and I, after thirty-one years, and everything's gone to the new place but the telephone, which has to stay until the new people take over the line. So I'm sitting on the floor of an empty room with the thing in my lap." He cleared his throat. "You can hear yourself think in here. It isn't as nice as they say. I'm sorry, you don't want any of that. I'm ringing about the permit."

"I got your message."

"You'll know the worst of it already. The Soprintendenza have been very good, really. They'll accept me as director for the season, that much is agreed. What they require is the cession. That's the form by which the outgoing director gives the permit up to the incoming one, and it wants the outgoing director's signature at the bottom, in ink, witnessed."

"Witnessed by whom?"

"Oh, a notary, a consul. That part was never the trouble. I explained the situation. I sent them everything, the certificate from Pennsylvania with its stamp, a sworn translation, a letter from me in my best Italian. It all came back on Thursday." The line hissed across the ocean. "They'd written over it in red. *Firma mancante.* Signature missing."

I laughed. It rose out of me before I had any say in it, a bark first and then a long helpless run that bent me over the sink. I slid down the cabinet door until I was sitting on the linoleum with the cord pulled taut over my head and the receiver jammed against my ear, laughing so hard that no sound came, only air. There was a clerk in Naples with a red pen, waiting at his desk for my father to come in and sign. There was my father, who had never in his life returned a form on time, being sent one more he would not answer. That set me off again. The cereal box had tipped over beside me and was pouring itself out across the floor.

"Ms. Reyes?" Halloran said, a long way off. "Is that—are you crying? I'm so sorry. I'm an old fool with forms."

"I'm laughing."

"Oh." There was a pause with the empty room in it. "Oh, thank God. I couldn't tell which."

When I could breathe again I wiped my eyes on the dish towel, and he told me what they would take instead: the heir's signature on a declaration, before a notary, with the certificate attached again. He would fax it to the museum on Monday. The twentieth was a Monday too, he said, and the Soprintendenza did not work Mondays in any sense that mattered, so if I could manage it by the fifteenth he would walk it over himself.

"And his notebooks," he said. "From last season. The north transects exist nowhere else, I'm afraid. He never got round to typing them up. He never got round to typing anything, did he. If they're in the house."

"They're here."

"Perhaps, when you've a moment. By the end of June, if it could be managed." The floor creaked under him, or the line did. "There's no hurry at all."

I picked a flake of cereal off the linoleum and ate it.

"There's one other thing," he said. "I meant to put it in a letter, and then it seemed a thing you couldn't put in a letter. The season starts on the sixth of July. Three weeks, four. There's a room in the dig house, a decent one with a window, and nobody's been given it. You'd be very welcome. In Emilio's place, I mean. Not to work, unless you wanted to. Only so that somebody—" He stopped. "Well. So that you could see it."

"I might be in Greece."

"Greece!" He was pleased for me, the ordinary way people were pleased about somebody else's vacation. "Sure, that's practically next door. Think about it. You needn't say anything now." Behind him a door opened and a man's voice said something cheerful in Italian, and Halloran answered it in Italian with his hand half over the mouthpiece, not cheerfully. "The new tenants," he said. "They've come to measure for curtains, and I'm still sitting here. I'll fax on Monday. Goodbye, Ms. Reyes. I'm glad you laughed."

When the line went I sat on the floor a while longer with the receiver in my lap. Then I found the broom in the cellar way and swept up the cereal.

* * *

The form came through the lab's fax machine on Monday in a long warm curl. I signed it on Wednesday in front of a notary at a check-cashing place on Baltimore Avenue; she stamped it without reading a word and asked if I wanted a lottery ticket while I was there. I faxed it to Naples from the lab that afternoon, a page at a time, the machine pulling each sheet in with a small sound of disapproval.

The chart went up on the first Saturday in April. In the lab I had cut it a window mat from four-ply board and hinged it in with tissue and wheat starch paste, and I carried it to the house flat in a portfolio and pinned the mat board, not the chart, to the strip of cork over my father's desk, where his site plans used to hang until their corners curled. The coast of Phocis hung over his blotter with its six perforations, or Mrs. Szabo's seven, and Fenn's numbers in blue on the shoulder of Parnassus.

His study was the back bedroom; it was the only room in the house he had ever kept in order. His field notebooks took up the bottom shelf of the bookcase, decades of them in yellow surveyor's covers gone brown at the spine where his thumb had opened them, each with a site and a year on it in his writing. He wrote small and fast, leaning forward, like a man running for a bus. I knelt and ran my thumbnail along the spines from one end of the shelf to the other, each binding ticking under it in turn, and stood up again. I did not take one down. On the Saturdays after that I came to sit in his chair and read Fenn's notebook in the room with the chart, and I did not take one down then either.

The June entries were short, some of them a single line, and the hand had grown so small that I brought the magnifier home from the lab to read them.

*Up again. Nothing. My boots are going at the toes, and the boy's mother has a cousin who'll stitch them, she says, if I quit going up the mountain long enough to take them off.*

*Tired today.*

* * *

I flossed in my own apartment, which by May I was using for very little else. The bathroom there had a medicine-cabinet mirror with the silvering gone at one corner and a light over it that buzzed. On the first Tuesday in May, a little before midnight, I was working the floss down behind the last molar on the upper left, where an old filling had a lip that caught the thread and frayed it, when I stopped with the floss wound around both middle fingers and still in my teeth. I went out to the kitchen like that. I found the phone book on top of the refrigerator and turned to the airlines with my hands tethered to my own mouth, and I dialed Olympic Airways, whose number began with eight hundred. A woman in New York answered at once.

"Athens," I said, and then I took the floss out.

She had a seat from Kennedy on Tuesday the nineteenth. I gave her my credit card number standing in my nightgown in the kitchen, and when I had hung up I finished the upper left at the bathroom mirror and did the lowers properly, since the floss was out anyway.

The bag lived in my hall closet, half packed, as it had in every apartment since college: a change of clothes and a toothbrush in a canvas duffel, kept against nothing in particular. That same night the floss went into its outside pocket, before there was a ticket in the house or a passport anywhere near the bag. The passport was at the back of a dresser drawer under the warranty for a toaster I no longer owned. I did not go looking for it until the week I left.

*A fellow off the Athens bus left a Herald Tribune on my table, a week old, and I read every word of it, the bridge column too, and I don't play bridge.*

Gloria rang my father's house the Saturday after that, because I was always there on Saturdays now and she had worked it out.

"Pat says you're going to Greece."

"I asked Pat to keep an eye on the house."

"So it's true." Something behind her clanked and settled, like a garage door. "I'm in the garage on the cordless. Don't ask. Who's doing the mail? Not Pat. Pat opens things."

"I'll put a hold on it at the post office."

"And somebody has to pick the circulars up off the porch, or the whole street knows nobody's home, and Dorothy's dish is still in that refrigerator, I saw it." She breathed out through her nose. "How long?"

"Till the ninth of June."

"Leave the key in the flowerpot, then. Not under the mat, everybody looks under the mat." The garage door, or whatever it was, settled again. "And call me when you get there. Collect. Ray'll have a fit, let him."

The rest came in whatever order it came. I bought a flashlight because Fenn had wanted a better one. The head of my department signed my leave form without reading where I was going, which was either a kindness or a habit. Two boxes of granola bars were for the plane and then for the mountain, and within a day of going into the duffel they had begun to shed oats into its seams; a bottle of Advil rattled in the side pocket against the floss. The microcassette had been in my jacket since March. I put it inside a gray wool sock, rolled the sock tight, and pushed it down into the toe of a hiking boot at the bottom of the bag. The watch needed no packing; I gave its crown its turns each morning at whatever window I was standing near, and by now I knew where the spring would begin to resist under my thumb.

On the Sunday before the flight I loaded the Pentax on the kitchen table at the house. I pulled the rewind crank up until the back popped open, dropped a new cartridge of Kodak into its chamber, and drew the tongue of film across to the take-up spool, which needed some coaxing before it caught. When the back was shut I wound on and fired at the kitchen ceiling with the cap still on the lens, twice, until the little window over the lever showed 1. There were thirty-six frames in it, and I had not taken one.

Fenn's notebook went in last, on top, where I could get at it. Its back half was blank, sixty-odd ruled leaves he had never reached. The back board had warped worse than the front, and the last leaves had cockled against it and stuck; I had not forced them in March, and I did not force them now. I put the notebook overnight in a sealed bag with a damp blotter, and in the morning the board came away from the endpaper with a sound like a lip unsticking. Inside the back cover was a pocket meant for receipts. In it, folded in three, was a carbon on onionskin from the American consulate in Athens, dated the sixteenth of September, 1968.

*Personal effects of FENN, August J., U.S. citizen: one man's overcoat, wool, gray, with contents of pockets, forwarded to next of kin, Miss Margaret Fenn, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania.*

Whoever typed it at the consulate had put one n too many in Pennsylvania and x'd it out. I folded it back along its own creases and put it where it had been.

* * *

The night before the flight I sat at my father's kitchen table with the duffel by the door and read the last entry again. It was dated in July, in the small leaning hand, and the pen had barely marked the page.

*July 21. Cold this morning, cold enough to see my breath at the slot, and it came and stayed long enough for a whole cigarette, which is the longest yet. Going in tomorrow first thing with the good flashlight and a ball of kite string. The boy's mother washed my shirts and wouldn't take a drachma for it. Back before Labor Day, and Margaret can stop telling the girls at church I'm in Mexico, because I'll be sitting on her front steps with a sunburn and a story she won't believe until she reads it in the Inquirer.*
