"Lupus? By the Twins, it is. Lupus, walking behind mules."

The man had been gaining on us since the bend, two others at his heels, and now he was at Valens's left side as though he had been asked. He was short and sunburned, and the dust of the plain was on the hem of his cloak. He had a pleasant, creased face, and he looked at Valens the way a man looked at a horse he had once had money on.

"You want somebody else." Valens kept walking.

"Do I? Show me your right hand, then."

The hand stayed at Valens's side.

"Corinth. The year the emperor was in Greece. You had a Gaul on his back in the sand in front of the whole colony, and the emperor rose in his box to see it finished. I was on the benches. I had two drachmas on the Gaul." He laughed. "I've forgiven you."

He kept pace beside us with his hands behind his back. "These two never saw you fight. I told them about it the whole crossing. I said, you'll know him by his hand. There's not another hand like it in Achaea." His glance dropped to it, and rose again to Valens's face, and he was still smiling. "And here's the hand."

My own hand was inside my himation, on the camera strap.

Ahead of us the string went on between the terrace walls, six mules with their jars of oil packed in straw. At the front of it Felix held his fist out behind him as he walked, and the goat came after the fist with her neck stretched for the barley in it.

"Go on up to the boy," Valens said to me.

One of the other two had come round on my side. He was big, and he carried a stick of olive wood, and he had no eyes for me. He was watching the back of Valens's right hand.

"My brother's a butcher," he said. "He's got two like that."

I heard the third one behind us. His sandals on the stones.

"And good day to you, lady," the first man said across Valens, to me. The third one laughed, low, at nothing.

"*Rudiarius*." The first man took Valens by the arm.

Valens stopped.

The man's other hand came from under his cloak with a knife in it.

I saw the knife. I saw Valens's hand shut on the wrist above it.

The mule behind us flung her head up and hung back against her rope. Something in her load cracked. Oil came down her flank.

The stick went past my ear. It came down across Valens's shoulders. He went forward into the man with the knife, and the two of them pitched off the edge of the road together, into the ditch below the terrace.

The ditch hid them. I heard them.

My himation came tight across my throat from behind. I was pulled back against somebody. Wine. Sweat.

"Be still, mistress. Be good."

"Mara."

From the ditch. Something struck stone down there, twice.

The strap was round my neck under my clothes. My hand was on it. I dragged at it, and the camera came clear of my clothes, and I twisted round in his grip and swung it at his head.

It caught him above the ear. I felt it in my wrist.

He let go. He walked into the wall, and slid down it.

I had the camera up again by its strap. Nobody came at me.

The cap was off. It went rolling along the road on its rim, small and black, wobbling and righting itself over the ruts with the white letters on its face going under and coming up again, past the mule's back hooves, past the big man's feet, until it met the edge of the oil and tipped over in it, face up.

In the olives a bird was saying the same three notes.

In the ditch somebody was breathing hard, and then nobody was.

The big man was at the edge of the ditch with his stick up.

Valens climbed out with the stick coming down at him. It landed on his forearm, and he kept coming, and the big man dropped it and ran. He went scrambling up the bank among the trees. The one I had hit pushed himself off the stones and went after him, holding his head together in his two hands.

The stick lay in the road. Valens stood over it. There was a knife in his hand, and it was not his own.

I wound the strap round the camera. I wound until there was no strap left, and held the camera against me with both arms.

Somewhere above us the goat was crying. Felix came running back down past the mules with his fist open and nothing in it.

At the road's edge he stopped dead, over the ditch, and looked.

One of the man's feet showed past the lip of the ditch. It was a good sandal, new, with the thong tied in a bow across the foot. The foot was turned out.

Felix went to the wall. He was sick against it, neatly, propped on one arm against the stones. He dragged a forearm across his mouth. Under the dust his face had no color in it at all.

"Who gets his knife?" he said.

Valens came past him to me.

"Are you cut?"

"No."

He looked for blood on me regardless. Then he dropped into the ditch. I saw his back, and his arm working at something low down, and when he shifted his weight I saw the man's face past him, turned up the bank, toward me. It was still a pleasant face. When Valens climbed back up he had a purse on a cut thong. He had left the man everything else.

"Get the string on to the ford," he said to Felix. "Water them. Keep the goat out of the stream."

Felix went.

Valens went to the mule with the cracked jar. Oil still ran from the straw. I went to her head without being told and held her by the headstall while she rolled her eye at me, and he cut the ropes on that side, and the jar came off into his arms, and he heaved it over the terrace edge, down among the olives. The mule stood with her hind legs braced and her ears flat. He laid a palm on her neck beside mine and ran it back along her flank where the jar had broken, and picked a sliver of pot out of the hair there and threw it after the man.

"Somebody will come by," I said.

"This is the god's road." He was looking up the slope into the olives. "Let the god's people find him."

"They will think it was robbers."

"That's what it was. A traveler with his purse cut."

Far down toward the sea, where the olives ended, a smudge of dust was moving, and in the dust a donkey, and people walking at a donkey's pace, coming our way.

"Now," he said, and slapped the hindmost mule on, and she went stiff-legged after the others.

I could not make myself look toward the ditch again, and did not try. I went past where the cap lay in the oil, its letters dark with it, and kept going.

The thong of my left sandal had pulled out of its toe-hole, and the sole slapped the stones each time it landed. The oil had got onto that one. The smell of it rose off my foot, thick and sweet, and my feet carried me on through it, one slapping and one not.

Valens came behind me with the last mule.

The camera was still in my arms.

Before the bend, the noise of water.

* * *

The ford was where a stream came down out of a cleft in the hill and crossed the road under stepping stones. Plane trees grew along it with their buds just broken. Felix had the string in the shallows below the stones, and the goat tied to the last packsaddle, up on the dry, and he was not saying anything to any of them.

Valens climbed the cleft by a goat path, and I followed.

Higher up the cleft the stream poured over a chest-high lip of rock and fell in a rope into a clear pool over pale gravel.

He untied the pouch from his belt and put it into my hands. Through the leather the watch was going.

He held his hands in the fall.

The water broke on them in spray. There was blood over the backs of them, the dead man's, gone brown already, and the water took the top of it and ran pink down his forearms into the pool. He turned his hands over in the fall. He turned them again.

They were shaking.

It started in his shoulders and ran out along his arms, and the water jumped off his fingers with it. He tried to work the blood from round his nails with his thumb, and the thumb kept slipping off the nail. He gave that up and held his hands still under the water, and they would not be still.

He was breathing through his mouth. He shifted round on the stones to put his shoulder between me and the hands, and I saw them anyway.

I let the camera hang on its strap, and put the pouch inside my himation, and waded in. It was snow water, and my feet stopped belonging to me. I took his wrists, one in each hand, and pressed them flat to the stone beside the fall, so that it poured over my hands as well as his.

The shaking went into my arms. Under my right palm his old scar was a raised line, and I could feel in it the marks where each stitch had gone in. I held on. The shaking outlasted anything I had thought a body could do, and I held on through all of it, with the cold climbing my legs and water in my sleeves, and I did not speak.

It stopped by degrees.

He let his forehead come to rest on my hair. He stayed there.

When he lifted his head, I took my hands away. His stayed flat on the stone. He took them down himself. There was a graze along his cheekbone with grit in it. I had missed it on the road, and he plainly knew nothing about it.

I had trodden on a clump of flat leaves at the edge of the pool, and they smelled green and sharp under my heel. "Onions," Valens said. "They hung them under the eaves. In strings, to dry."

He drew the stranger's knife from his belt and washed it in the falling water, running his thumb along the groove to clear it.

I climbed out and sat on a rock with my feet in the sun.

The camera hung against the front of my himation. I looked it over. Somebody's hair was wound into the little crank on top, a few dark curls of it, and a smear lay on the top plate that had been wet and was not any longer. I worked the hair out with a thumbnail and wiped the smear away with the hem of my chiton. The ring at the front of the lens had taken the blow and was pushed in at one point. In the finder the plane trees stood upright and in focus.

I had carried it since the lodgers came. All week men had slept along Kallia's gallery in rows, Thessalians and Achaeans and a priest's cousin from Elateia, and my duffel lay below the stairs among their bundles, and I had heard strangers turn over in the dark a yard from it. The camera was the thing in that bag a thief would take first, and the thing I could least explain to anybody who took it, so each morning it went under my chiton on its strap and rode there till night, knocking me whenever I hoisted water. Its cap was still in the oil by the ditch, where I meant it to stay.

"Is it broken?" he said, still facing the rock.

"Only the rim is bent."

He stripped his cloak off. One side of it was stiff and dark. He held it out to me behind him.

I took it into the pool and pushed it under where the water fell, and worked the dark side against itself with a handful of gravel from the bottom. Cold water. Hot would have set it.

"They gave me the wooden sword at Corinth," he said.

He was washing his forearms. Across the left one a welt stood up where the stick had caught it, red and raised, and the knuckles of that hand were split.

"He had the year right, the one on the road. The emperor wanted the Greeks to see a proper fight, so they gave him one. I'd been in that sand five years by then. When there was somebody to impress, they put me up."

He did the backs of his hands.

"The Gaul was older than me, and slow on his left side. Nobody on the benches knew that. The emperor never got out of his chair. I put the Gaul down, and he sent the sword out to me across the sand, and a magistrate wrote my name in his book. The lanista had a copy made for me on a tablet. I kept it in a box."

Down at the ford Felix was telling the mules something, and the goat was answering him.

"After the sword I needed work. Celer was legate then, on the proconsul's staff. He wanted a man to walk behind his wife, one that people would know from the sand and stand back from. Plautia. I swore to her in his atrium, in front of his household gods."

The water off the cloak went pink and then went clear. It was good wool, fulled hard, darned at the hem with green thread.

"Two years I walked behind her. To the baths. To her mother's house. Out to Kenchreai, to the Egyptian goddess, on her days. When he was away I slept across her door. Nobody laid a hand on her in two years. I was a good bodyguard. That was the use of me."

He sat on the lip of the pool with his boots in it.

"By then the emperor had given the Greeks their freedom, and Celer's post had gone with it. He'd stayed on at Corinth with nothing to be legate of. He had debts. He'd bought a house he couldn't pay for, out where the cypresses are. The year of all the emperors, in the summer, he had me called to the atrium at night and said I'd been in her bed. He was sorry about it. He said so, more than once, with her standing there. She didn't look at me. Two of his house slaves said they'd seen it. One was the girl who did her hair, and she cried all through it. They'd been shown where to stand."

I held the cloak down in the fall, and the water went through it.

"Put a wife away for that, and you keep a share of what she brought. Her father had given plenty with her. They flogged me in his courtyard, by the fishpond, where the house could watch. While they were at it, his steward went through my box and took the tablet."

The cold had my feet again.

"After that Celer said the sword was nothing. His story was that he'd bought me from the lanista the month before the games, so the lanista had no right to free me, and the magistrate had written down a mistake. So I was his. A slave who'd been in his wife's bed. The magistrate's book is still in the record office at Corinth, with my name in it." He moved one foot in the water. "I was over his garden wall the same night. A fisherman took me across the gulf for my belt buckle. I carried on the Kirrha quay that winter. They asked my name and I gave them Valens. The mules came after, off a Corinthian dealer who couldn't feed them till spring."

"Her brother took her home," he said. "Celer kept the boy."

I wrung the cloak out. It took both hands, twisting against each other, and a long pour of water ran out of it and splashed round my feet. I shook it and spread it on a rock in the sun.

He was watching me do it.

"Last autumn a freedman of her brother's found me at the harbor," he said. "I was loading. The brother's gone to law for her dowry. He wanted me to come and stand up and tell the court it was a lie. I said I wouldn't. He'll have told them where he found me. Inside the month that notice went up in Corinth. You saw the month on it."

"Yes."

"I saw it too."

"I read it twice," I said.

"And still came to the yard."

"My question wanted carrying."

"There was Praxias."

"Praxias wanted half a drachma over three."

"Five hundred for me walking," he said. "Whoever wants five hundred brings a rope. Those three brought none. He doesn't want me back. He wants me in a ditch."

He reached for his cloak. It was still wet through. He put it on anyway.

"He had you by the cloak."

I felt for it. The skin was raised in a line across the front of my throat where the edge of the wool had dragged, and it stung.

"It is only the wool," I said.

He was first down the goat path to the ford.

* * *

The string had drunk and was standing in the shallows, nosing at the plane roots. Felix sat on the bank with the goat's head in his lap. He had found a crust somewhere and was giving it to her a crumb at a time, and she was letting him.

Felix had chosen her at Kirrha that morning. The Aigion man kept his goats in a pen of hurdles at the root of the mole, where the first boats of the year were tied up with the new pitch still soft on their seams; the goats had crossed the gulf in one and were not yet sure of the ground. Felix was in among them before anybody could stop him. He came back holding a brown she-goat with a white blaze by the horns, the one that had put her head into his stomach, while she tried to do it again.

"This one," he said. "She's got a temper. The god likes a temper."

The Aigion dealer told Valens his price. Valens left it to me, and I paid it myself, out of my rag into the man's palm, and the rag was thin after it. We had eaten first, sprats a woman was frying at the mole and selling in twists of fig leaf, and Valens had paid her for all three, and Felix had eaten his own and half of mine and talked through both. A boat from Lechaion had come in on the morning wind and was unloading at the mole's end. Valens had stood on the quay the whole while with his back to it and his eyes on the pen, while the oil men brought his jars along the mole and argued with him about the straw.

At the edge of the ford I washed the oil off my foot, and pushed the thong of the sandal back up through its hole with a twig, and knotted it underneath, double, to hold at least as far as the top of the hill.

Felix had seen the camera. It hung outside my clothes now, against the front of the himation, where I had forgotten it.

"What's that? Is it silver? I saw one of them go off through the olives holding on to his head. You hit him with it?"

"It was my father's," I said, and it went back inside my clothes.

Valens had left the string for the bank. Felix had the water skin off the last saddle; he drank, and spat, and drank again.

"It was that crust," he said. "It was off."

"Don't give the goat any more of it," Valens said. "If anybody in the town asks, we came on a man in the ditch below the ford, and the road had been at him before us."

Felix lowered the skin. "I could say I saw them run. Two of them, into the olives, and I chased them with a stone, and the tall one had a sword, and I hit him over the head and he dropped it." His voice was coming back. "I could have the sword."

"You came on a man in a ditch."

"That's nothing to tell."

"Tell it anyway."

Valens chose a stone from the bank, near a jar's weight, and roped it opposite the single one on the mule that had lost hers.

At the stepping stones I gave him back his pouch. He put the purse in my hand.

I opened the purse. There was silver in it, Roman denarii, some so new that Vespasian's heavy face had hardly been rubbed, and Corinthian bronze underneath. What the goat had left in my rag would not have bought the god his cake. This would, with plenty over.

"It is more than the god wants," I said.

Valens was down the string, checking girths. "Keep what he doesn't," he said.

I untied my rag and tipped the dead man's silver in with the little that was mine, and knotted it again. Apollo had been taking money from men with blood on them since Orestes, and had never been known to send any back. I let the empty purse go in the stream. It sank among the stones, and the current took the thong out straight behind it.

The walkers reached the ford while it was sinking: a gray-bearded man leading the donkey, two women behind with baskets on their heads, and a boy. He let the donkey drink, standing in the water beside her, and considered the mules, and us.

"There's a dead one below," he said. "In the ditch, past the terrace. Somebody's had his purse."

Felix was on the bank with the goat by the horns.

"We came on him," Felix said. "His purse was cut. We came on him and came on up, because of the oil."

His chin was up, and he spoke it to the graybeard's face, and the graybeard took it as it was given.

"It's been like this since the freedom. Robbers off every boat." His glance went to Valens. "You've been in the thorns."

"There's thorn all along this road," Valens said.

The younger of the women had set her basket down and was telling the other about the sandals on him, how new they were, what a shame. My rag hung at my belt with his silver in it. I kept a palm over the knot.

"My son's run back to Kirrha for the headman and a cart," the graybeard said. "Somebody ought to sit with him till they come, and it won't be me." He clicked the donkey on over the stones. The women took up their baskets and followed, and last the boy, looking back at the goat.

"He believed me," Felix said, when they were round the bend above. He sounded surprised.

"He'd settled on robbers back on the road," Valens said.

The goat would not go.

She had found the barley he dropped in the road and eaten every grain of it while we were busy. She stood at the foot of the climb with her head low. Felix pulled. She lay down.

"She's doing it on purpose," he said. "Is she? Is she ill? Are goats sick when they see a dead man?"

"She is full," I said.

He looked at her, and at the road climbing away above us, and then he got his arms under her and stood up with her across his shoulders, her forelegs in one fist and her hind legs in the other, as the shepherds carried them on the mountain. She blinked and let him.

"I'm calling her Spicula." His voice came from under her. "After the murmillo. The one the emperor gave a house to."

"She is the god's," I said.

"The god won't mind what she's called."

The goat was heavy and he carried it. With her across his neck he looked like the Calf-Bearer in the museum on the Acropolis, if the Calf-Bearer had been twelve and in a temper. For the first part of the climb he asked nothing whatever, which in my hearing he had never managed before. At a hairpin under a wild fig he waited for me, red in the face under her.

"Did you see him do it?"

"I heard it."

"Quick, though?"

"I was watching something else."

His look said I had cheated him. He took the goat on up to where the string had stopped to blow, and waited there for Valens, and put the question to him instead, straight.

"Did he go down straight off?"

"No."

Felix went on in front with the goat.

Above the ford the road took the hill in bends, stony, between walls. Overhead the two great cliffs over Delphi had the sun full on them and had gone tawny. Traders came down from the town against us, going to sleep at Kirrha for the boats, and others were coming up behind, and each of them said good evening and got good evening back. A man with a tray of lamps on his head asked whether he would reach Kirrha by nightfall.

"If you don't stop," Valens told him.

He walked at the tail. Twice I glanced back, and he was walking half turned about, watching where we had come from.

At the next bend I stood aside for the string until he came up.

"You need not take it in," I said. "The question. There are other days. Next month would do as well."

"Three days," he said.

"They will be there. Those two, or others."

"A knife in the precinct would have a hundred men on it. They'll want me on a road, not in a crowd." He shoved the tail mule over by the rump. "The goat comes to the yard the evening before."

We walked on. "When it's over, the mules are for the high grazing," he said. "Above the cave. Up there you see a man coming a whole morning off. I'll stay up the summer."

The sherd with my question on it was in my belt, against the knot of the rag. Its edge found me as I walked.

"Kallia will hear at the spring tomorrow that a man is dead below the ford," I said.

"She hears what the donkey man heard."

"I am not telling Kallia a story. She gets the whole of it from me, or nothing."

"Kallia, then. Nobody else."

It was three days to the seventh of Bysios.

Ahead, Felix had stopped at the turn of the road to shift the goat on his shoulders. She was asleep. Her head hung beside his ear with the eyes shut, and her jaw kept moving.

Valens was at the outside of the bend, where the wall was low. He was looking down. I joined him at the wall.

The whole plain lay under us, bare and darkening, from the foot of the hills to the shore. Out past it the gulf still held some light, and the boats were in at Kirrha with their sails furled. The road ran white over the flat to the first of the terrace walls. I knew the spot by the turn of the wall below the olives.

A cart had stopped beside the ditch.
