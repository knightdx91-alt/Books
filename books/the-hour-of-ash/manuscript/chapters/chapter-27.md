I had expected the Sibyl's cave to be a door, a live one, cool under the hand and quiet in the way the Corycian had been quiet on the morning I crawled into it, and I had expected it on no better evidence than Fenn's refusal to tell me what was in there. He refused at the landward end of the Puteoli fish wharf as a spring evening ran out, holding my daughter up before his face with both his arms straight out.

"Will you get a load of that, she's got it right where I put it, the whole of Jupiter up on top like a shaving brush, and people tell me I'm no good at my job."

He meant her hair. It had come in thicker over the winter and rose off her crown in a black brush that no amount of Grata's spit would lay down.

He was up off a bollard while the string was still among the warehouses, too soon to have made out our faces. He wore no robe that evening but a traveling tunic of plain unbleached wool that stopped at his knees, his shins below it thin and white as peeled sticks, with a broad straw hat hanging down his back on its cord. I had written him that we were bringing a load up and that he might see the girl, and nothing more; he had written back one line to say he would meet us at the fish wharf.

Aelia hung at the end of his arms and kicked. A hand came free of her wrappings and found the cord of his hat where it crossed his throat, and pulled.

"She's strong," he said, strangled. "Takes after somebody. And the husband, he's hauling tomorrow?"

"To Misenum, with brick for a villa going up on the cape. He'll be back by dark."

"Then tomorrow you're mine, the three of you." He unpicked her fingers from the cord and gave her back to me. "We're taking a walk to Cumae."

"What's at Cumae?"

"The Sibyl."

"The Sibyl's been dead since before Rome had a wall."

"So she won't mind company. Wear shoes you can walk in, because it's further than it looks on the map, and the map's a liar anyhow, whoever drew it never left Neapolis."

"Fenn, what's at Cumae?"

"I'm showing you, I'm not telling you. Tell you now and you'd only argue, and I mean to watch your face when you've got nothing to argue with."

Valens had led the dun up behind me, the other three strung after her and the jars swaying in their nets, and Lucius rode on top of the second load with his fists in the netting.

"What does he want?" Valens asked me.

"To take us to Cumae tomorrow, when you're at Misenum. The children too."

"How far?"

Fenn answered for himself in his good Greek, with the small bow that went with it. "There by noon and back by lamplighting, sir, at a child's pace; the road is fair as far as the lake of Avernus, and beyond the lake there is a path across the ridge that I have walked oftener than I can remember."

"The boy can't walk that."

"He shall ride. I will hire an ass."

Fenn turned to the quay, and I turned with him. Among the dripping baskets at the octopus seller's stall an old gray ass stood tethered to a ring in the wall, dozing with its ears half down and its weight on three legs.

"Not him," said Valens. "It's resting the near hind. It's favored that leg since the corner."

"You choose for me, then, sir. I shall pay whatever you tell me, and quarrel about it afterward with myself."

The carters' inn by the tunny sheds had beds of straw, and the straw had been in them since the cold months, possibly the cold months before those. When the children were down, one at either hip, I took the satchel on my knees and packed it with everything I had carried into the Corycian, as I always did when I was a day from Pompeii; then I tipped it all out on the blanket to see that nothing was missing, and packed it again. Valens lay stretched on the far edge of the mattress, his fingers laced behind his skull.

"Is it the door," he asked, "that he wants to show you?"

"He won't say. He says I'd only argue with it."

"You would. Bring the boy's hat. The sun on the sea burns him through his hair."

"I've packed it."

"It's one day."

"I know."

If he made anything of the satchel he kept quiet about it, and in a while he rolled over and slept.

I had not asked Fenn whether it was a door. This was someone who had given me the year the same afternoon he first heard my voice; if I had asked him there among the octopus baskets he would have answered there, and I would have taken the answer to bed with me. I wanted a night of it unanswered: a door that kept its own time and not his, whose rhythm owed nothing to tables worked backward from a schoolbook, a door within a morning's walk of where my children slept. I had seen good pieces come apart in the hands of people who could not leave them be.

* * *

Valens had done his choosing in the dark. In the inn's yard, when I carried Aelia out bound to my front and Lucius beside me with a fist in my skirt, a small gray jenny was drinking at the trough, rush panniers hung over her back, and Valens had her off fore up on his knee and was running a thumb inside the rim of the hoof.

"She's sound," he said. "She bites. Not hard."

He let the hoof go and lifted Lucius into the near pannier onto a folded blanket, ran a rope over the basket's mouth, and put the end of it into his son's two hands.

"Hold that."

Lucius sat bolt upright in the rush with the rope across his lap and his fists locked round it, and as far as I saw he did not loosen them till the sun was high.

Our food went in the other pannier, and Valens moved a loaf across until the jenny stood level. He gave me the look over her withers that I had had from him on the morning of every journey since the ambush, and went to his string. Fenn waited at the yard gate with his hat on and a stick in his hand, and came across only when the mules were past the last of the sheds, their noise going away up the Misenum road.

He took the streets uphill so fast that I had to lengthen my stride to stay with him, the jenny's rope round his wrist, and he climbed the steep places without stopping for breath. Our street rose between garden walls, and at the top of the last rise he turned off it onto a track bending inland.

"Cumae's west," I said. Our shadows fell behind us down the track, and I needed no compass to read them.

"It's on the way."

"It isn't. It's the opposite direction."

"It's on my way. Five minutes, and you'll thank me, and if you don't thank me you're on cheese duty."

The track crossed a lip and dropped into a bowl among low hills, and the floor of the bowl was white. It was a crust of white and gray broken into pits and cracks, with steam coming from every crack, in threads or in slow fat puffs that leaned in the morning air and went to nothing. The smell met us on the lip. It was the smell of our own street's fullery, on the days the fuller's boys hung wet tunics on a wicker frame over a pan of burning sulphur to whiten them; I had smelled it every week for four years without wondering where the fuller bought his sulphur, and this was where. Two men carrying baskets on their backs were scraping yellow crust off the rocks round a vent with short iron hoes, their arms yellow to the elbow. Within a dozen breaths my eyes streamed and my throat tasted of struck matches. Somewhere on our left something bubbled, thick and slow, like porridge left on the fire.

"Smoke," said Lucius out of the pannier. It was his first word of the morning.

"This is where the town gets its sulphur and its sore throats," said Fenn, "and the doctors send the rich men up to sit in the steam for their chests and their wives, and the bath keeper round the back of that hill charges them for every breath of it."

The bronze crescent on its red thread had worked out of Aelia's wrappings onto the outside of the sling, and I tucked it in again. Sulphur in the air would darken bronze in an afternoon; it was Grata's gift, and Grata would demand to hear who had let her good bronze go brown as a bad tooth.

Fenn strode onto the white, chose the thinnest-looking crust, and stamped on it with his heel.

The ground answered with a deep hollow note we felt in our feet, a drum struck in another room, and the jenny's ears went flat; I had the rope off his wrist and the jenny two steps nearer the lip without having decided to move.

"Fenn! Get off it!"

"It does that." He stamped again, delighted. "The floor's hollow, the Spectrum would fit under here, ice and all, and the bath keeper says when his grandfather was a boy they'd hear it clear down at the road. Relax, would you, it's been doing this since Hannibal." He saw my face. "All right, I'm coming."

He came picking his way back and stopped at a vent close to the lip; the sulphur had grown round the crack's mouth in a crust of small bright yellow crystals, fringed along their tops with something white that the steam had left, and he stooped over it.

"Lemon meringue," he said.

The men with the hoes lifted their heads at the English, and the older one called across in Greek.

"Chaldean! Is it going to rain?"

"After the Ides," Fenn called back, "and not a day before, so bring a hat."

"At the automat," Fenn said, "they did a lemon meringue so stiff it would hold a spoon upright, with the meringue on top a mile high and the little brown peaks on it, like this, and you could look your slice over before you paid, so you'd walk the row hunting for the tallest one, everybody did it, with some guy behind me breathing down my collar." He sketched the peaks in the air above the crystals, not touching. "You'd go in for a cup of coffee and leave with the pie, every time."

In his room, a year ago, it had been coconut cream. I let him have the lemon.

We left the bowl by another track that came round west at last, the steam still showing over the lip until the hill shut it off. Fenn walked ahead and then dropped back beside the jenny, and for some way he did not speak.

"Going in, at the Corycian," he said, "was it cold?"

"Very cold. It met me halfway."

"How long did it hold?"

"I didn't time it. I was busy going in."

"Sure you were." He swung the stick at a thistle and missed it. "Was there a string? Kite string, white, tied round a stone by the slot. You'd have stepped right over it."

"I didn't see any string."

"Thirty years," he said, "goats got it, goats'll eat anything that holds still, I watched one eat a postcard off me once, stamp and all, and look at me after like I owed it a letter."

The coast opened out beneath us. A long shallow lake lay behind a sandbar that carried a raised road between it and the sea. Its whole surface bristled with stakes, gray poles stuck in the still water in rows by the hundred with ropes slung between them; men in small boats worked their way down the rows lifting the ropes, which came up black, knobbed and dripping.

"Oysters," said Fenn. "Best in the empire, every rich man in Italy eats them and tells you about it after, at length. Can't stand them, never could, it's like eating something that's still making up its mind."

"You live over a tunny shed."

"Tunny's long dead when it gets up my stairs. It's all I ask of a dinner."

"Valens didn't like this," Fenn said presently. "Me taking you off."

"He chose your donkey."

"He did, and looked her in the feet, and put the boy in the basket like he was loading eggs. That's a guy who'd have come along if somebody hadn't wanted brick. I'm no great shakes on people, but I know that much. What was he, before he took up mules? Because I've seen men built like that down at the fleet, and none of them got that way hauling brick."

"He was a gladiator, at Corinth. Long before me."

"No kidding. Like Kirk Douglas, with the chin and everything, I saw that one at the Boyd three times and bawled like a baby when it was over, and don't you tell him that. He talk about it?"

"No."

"Who would."

Past the oyster stakes our way bent inland to climb between steep wooded slopes, and quite suddenly a second lake lay below us, small, round and deep, sunk in its own bowl, with the hills standing straight up all round it. Its water was a green so dark it read as black, and so still that the trees on the opposite slope stood in it upside down without a tremor. Along the near shore lay the remains of something large: stone quays, a line of tall brick sheds with their roofs fallen in, a channel cut toward the oyster beds with its mouth silted shut and reeds growing in it.

"Avernus," said Fenn. "Front door to hell, and Aeneas paid the dead a call from round here someplace, and every guide on this coast will sell you the very spot, and no two of them agree. Agrippa put a navy yard on it and it silted up on him, and the fleet moved to Misenum, and so did I, a century late."

"The Greeks called it Aornos," I said. "Birdless. The air over it was supposed to kill anything that flew across."

He pointed with the stick. Out on the black water, a stone's throw from the shore, eleven or twelve ducks were going about their business in a loose brown crowd.

"So much for that."

"Duck," said Lucius.

We ate on the shore below the ruined sheds, sitting on a fallen block of quay, with the jenny cropping nearby and her panniers lifted off onto the stone. Lucius, lifted out to eat, preferred to stand at the edge of the block and watch the ducks while the crust crumbled away in his fingers. The bread was dry and we ate it anyway. Aelia, unwound from the sling and laid on my folded cloak, slept with her arms flung up past her ears. I unbuckled the satchel and got the camera ready, because the boy at the stone's edge with the black water before him and the ducks beyond it was something I wanted, and the cloth was off it before I remembered who else was sitting there.

Fenn had stopped chewing.

He sat with an olive halfway to his mouth and stared at the Pentax in my hands.

"Is that a camera?"

"My father's."

"Don't." He put the olive down on the stone and cleaned his fingers on his hem. "Not one word about it, when I get there I'll walk into Wanamaker's and buy one off the counter like a human being." He reached a hand toward it and drew it back. "Is it loaded?"

"Yes."

"How many frames left?"

"Some."

"Do me," Fenn said, "would you? One, ahead of the boy, here, with the lake, where's the light, is the light right, you tell me where to stand and I'll stand there till I grow roots."

I stood him on the end of the block with the lake behind him and the sun across his left cheek, the hat pushed back so that his face was in it. He fussed the tunic straight. He took the hat off and replaced it. Then he felt in the breast of the tunic and produced a small brass box and offered it for my inspection, grinning.

It was a Zippo. The brass had gone brown everywhere but at the corners, where years of thumbing had kept it bright, and the lid sat a little crooked on its hinge.

"For the picture," he said. "Like the Marlboro man."

Stooping, he pulled a dry stalk of grass from the crack between two stones, bit it off short and set it between his lips for a cigarette, then flicked the lid back and cupped a palm round the top, as smokers did on windy corners. The hand shook in the cup, and he let it shake, and his thumb found the wheel.

The wheel turned with a dry scrape. Two thin sparks came off it, pale even in the shadow of his hand, and fell on the wick and died.

He tried again: scrape, one spark, nothing. He squinted into the glare, the grass stalk jutting from his face, a man on the corner of Broad and somewhere in a wind, determined.

"Hold it," he said round the grass. "Hold on, it works, it works sometimes, you've got to warm it up."

I had his face in the finder, the stalk, the cupped hands, the thumb bearing hard on the wheel, with the black lake standing behind him full of upside-down hills; I took it while his thumb was still trying.

He heard the shutter and lowered his hands.

"That one's no good, it didn't catch," he said. "Do another when it catches."

"It's the one I wanted."

"Of a lighter that won't light."

"Of you."

He took the stalk out and threw it at the ducks, stowed the Zippo in his tunic, and sat again by the cheese.

"You're the doctor," Fenn said. "Once we're back you get that one printed up and mail me a copy, glossy, and I'll stand it on top of the TV where the company can see it. Now the boy, get the boy."

But the boy had given up on the ducks and settled with his back to the lake, eating the rest of the crust; what I had wanted was gone. I wrapped the camera in its cloth again.

* * *

From Avernus a path climbed westward up the ridge, too steep for carts, through scrub oak and flowering broom, and the jenny took it with her ears forward. Lucius rode it asleep, curled in the rush, his grip on the rope given up. At the crest Aelia woke wanting milk, and wanting it now, and I found a rock among the broom and opened my tunic for her. Fenn walked the jenny on a dozen yards and let her crop.

"Take your time," said Fenn. "I'm admiring Italy. Somebody has to, and the Italians are all busy."

He admired it until she was done. Under us, on our side of a great dune, a town of red roofs sat inside old walls; past it, at the water's very edge, a hill of dark rock stood up by itself, walled round its crown, with the roof of a temple showing over the wall.

"Cumae," said Fenn, without turning round. "Oldest Greek town in the country, and doesn't it look it."

The way to the cave skirted the rock's seaward foot, beneath the temple wall, along a terrace where the sea wind blew straight in and laid the grass down flat. The entrance was a doorway cut in the cliff, tall and narrow, wide at its foot and drawing in toward its top. Beside it, on a stool, sat a man with an unlit lamp at his feet and a row of hive baskets upturned on a plank against the cliff behind him, staring out to sea with a pot lid in his lap.

He knew Fenn. He turned at the scrape of the jenny's hooves, and his face did not change much, but it changed.

"The Chaldean," he said. His Greek dropped its endings the way the Greek of Neapolis did. "Is it that month again?"

"It is," said Fenn. "I have brought guests, which you must admit is an improvement on me."

"Two coppers each. Children for nothing, and it's children touch everything." His palm came out for the coins. "Honey, if the lady wants it. The Sibyl's own, off the windows. Less of it than yesterday. The third window swarmed at sunup, queen in front, the whole house of them, and made for Misenum. I stood on this terrace beating a pot lid at them like a fool. None of them so much as looked round."

"I am sorry for your loss," said Fenn.

"Misenum," said the keeper to the sea. "What does a bee want with Misenum?"

"I went there once myself," said Fenn. "For the pay."

"You came back, at least."

Lifting Lucius out of the pannier woke him. I stood him on his feet, where he swayed with a fistful of my skirt, gazing at the doorway. Fenn hitched the jenny to a ring near the hives, and she bit him on the forearm.

"Not hard," he said, shaking the arm out. "Not hard, says the gladiator."

Inside, it was a corridor. It ran straight into the hill from the entrance, so straight and so long that the end of it was only a small brightness at the bottom of a great length of shadow, and its walls leaned toward each other as they rose, so that it was wide at the floor and narrow at the top. The stone was yellow tuff, so soft a key would scratch it, and it had been cut with a pick, the strokes running in slanting rows along both walls, each a thumb wide, overlapping like the courses of a basket. I put out the hand I had spare and touched them as I went, which I could no more help than blinking. They were dry and faintly gritty and left a little of themselves on my fingers.

Along the left-hand wall, every twenty paces or so, a side passage had been driven through to the cliff face, ending in an opening on the sky, and each let the afternoon in. It came low and slantwise off the sea and fell across the floor and up the opposite wall in a broad clean slab of light, so bright the pick strokes stood out in it like writing; beyond it the shadow closed in again until the next. The sea's noise came in at each opening and fell quiet between them, and the dust we raised turned gold as it crossed the light and vanished beyond it.

"Who cut it?" I said.

"Who can say? The priests swear it was the god, I say some Greek with a pick and a contract." Fenn moved into the first bright patch, swung round to face us and went on backward with his arms spread wide. "Step right up, ladies and gentlemen, boys and girls, on your left the windows of the Sibyl of Cumae, oldest fortune-teller in the business, seven hundred years at this location and not one day off for good behavior. She writes your future on an oak leaf, folks, a leaf, and lays it in the doorway along with everybody else's, the door swings open, the wind comes in off the sea and shuffles the deck, and you walk out with the fellow's behind you." He tipped his hat to an empty square of sun. "No refunds."

"She sold books too," I said. "To a king."

"To Tarquin, the last king they had before they got smart. He laughs at her price, so she burns some right there in front of him and asks the same for the rest, and he laughs again, and she burns more, and in the end he pays the full price for the last few. What wouldn't I give for the ones she burned?" He backed away out of the light. "That's the racket, kid. Nobody ever went digging for the stuff they kept."

Lucius had hold of my forefinger. At the end of each bright stretch he hung on the finger and leaned toward the shadow, and I towed him on into the following light. The third time he plumped down on the floor and had to be lifted. The sling's strap had been biting my neck since Avernus, my feet were hot in my shoes, and the satchel with everything I had packed and unpacked in the night bumped at my hip with nowhere to set it down.

Fenn had vanished up one of the side passages. I heard his voice out of it, hollow.

"Mara, come here, come look at this, quick, before it moves."

The passage went twelve paces in and ended in a window in the cliff, square-cut, as tall as a doorway and open to the sky. He was up in it, sitting sideways in its frame with his back to one jamb and his sandals braced on the other, the whole bright sea behind him; his hat was off, and the wind had his white hair on end.

"There's the island, Ischia, they call it Aenaria round here, and the Greeks call it monkey island, and there's not a monkey on it, I went over and checked." He leaned far out to look down. "And straight down there's a fisherman asleep in his boat, and he's the main attraction, he hasn't moved since before we got here. Hand her up, hand me the baby, she'll love it."

"She's asleep too."

"She can love it later. Come on up yourself, then, there's room for two in here, best seat in the house." He squinted down at me from his window. "What's the matter with you? You walk like somebody's grandmother."

"I'm somebody's mother," I said. "Twice. The newer one's strapped to my front."

"Is that so," he said, and hung out above the water again, a hand hooked on the rock and his legs swinging over a fall I preferred not to consider.

"How old are you, Fenn?"

"Sixty-nine in August, give or take a calendar. Why?"

"No reason."

"You think I can't get down from here." He shouted down to the sleeping fisherman in Greek to ask what he wanted for his fish.

I sat on the sill of the passage, where the tuff had been cut into a low step, and Lucius leaned on my knees. The ridge had left a pain in the back of my leg.

He quit the window in his own time, dropped the last of the way, and landed with a grunt.

"It's down this end," Fenn said.

The corridor went on through its light and its shadow. Near the end the bright patches came closer together; then the walls drew back, and there was a room, a square chamber cut in the rock with a low bench running round three sides of it and a vaulted ceiling gone black from lamps. Feet had worn the floor to a shallow dish. Little clay figures stood on the bench, a hand high, a girl holding a jug, a woman with a child, and people had pushed so many coins into a crack in the wall that it had gone green. On the bench a sailor off some fleet galley sat carving his name in the tuff with the point of a knife; he had reached the third letter, and never raised his head.

"Here she sat," said Fenn in his barker's voice, but without much effort in it, "and the god got into her from underneath and she foamed at the mouth and so on and so forth." "Spell it well, sailor," he added in Greek, going by. "The lady reads everything." He kept walking. "That's for the customers, this way."

On the landward side, short of the room, there was a cut I would have walked by: a low narrow passage driven a few yards into the hill and stopping blind, with no window and no light of its own; the keeper kept his spares there, hive baskets, plaited and domed, stacked with a broom and the neck of a smashed jar stopped with a rag. Fenn handed me the broom, carried the baskets out to stand against the corridor wall, and went in stooping. I followed with the broom in one hand, the baby on my front, Lucius behind me in the half-light.

The passage ended in a face of rock. It was the same yellow tuff, the same pick strokes, nothing to it. Level with his shoulder someone had scratched two strokes into the stone, crossing, and the edges of the strokes had been worn soft and dark by hands.

"X marks the spot," said Fenn. "I was thirty-six years old, what were you expecting, a map?"

He put his palm to the rock beneath the mark, with the fingers spread, and kept it so.

No word out of him; his head tipped toward the hand. The corridor's light reached his shoulder in that passage and no farther, and his hand was beyond it, in the half-dark on the tuff, waiting. I stood behind him with my daughter asleep against me and watched a man of nearly seventy wait for a wall to turn cold. No cold came; I saw it in the way his fingers lay, easy, no answer in them. He kept his hand there anyway, a while past knowing and then a while past that, before he let it drop and rubbed the palm down his thigh.

"Nope," he said.

"Fenn."

"Say something."

"What?"

"Anything, your name, go on, out loud, right at it."

"Mara," I said.

My voice went at the rock and was handed back off it, small, flat, a little worn, and ran on past us into the corridor; somewhere far off near the doorway it sounded again, fainter, from the walls.

"Hear that? It bounces." He tapped the rock by his mark with a knuckle, twice. "I used to stand right here and tell it my name, out loud, like roll call, and it went in there and stopped, like talking into a pillow. Your own breath, the whole hill gone deaf on you. In August you're frying out on that terrace, and in here you could hang a side of beef."

"Eros's is only cool," I said.

"Eros's is a little one. This was a beauty. Biggest I ever found, and I found it on my own, no carter in a wineshop, no fever, I got it out of Virgil and a picture I'd seen in a magazine on a ship once, and I walked in here with a lamp the summer of forty-three and there it was."

"It's dead."

"As a doornail." The grin was in his voice. "That's a joke. Door. Nail. No?"

Lucius laughed, at the voice and not the joke, and the blind end of the passage laughed after him.

"Tough room," said Fenn. "Half the house. I've played worse."

"When?"

"Spring of fifty. Seven years I hiked it the first of every month, rain or shine, and it was cold whenever I came, the same cold, and then one morning I'm halfway down that corridor and I already know, because I can hear my feet. Just my feet, slap, slap, on the floor. Seven years I never heard them in here. I came in and laid my hand on it and it was like any other bit of the hill. Warm like a wall's warm, that's all it was, a wall."

"What then, that morning?"

"Sat out on that terrace till the sun went. The keeper had all his hair then. He brought me out some wine and never asked what for, and I drank it."

"Why? What killed it?"

"Nothing killed it. That's what gets me." He threw his hands out, and the left one went on going after the right had stopped. "Nothing on the tables, and nobody on this coast felt a thing, I asked the length of it for a year. It just quit. One month it's cold and the next it's a wall with my mark on it."

"Did you keep coming after?"

"Every month for a year, in case it was only sleeping. You sit up with a sick friend, don't you? After that, once a year."

"Then why bring me?" That was sharper than I meant, and Lucius startled at the sound. "Why bring me at all?"

"To show you."

"Show me what? That it's dead?"

"That it was here! One right here in the Sibyl's own cellar, under all those customers, and the only folks in the empire who know it are standing in it. Carter finds King Tut, and does he sit on it? He sends for somebody to come look." He clapped the hat on again. "Soon as I'm back I'm writing it up for the Geographic, with pictures, I'll get the keeper in, I'll get his bees in."

There had been another one. That had been the whole of my night's hope, a door that was not his door, and here it was, and he was right about it, and it was a wall.

"And you lived where, all those years?" I asked. "When it was alive."

"Near the boats, over a rope walk. Swell little room." He tipped his chin at the town, off past the rock at his shoulder. "Out the window, there's the top of this hill with the temple on it. I'd check the hill mornings before I checked the weather."

"After it died?"

"Misenum. The fleet paid, and it's an hour from here if you step out. After that it's sixty-two, and Puteoli, for Eros's, you know about that."

"Before Cumae, when you first—"

"Phocis." He leaned a shoulder on the rock beside the mark and crossed his ankles, the way men leaned on lampposts at home. "A goatherd's hut under the Corycian, two winters. I wanted to be handy in case it had a change of heart. No dice." He laughed at himself a little. "Guess what I left up there? Back home, at the mouth, I mean. My coat."

Aelia stirred against me and went heavy again.

"Best coat I ever owned, gray Navy wool, you could've anchored a destroyer with it. I sat at that mouth in it for hours, waiting on the cold to rise out of the slot and stay put till I could trust it, and your breath smoked up there at sunup, even in July. When I did go in I shucked it, because you can't get down that crack on your belly in a coat, and I folded it on the rock where I'd tied off the string, nice and square, Navy style, with my cigarettes in the pocket so they wouldn't get wet. Like a good scout. Be back for that in an hour, I'm thinking. Then it's forty-one, and I'm in my shirtsleeves in Phocis with snow falling on me."

"No coat."

"Not a stitch of it. Every winter since, soon as the wind gets in under a door, there it is, I can feel the weight of it on me. Some goatherd came along and got himself a coat out of the deal, you can bet on it. I hope it fit him, it never did fit me in the shoulders."

The carbon was in the satchel on my hip, folded small in the pocket inside the notebook's back cover, slipped in by the consulate, or by his sister. A gray wool overcoat, it said, with whatever was in its pockets, sent home to the next of kin. In his room he had said he knew what was in the notebook. That, he had no notion of. I laid my fingers on the satchel's flap and did not undo the buckle. Beside me Fenn stayed at his mark, quite comfortable.

* * *

Lucius was sitting in the dust where Fenn's passage met the corridor, drawing in it with a fingertip. I wanted to sit too. I dug the bone out of the satchel for him, since that was why it had come, and settled on the end of the Sibyl's bench in the chamber's mouth, my shoulders to the smoke-black wall and my daughter's weight dragging forward on the strap.

He got up with the bone and looked the length of the gallery, toward the far small brightness of the doorway and all the bars of sun between. He drew his arm back and threw the bone along the floor as hard as he could.

It went knocking away from him. It struck, jumped, struck again, tumbling end over end out of the shadow into a square of light where it flashed white, then on through the light and beyond, still going. We lost sight of it before we lost the sound: a quick dry clatter running away from us along the corridor, which the walls threw back from everywhere at once, from ahead and behind and out of the blind passage at Fenn's mark, until the hill was full of a small bone falling.

Lucius stood very still and listened to it. Then he set off after it, running, through the light and into the shadow, and I was half up before Fenn's hand stopped me.

"He's fine, it's a straight line, where's he going to go, Naples? Let him run, he's been riding in a basket all day like a sack of mail."

In time he came trotting out of the third bright patch with the bone raised above his head, beaming, his knees yellow with dust.

"Bone."

"That's some arm," said Fenn. "C'mere, buddy, let me show you something, it'll change your life."

He dropped to one knee in the light nearest the chamber, where the dust lay soft and pale in the floor's corners, and with the end of his stick he drew a square standing on one of its points, quick and true, with a little square at each corner and at the bottom one a shape with five sides.

"That's home," he said, tapping it. "You hit it, you run the bases till you're back here, and somebody buys you a hot dog."

Lucius stood at the edge of it with the bone and looked hard at the lines.

"Now, who are you? You're Richie Allen." Fenn took the bone from him and set it on the left-hand corner, carefully, square in the middle. "He played third in sixty-four and got rookie of the year, the only guy on that club who didn't fold up in September, and he hit one over the roof in left, nearly, and struck out a ton besides, but you don't talk about that in front of the children." He stood Lucius on the five-sided shape by the shoulders, facing out. "Okay, you're up, swing."

He said the end of it in Greek. Lucius knew that word; he swung his empty arm, hard, with his whole body after it, and nearly fell over.

"Run!" said Fenn, pointing.

Lucius was off. Four steps took him to the near corner, where he halted on the little square with both feet on it, exactly, and looked round to Fenn for what came next.

"You catch that?" Fenn asked me. "He stops on the bag, I was nine before I learned to stop on the bag, my sister used to scream at me from the stoop. Again, second!"

Lucius ran to second and stopped on it. He ran to third, where his bone was, and stood on the bone; and when Fenn shouted he ran for home and stood on home with his fists clenched at his sides, breathing hard, waiting to be told what that had been.

"Safe," said Fenn, sweeping both arms wide across the air, low. "Safe at home, and the crowd goes wild." He made the noise of the crowd going wild, a long hoarse roar out of the side of his mouth.

He hitched his tunic up into his belt and showed Lucius how it was done.

He took the diamond at a dead run, white shins flashing under the hitched tunic, the bone kicked off third into the shadow as he rounded it; coming for home he threw himself down feet first onto his hip and slid. He slid his own length through the dust and stopped with his heel on the five-sided shape and his whole left side yellow from shoulder to ankle; there he lay on the Sibyl's floor with his hat gone, wheezing and laughing at once. "Safe by a mile, and if the ump says different he can take it up with the commissioner."

"Again," said Lucius.

From the bench I watched them. The bars of sun had climbed the wall while we stood at Fenn's mark; they lay higher now, and longer, reddening at the top, and the sea's noise in the windows had shifted as the wind backed. My mind was on the ridge path at dusk with a child asleep in a basket, and on how dark it would be by the shore. Of us three awake in that corridor, only I wanted to go home.

"We should start back, it's a long walk," I said.

Fenn found me with one eye from where he lay.

"One more inning," he said. "Come on, Mom, he's just getting the hang of it."

I gave them one more. Lucius ran it with the bone held out before him in two hands, the way the Delphi priests bore the god's things in procession, and stopped on every corner, and Fenn ran beside him at a crouch, calling the plays.

Outside, the keeper was still on his stool in the evening light, the pot lid in his lap. The swarm had not come back. Fenn bought a jar of honey from him for Grata and paid over the odds, and the keeper bit one of the coins.

"They'll be at Misenum already," he said, "living on some admiral's roses. Next year, then. Same month."

"If I am still here, my friend."

"You say that every year."

The ridge path home went slowly under a low sun, with Lucius asleep in the pannier. Somewhere on the descent to Avernus the youth drained out of Fenn between one bend of the path and the next. He walked holding the edge of the pannier. What talk he had came short.

"Long day."

"It is."

"My feet." And later, by the ruined sheds: "Don't tell Valens about the slide."

"He'll see your tunic."

"I'll tell him I fell down. It's even true."

"That keeper knew you."

"He would."

"Is this the month it died?"

"Spring," said Fenn. "Yeah."

Aelia woke where the stakes began, her first waking since her feed on the ridge. She lay against me with her eyes open and roving over the sky, which by then filled nearly all her view, and she made a long soft noise at it and did not cry. The men were bringing the boats in along the oyster stakes, and their voices carried across the lake, clear and small.

When the stakes were behind us Fenn said, "You walk out there? To Eros's?"

"Every Kalends."

"Still cool?"

"It was cool on the last Kalends."

We walked on a way. The jenny's hooves knocked on the raised road.

"Write me," he said. "After the Kalends. Every one. Just that it's cool."

"And if it isn't?"

"It will be," said Fenn.

We topped the last rise above Puteoli with the sun down in the sea and the town below lighting its lamps. The bay beyond it was going purple from the far shore inward, the water first and then the long hills across it, and lamp after lamp was lit yellow down the mole, while Fenn went down toward them with the jenny's rope looped over his wrist and his stick on the stones, and I followed with my daughter awake against me, watching the color leave everything except the lamps.
