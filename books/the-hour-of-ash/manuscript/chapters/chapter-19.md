By the twelfth morning of my marriage Pacuvius's goose ship was coming round the last cape into the bay, and I had slept all eleven nights before it on her deck, between my husband and a mule. The mules stood amidships inside a square of hurdles lashed to ring-bolts, the four of them in a row with their heads over the topmost rail and their ears laid back at the sea. Valens slept along the outside of the hurdles with his shoulders to them, and I slept along Valens. Felix slept wherever a sailor had last moved him on from, which by the third night was the coil of the anchor cable up in the bows; Pacuvius's girl slept under an awning at the stern with her father across its opening on a folded sail. Nobody aboard that ship had a door to shut on anybody.

We had cleared Lechaion before evening and gone west down the gulf for two days, Achaea on one hand, the mountains of Phocis on the other, until the shores pinched in at the narrows; Felix hung over the rail there and claimed he could have spat on either. Beyond the narrows the islands stood off in the haze. Beyond the islands came two days with no land anywhere, when the master went to the stern each evening with a cup, poured some of it on the water, and prayed in a voice he never used on us. Italy, on the sixth morning, was a low brown line in the west that Felix refused to believe was a country. We went down its coast and round the toe of it, and the Straits took the best part of a day; the crew told Felix about the whirlpool on the Sicilian side, each man improving on the version before his, until he went and sat with the mules. Pacuvius's girl was sick for every mile of the Straits and well again by Rhegium. Every evening after that she sat under her awning and practiced the greeting she meant to give her husband's mother, in a whisper at first and then aloud, until the steersman could say it with her.

On the eighth night, off some coast none of us could see, Valens put out a hand in the dark and closed it round my ankle, and did not let go till morning, as if the ship might take it into her head to drop me overboard. That was the nearest we came, in eleven days, to being married in private.

Early that day, in the dark, Pacuvius's girl found me at the hurdles, where I was trying to get my hair into order of a sort without a comb. She had on her good blue, and a brooch in her fist.

"It won't hold," she said. "The pin. It goes in and it comes out again. She'll look at my brooch before she looks at my face, my father says she looks at everybody's brooch, and if it won't hold I'll have to fasten myself up with a thorn."

It was a bow brooch of bronze, the cheap kind, the pin cast together with the bow, and the pin had sprung where it coiled at the head, so that it lay slack against the catch and jumped free of it at a touch. The coil wanted closing by a hair. I had nothing to do it with except the knife Plautia had given me out of her case, which I still had in my belt. I worked the point of the little blade in between the turns of the coil and levered them close against the flat of it, slowly, a fraction and then another fraction, with the girl's breath on my knuckles the whole while, until the pin came up against the catch with some spirit in it and held there when I shook it.

She pinned her hood with the brooch, unfastened it, and pinned it again twice more to be sure.

"My mother had one that did that," she said. "She stopped it with wax." She was already going aft. "Don't tell my father it was broken," she said over the bundles, and was gone to show him.

The bay opened as the sun rose. We came past an island that stood out of the water on cliffs, and a cape with a temple on its end and a fisherman's nets spread to dry along the temple's stair; when the cape had dropped astern the whole curve of the shore lay ahead, bending away north and then west beyond where my eyes could hold it, with towns along it and the white fronts of villas down at the water between the towns. The water was so still that a fishing boat a mile off drew lines on it with its oars, and the lines held.

I had been watching for the mountain since first light and getting it wrong. I had picked out a ridge behind the cape, and then, as we cleared it, a hump of hill beyond the big town on the north shore, because what I wanted was the outline painted on the wall over the ovens of every pizzeria on Passyunk Avenue, two humps with a fishing village at their feet, and the mountain had only the one.

It stood at the head of the bay where it had to stand, between Neapolis and a town I could not yet see. Once I had it, it was hard to believe I had missed it. The slope began at the beach and rose so long and so gradual that a man might have walked it all morning without noticing he was climbing, and from the rail it was green. Vines ran in rows across the lower ground, with farms set down among them and their tiled roofs catching the light; a belt of darker woods ran round the mountain higher up; above the woods was the top, broad and rounded and bare, with pale stone showing through the scrub.

With straw from the hurdles still on his tunic, Valens leaned his forearms on the rail beside mine.

"Fine mountain for vines," he said.

"Very fine," I said.

"It lies right to the sun. With the sea under it, that slope'll see no frost to speak of."

"None at all."

"Whose is it?"

"Many people's, I should think. A farm each."

"That's the way to own a mountain. A man can walk round his own piece of it in a morning and see to it himself."

I agreed with that as well. I agreed with all of it, and I was glad he had said it, and I studied the farms on its lower slopes until I could have drawn them.

The black had her teeth in the hurdle lashing again, and he left me to see to it.

Alone at the rail, I drew the Pentax up by its strap from under my dress, where it had hung since Lechaion, and the pushed-in place on the lens's front ring flashed as it cleared the cloth. I put it to my eye, and in the finder the mountain was small enough to cover with a thumb, very green, with the edge of the sail clipping the sky on the left and a gull flying through it once. I turned the ring until the farms came sharp, and my forefinger found the shutter button and rested there.

Seventy-nine. It came unasked and sat in my head, two figures with nothing round them, while the mountain stood small and green in the glass.

My finger left the button. I let the Pentax hang against my stomach on its strap for a time before I fed it back under my dress, and the mountain on the other shore went on being green.

Up in the bows Felix was shouting at a long ship that rounded the headland at the far western end of the bay, low and narrow and painted dark, with a bank of oars down each side working together and a small sail furled on a mast that raked forward over her bow. The oars came up wet and flashing, went in again, came up. She passed us at a great distance and fast, with no wind to help her; her wake crossed the still water and rocked the mules long after she had gone.

"Is that the emperor's? Who's rowing it? How many are under there?" Felix had one foot on the rail. "Will they take a free man, if he goes and asks?"

"That's the fleet out of Misenum," Pacuvius called from his stern, "and they'll take a free boy if he goes and asks them, and they'll sit him on a bench under the deck where he never sees the sky, and he can row the admiral round this bay till his beard comes in gray. Go and ask."

Felix took his foot off the rail.

"I wasn't going to," he said.

* * *

Puteoli's mole marched seaward on arches. It was a road carried on piers, a long row of them, with the swell running in under each arch and out beyond it, and along its top went a paving with columns set at intervals and a statue on every column, men in togas streaked white by gulls. The piers were concrete, poured somehow under water and faced with brick, and the sea had eaten the brick off the lowest courses so that the gray core showed through, packed full of pebbles, with a fringe of green weed along each pier that lifted and lay down again with every swell. The crew got out the long sweeps and walked us in along it to a berth near the shore end, between grain ships from Alexandria that were broader than anything at Lechaion and carried a little three-cornered sail high on the mast above the yard, which no other ship in the harbor flew.

There were more people on that quay than I had seen in one place since the road up from Lechaion on the day of the games. Porters went to and fro on the planks of the Alexandrians with sacks on their shoulders and their heads down, and grain dust from the sacks settled in their hair. A man was selling something fried in oil out of a pan on a brazier, and a priest in white linen with his head shaved was arguing with him about it in a Greek I understood and then in a language I did not. A dog slept at the foot of a plank, and the porters stepped round it.

Pacuvius's girl went down our plank first, in her good blue with the brooch at her shoulder, and her father behind her for once instead of at the rail. The mother-in-law was waiting at the foot, a small woman in brown, with one slave holding a parasol above her and another holding nothing, and she read the brooch before she read the face. The girl said her greeting, the whole of it, in her careful Latin, with her fingers laced in front of her, and the old lady heard her out to the end and answered her in Greek, quick Greek, the Greek of the quays, of which I caught perhaps half: that the house was up at the highest part of the town, that there was fish, that the girl was thinner than she had been told. Then she asked who had slept on the deck.

"Married people," the girl told her, in a Greek that was suddenly as good as anybody's. "Corinthians. They had mules."

She looked at us once, and at Valens for longer, and turned to the porters about the girl's chest.

Where the mole met the land an awning had been stretched between two columns, and under it the harbor's man had a table, with tablets stacked at one end, and a slave behind him holding a case of styluses. Every bale and beast landed on that mole went past that table. When we reached it with the mules he had Pacuvius's list of cargo up in front of his face, so near that the wax all but touched the end of his nose, and he was reading it there, moving it from side to side by small degrees, while Pacuvius loomed at his shoulder, telling him what was on it quicker than he could read. The clerk was long in the neck, with his hair combed forward, and there was a smudge of black on the tip of his nose.

He lowered the list, wrote something on a tablet, and turned his long neck toward our mules.

"Beasts for sale?" he said.

"They work," said Valens. "They're mine."

"And that?"

Felix had the fig on his hip, jar and all.

"It's a fig," Valens answered.

The clerk made another note, and the slave behind him moved the case of styluses into the crook of the other arm. "Your name."

"Valens. Gaius Iulius."

The stylus stopped. "Whose freedman?"

Valens reached under his tunic for the leaf and opened its two boards on the table. The clerk took it up two-handed, raised it to his nose, and read it there, slowly, every line, the witnesses and all, while the porters went by behind us with their sacks and the swell slapped under the mole. Then he shut it and left it lying, came out from behind his table, and stood in front of Valens to peer up into his face from nearer than I had ever seen one man look at another in a street.

Valens let him, and stood as still as the column beside the table, gazing over the clerk's head at the water.

"Corinth," the clerk said. "Nero's year." He returned to his stool and wrote. "That was a lucky year for some. And the woman?"

"His," said Pacuvius, behind us. "Married at Lechaion, on the stones, in front of my crew, and I'll swear to it here or anywhere you like."

"And the lad?"

"I'm free," said Felix. "He freed me himself, at Kirrha, in front of the slaver and the whole quay. Shall I show you? There's nothing to show, he only said so. Isn't that enough here? It was enough at Kirrha."

The clerk brought his tablet up to his nose and read over what he had written. "Plenty," he said, and waved us on, and reached for the next man's list.

"If it's letters you're waiting on," Pacuvius told Valens, "there's a wine-shop at the foot of the street with a board hung behind its door, and every master on this run leaves his letters on that board, and not one of us carries a letter a step past it, me least of all. That's the last I'll say to you." He was back up his own plank before anyone had thanked him.

The leaf went back under Valens's tunic, with his belt drawn tight over it. We went along the waterfront, the mules behind us in a string, and he held his tongue until we were clear of the awning and the porters and the dog.

"He knew me," he said.

"He has your name. It is in his wax now."

"He walked round his table to look at my face."

"He read the master's list with his nose in it," I said. "He read his own writing with his nose in it. He has black wax on the end of his nose from doing it."

"He looked hard."

"He's wax on his nose like a pig's been at a candle," said Felix. "See it? Does he eat it?"

At the end of the quay Valens glanced behind us once. Under the awning the clerk had a sailor's tablet up against his face, and the sailor was waving his arms at him.

The wine-shop was the first door off the quay, before the street started uphill into the town, and the board inside it was hung with ships' letters, tied up in thread and tagged with the names of whoever had still to fetch them. Valens ducked in while Felix and I held the mules, and I heard him in there asking the wine-woman to hold anything that came for him by sea from Corinth. While he was in there I caught myself looking down the quay for Myrrhine among the women landing from the boats, with her lady's knife in my belt and no ship but ours yet in from Lechaion.

"Who'll it be from?" the wine-woman said.

"A lady's brother."

"And where do I send when it comes? I don't send, mind. Somebody comes for it, or it stays on the board till it's eaten."

"Pompeii," he said. "I'll send to you."

Valens reappeared with a beaker of wine for each of us that nobody had asked him to buy, and we drank standing in the street with the mules' noses at our shoulders; by noon we were out of Puteoli on the road east, with the harbor below us on the right and the smells of the town thinning out as we climbed.

* * *

The road to Neapolis went into a hill. A ridge ran down to the sea between Puteoli and the city, and somebody under Augustus had cut a road clean through the foot of it rather than go round: a tunnel as high as a house and wide enough for two carts to pass if both drivers were patient. At its mouth boys with pine torches were waiting for custom.

"Light you through, mistress? A copper, and you'll see your own feet."

"Do you live in there?" Felix asked. "What if it goes out? Can I carry one?"

"You can carry your mule," the torch boy said, and Valens paid two of them and sent them on ahead.

It was the first tunnel I had been in since the Broad Street subway, and it was worse. The dust lay ankle deep and rose with each step into the torchlight, so that the boys in front walked in two balls of brown fog, and the dust was up my nose and in the creases of my eyelids before we had gone fifty paces. The carts bound for Puteoli announced themselves from far off, their wheels and their drivers' voices booming along the rock, and then loomed out of the fog almost against us, with their lamps swinging and the oxen's breath on the air. The mules kept their ears flat in there and gave no trouble. Far ahead a small gray patch hung in the dark that I was slow to believe was the end. When the sun found us again beyond the hill, mules and people were one color. Felix spat brown at the verge and said he had eaten half of Italy already.

Neapolis spoke Greek, and I heard it before we were through the gate: a woman on a balcony scolding a child in Greek with its vowels drawn out longer than they were drawn in Phocis, and I understood every word, and found I had stopped in the street to hear the rest of it. There were Greek letters cut over the doors and Greek names on the shop boards, and in a colonnade by the market old men in Greek cloaks sat arguing about something with enormous pleasure. Felix asked one of them where they did their fighting, in a city so big.

"Footraces," the old man said, "and the lyre, and poets. Every four years, for the emperor."

Felix left him with his face screwed up tight.

We slept that night at an inn beyond the city's eastern gate, on the road along the shore, where a little river reached the sea through a bed of reeds. A woman with a ring of keys at her belt met us in the yard.

"Mats upstairs, two coppers a head. The beasts are four the lot, and the boy sleeps with them or pays for a mat like anybody. Money first. The last lot left in the night and owed me."

The mules went into stalls round her yard. There was a fig growing in the yard, and Valens walked round it once and came away, and Felix went in with the mules. The inn's room for travelers was a long loft up in the roof with mats along both walls. Two carters from Nola, taking cheese to Puteoli in the morning and smelling of it, had taken the end under the gable already, with their cloaks over their heads, and we had the end by the ladder.

Through a slot where a tile had slipped against its neighbor, three stars showed, and part of a fourth. With a bit of lead and a hammer that tile would have been back in its course in the time it took to say so, and I lay working out how I would get up to it from the ladder.

"This town of yours." Valens's mouth was very near my ear. "Where do the beasts drink?"

"At the street corners. There are fountains every few houses. The water comes in under the paving, in pipes of lead."

"And carting? Who wants it?"

"Everybody. They are still building."

"Building what?"

"Building back. There was an earthquake, ten years ago, nearly to the season. It brought down half of what was standing."

He lay quiet a while, and the carters breathed at their end of the loft.

"Ten years," he said. "They're slow."

"Some of us," said one of the carters, muffled by his cloak, "have cheese to sell on the Puteoli quay by sunup, and a wife there who can tell from the cheese what time I sold it."

Valens laughed once under his breath, and settled. Soon his breathing changed, and so did the carters', and below us a mule knocked a hoof against her stall and was still.

In the morning the carters were gone before us, and Felix was in the yard with their news.

"They've a school for fighters there, the cheese men said, and an amphitheater that'll seat every soul in town and their neighbors with it, and the neighbors came to the games once and carried half their men home. They fought it out on the benches. Stones and knives. And the Senate at Rome stopped their games for ten years over it." He was outraged for them. "Ten years! And all that while they had to go by it empty? They're back now, every spring, the cheese men said. Will it be this spring? Have we missed it?"

"We've a mule with a loose shoe," Valens said. "Hold her."

It was the black's off fore, in the iron sole they laced over a hoof for stony roads, and its straps had worked slack overnight. Valens had the hoof clamped up on his leg and the lacing threaded through its rings before Felix had a hand on her halter; he drew it tight, tried it with his thumb, and set the foot down, and the black stamped it twice on the stones of the yard to learn if she liked it, and decided against.

All that second day the road kept to the shore between the sea and the mountain. On the sea side there were villas in a string, built out on the rocks with their porticoes over the water, and below them fishponds hewn in the rock and fed by the tide; at one a slave stood in a little boat in the middle of the pond scattering something, and the water round his boat thrashed. On the land side the vines were trained up elms planted in lines across the fields, the vines' arms tied into the trees with withies and slung across in swags from one tree to the next, so that every field was a long colonnade of green. The Romans called that marrying the vine to the elm. Twelve days into my own, I was in no position to laugh at anybody.

The mountain was above us on the left all day, so near now that its farms were farms: dogs on the walls, washing spread on the roofs, a woman high up on a threshing floor who straightened her back as we passed below and watched us before she took up her flail again. From that close it was simply farmland, tipped up on end.

We ate at noon on a bank at the roadside, bread and a hard cheese Felix had bought off the carters, with wine from a farm gate; it had been made on the hillside over our heads, and the jar it came in had been lined with pitch, and the wine had taken the pitch into itself. Valens drank his and lifted his eyes to the vines it had come from, and sent Felix back to the farm gate for another jug.

The road took us through the edge of a small town on a spur of ground between two streams, with its houses built close down to the sea, and on past more villas, and in the last of the afternoon down near the shore again to salt pans: shallow square beds of seawater shut off from the sea by low banks, crusted white at their edges, with men in loincloths raking the crust up into heaps that shone. Valens kept the mules to the crown of the road there, where the ground was firm.

We came to Pompeii from the shore road, up a long slope between tombs. I walked at the dun's shoulder and read every tomb front that faced me as we climbed, and there were plenty to read: curved stone benches with lion's feet at their ends, altars on stepped bases taller than a house, real doors of marble on two or three, shut fast, and on none of them anything painted in the shape of a door. At the top of the slope the gate barred the way with three openings in it, a broad one in the middle for wheels and a narrow one each side for feet, and the town's walls running off from it on both hands.

* * *

Past the gate the street ran downhill between high curbs, paved with big gray blocks of lava jointed in no pattern at all and worn into two deep ruts by wheels, and at the first corner three tall stones stood across it in a line, spaced so that a cart's wheels would go between them and a person's feet over them. The mules had never met anything of the kind, so Valens himself walked the dun through between the stones, talking to her, with his other hand on the stone she was eyeing, and the others followed her because she had gone.

All of that town was being mended; half the houses on that first street had something wrong with their fronts and somebody seeing to it. One was held up by props, big timbers slanted out from its wall into the roadway and wedged against the curb, so that the mules had to be led round them close under its eaves. On another a mason up a ladder was pointing the joints of a brick patch the size of a bed, singing to himself, and the song dropped down the ladder in pieces whenever his head came round. Where two streets crossed, a temple was getting new columns: fresh drums of pale yellowish stone stacked on old gray ones that had been left standing to the height of a man, and above them a crane made of timbers with a great wheel at its foot that two boys walked round inside, shouting to each other, while a block rose on the rope by small jerks as they trod. A lime pit at the side of the road smoked where a man was raking water into it. A length of the paving had been lifted for a trench, and in the trench lay a new water pipe of lead; beside the trench lay the pipe it replaced, in lengths, every length made from a sheet rolled round a pole and soldered down its length.

At the next corner, on a house wall painted red to above the doorframe, a crack had been filled and painted over, eaves to curb, and the red they had painted it with had dried pinker than the wall. It made a stripe a hand wide down the house's front. Anybody in the street could see exactly where that house had split. People were walking past it, and two doors down a woman was leaning on it, talking to a girl with a basket. My thumbnail was in the edge of the stripe, feeling where the new plaster had been laid over the old with nothing to key it to. A join showed or it did not. This one showed to everybody who came down that street, and the plasterer should have been made to stand in front of it from dawn to dark and hear what they said. Valens called my name from the corner, and I took my thumbnail out of the house.

On almost every wall, over the shopfronts, between the doors, on the house fronts themselves, names had been painted up in red and black in tall careful letters, and every name was asking to be made something. Where Valens stood, a white panel had been plastered onto the wall expressly to carry one of them, and Felix had planted himself before it with his hands on his hips.

"Thirty pairs," Felix read out, loud enough for everyone at the crossing. "Thirty pairs of fighters, at the Ides, and beasts, and the awnings up for the sun the whole day. See? There. They're giving games."

What the panel said, in big red capitals, was that the muleteers asked you to make Marcus Satrius Proculus aedile, and that he was a man worthy of it.

"Thirty pairs," said Valens. "In a town this size."

Felix lingered by the panel, mouthing at the shapes of it, and then caught us up and went along ahead of the dun, facing us, to tell us which day the Ides fell on.

Further down, on a long straight street running east, we stopped at a crossing to let a cart by, and Valens said, low, "The muleteers have a man, then."

The woman whose counter we had halted beside spoke before we could move on.

"You'll take those beasts off my front or you'll buy something," she said, "one or the other, they're standing in my custom, and you'll not find a better hot wine this side of the forum, there's pepper in it, so you may as well buy it as stand there."

Her counter reached the street's edge in two arms of masonry set at a right angle, faced with offcuts of colored marble, green and yellow and a purple shot through with white, pieced together wherever a piece would fit; set into its top were the mouths of big jars sunk into the masonry, with wooden lids on them and a ladle hung on the lip of each. A pot steamed on a brazier at the inner end. The keeper of it was perhaps forty, broad, quick, with her sleeves pinned up above the elbow and a scald scar on one forearm. Valens bought three cups, and she filled them and kept talking.

"You've come off a ship and then a road, it's on you plain, the both of them," she said, "and the boy's walking still as though the street's heaving under him, that'll pass, it takes a day for every day you were on the water, my husband's brother says, and he should know, he's never been off one. Rooms is what you want, and tonight, near a gate for the beasts but not too near; there's an inn at the Sarno Gate will stable them and rob you, and one at the Stabian will stable them and rob you less. And work for him, there's not a man here with a beast and a back to him that's not carting, ten years we've been at it, ten years since the earth went, and the forum still looks like a builder's yard. Drink that before it cools."

The wine was hot, and the pepper in it shot up the back of my nose.

"Are there rooms to be had?" I said.

"Know of them. I've got them." She put her hand on my arm, on the sleeve, and talked on with it there, warm from the pot. "Next door. It was a cobbler's, and the cobbler died owing me the winter, and his nephew walked in from Nola and took the leather and left me the smell. A shop on the street, two rooms behind, a stair to the roof. Twenty-four sesterces the month and the first month now, and the water's at the corner, and you'll not wash anything in my jars. You're Greek, you've the walk, and you've not been married long, you stand too near him for that. It wears off."

"Twelve days," I said.

"Well, then." She took her hand back and wiped it on her apron. "Pomponia Grata. Everybody says Grata, and anybody who says otherwise is after something. My husband's in the back. If you hear dice, that's him, and if you hear shouting after the dice, that's me."

Valens paid her the first month from the purse at his belt, and the money vanished into her clothes, and she touched him on the arm too as she went by him to the door next to hers. He looked at the place on his sleeve where her hand had been.

The cobbler's shop had a wide opening on the street with a row of grooved stones along the sill to take the boards of its shutters, and the boards stood stacked inside along one wall. The floor was beaten earth with scraps of leather trodden into it, and in a corner lay a wooden last, too broken to be worth taking, with a cobweb strung from it to the plaster. Behind the shop lay two rooms with a door between them. The back one had a bed frame whose cord had rotted through near one leg, a window set high up onto a light-well, and a stair of wooden treads climbing the outside of the house at the back to the roof.

Valens had the fig on the roof before he had looked at the bed. Felix took the mules off to the inn Grata had named, to be robbed less, with Grata's directions shouted after him the length of three houses.

When the boy had gone, and the light in the window had turned yellow, Valens took a seat on the end of the bed frame, and the cord at the rotten end let him down an inch.

"Which gate is nearest?" he said.

"The Stabian. Back to the crossing with the fountain, then left, and down that street as far as it goes."

"Tomorrow I'm out at that gate after carting, with the string."

"Then I shall walk out by myself," I said, "in the afternoon."

"There'll be men sleeping in those tombs."

"In the afternoon they will be awake."

"Go when the road's full," he said, "and keep to it going and coming."

The door between the rooms was planks on leather hinges, with a bar to drop across it on the inside. He rose and shut it, set the bar in its brackets and lifted it clear, and closed it once more, slowly, to see how it hung on its leather.

"It sticks at the sill," he said.

I put my back against it and pushed, and it went the rest of the way into its frame.

* * *

Valens and Felix had left with the string in the dark, to a contractor at the Stabian Gate who paid by the load for anything with four legs that would carry rubble out of the town. I woke alone in a room with a door, and lay looking at the bar across it.

The cobbler had left the shop floor full of himself: crescents and tags of leather trodden into the earth everywhere, a scatter of nails, a bone awl with its point snapped off. I was on the floor prising the leather loose with the broken awl when Grata put her head in at the opening with a cup in each hand. She gave me one, stood on the sill and drank the other, and gave me the street.

"The fuller at the corner beats his wife on market days and she beats him back on feast days, so it comes out even. The baker over the way has a new oven that draws badly, so don't buy his bread before the third hour. That house with the props in front of it belongs to a man in Nuceria who won't mend it till the town pays him to, and the town won't, so there it'll stand on its sticks for another ten years. And the woman at the fountain with the blue jar will take in your washing and lose half of it, and swear on her mother she never had it."

She had no question for me about the shop. She knew what I would do with it, and said so.

"There's work in you as well as him, you've hands that are for something, I saw you at that wall yesterday with your nail in it, half the street saw you." She drank. "That was my cousin did that wall. He's a bad plasterer and he's family, so it stays."

On the wall of her own house, beside her counter, there was a patch of new whitewash the size of a door. It was still dark with wet at its edges, and through the white the shapes of red letters showed faintly, as they would until the second coat. It had appeared since we came, and Grata saw me looking.

"Don't lean on that," she said. "It's wet. My son's at the baths. A lad who means to stand for aedile one day has to be seen at the baths by the right people with nothing on, and the right people are always at the baths, and when any of them works, the gods know."

In the opening of her own shop next door a man in a sleeping tunic had appeared, scratching his chest and regarding the street with no love for it.

"Is there bread?" he said.

"There's bread at the baker's," said Grata. "Where the oven draws badly."

That afternoon I went to the Stabian Gate and out through it onto the river road, and found only a few tombs along it, two of them curved benches of stone with the dead men's names cut along the backs where the living sat, and a marker or two farther along toward the river, which wound through the flat country below the town with barges on it under brown sails; none of them had a door, painted or otherwise, and I was back in the town with the sun still up.

Valens came home at dusk with stone dust in the creases of his neck and furring his arms, Felix at his heels and full of the amphitheater. We ate on the roof beside the fig, bread and some of Grata's beans in a pot, with the light leaving the tiles round us and the smoke of other people's suppers rising over every quarter of the town.

"They've sunk it in the earth," Felix said. "The whole thing. You walk in at the top and look down. The loads go out past it, there's a dump in the fields beyond, and you come back by it empty, and you look right in through the arches at the end. Did you know it would be sunk? Have you seen one sunk before? Why would you sink one?"

"It's cheaper," said Valens. "You dig, and the earth makes the seats."

"Can I go in, when the loads are done? Nobody was in it today. Will there be games at the Ides? There were thirty pairs on that wall."

Valens was cutting a new noseband for the black out of a strip of the cobbler's leather, and went on cutting.

"There were," Valens agreed.

Felix went over to the parapet in case the amphitheater showed from there, which it did not, and reported as much.

"Find anything at your gate?" he asked me. "Was there anything out there?"

"Barges on the river."

"Fighting ones?"

"Ones with cabbages in them."

"Not at that gate," I told Valens.

"How many gates are there?"

"Eight, in all. Only two have tombs worth the walk."

He tried the noseband against his own hand for size, and pared more off it.

"And the other?"

"The Nuceria Gate. Tomorrow."

"What's tomorrow?" said Felix. "Looking for what, at the gates? Are there fighters buried out there? Do they put their fights on the stones?"

"Some do," said Valens.

"Where?"

"Out where the dead are. Eat your beans."

On the next afternoon it was the Nuceria Gate.

Inside the passage a woman had a basket of roses on the step beside her, made up into garlands and loose, and she held one up at me as I came by. "For your people, lady. The road's half full of roses already. You'll not want to go out bare." I passed her empty-handed, and she called after me something about Greeks.

The road outside ran beneath the town wall instead of striking off across the country, east and west, so that the town rose above it to the north and the fields dropped away southward, and the tombs stood all along both its edges, close as houses in a street, the country behind them and their faces to the road. This was where the dead go out, as the god had put it in Valens's mouth, and they had followed the road out in their hundreds and settled in. Families sat eating on the benches built along the tomb fronts for anybody passing. Behind a low wall a plot was crowded with little stones cut in the shape of a head on a pair of shoulders, faceless, some with a knot of hair carved at the back to show they were women; the roses on an altar beside it were browning at the edges in the sun. In the shade of a tall tomb with a marble woman seated on top of it a man was asleep with his jaw dropped and a fly walking on his cheek, and a candidate's name had been put up in red letters on the tomb's side, the same as on any wall in the town. Farther on, a boy came driving geese along the crown of the road with a long stick, and the geese passed me with their necks out.

It was on the town side of the road, between a big tomb and a little walled plot. I knew it from twenty paces off, with not a letter of it read.

It was a small square house of rubble, plastered white, with a low gable over its front and no higher at the ridge than Valens's head, and its front looked over the road into the afternoon sun. On that front, from the ground nearly to the gable, a door had been painted, at the size of a real one as near as the little house allowed, and painted to look like wood: two leaves, each in three panels, done in a yellowish brown, with the grain drawn on in darker strokes of a fine brush, and nail heads set down the edges as black dots with a fleck of white on each to make them shine. Each leaf had a ring for a handle, and under each ring the painter had put its shadow, falling the opposite way from every real shadow on that road. Above the door, set in the gable, there was a slip of marble with an inscription on it. It said that Aulus Numisius Eros, freedman of Aulus, had made this for himself while he lived, and that he had lived fifty-one years.

I stood in the road in front of it while a cart went by behind me with an axle that wanted grease.

I put the backs of my fingers to the white plaster next to the door. It was warm, as warm as the hood of a parked car on a June afternoon, with the whole day's sun in it. I moved them leftward onto the painted wood, the lower panel of the right-hand leaf, and it was cool.

It was faintly cool, cool as a glass of water left standing an hour by a kitchen sink; on that warm road, with the afternoon sun square on it, it had no business to be cool at all. I moved back onto the white, and it was warm. Onto the painted jamb, done to look like stone: warm. Onto the leaf again: cool. Paint and white lay on a single coat of plaster, and where the brush had crossed from one to the other it had not even lifted. I knocked on the leaf with a knuckle, low and then high, as I had sounded many a plaster panel at home for a hollow behind it, and then on the white beside it. They gave back the same dull note, with rubble behind both. I spread my fingers and gave it the whole of my palm.

The air in front of it was the road's air, and the road's noise went on round me as before. Two tombs along, the family on the bench were passing a jug and laughing at some joke of the grandmother's; the geese were still shouting somewhere beyond the next tombs, and a mule was being cursed at the gate. The plaster against my palm was smooth and a little gritty and only just cool, and it stayed a picture of a door.

A small boy of that family was at my elbow. He had rose petals stuck to the bottoms of his feet. He looked at the door, and at my hand on it, and at me.

"Is this one yours?" he said.

"No," I said.

"Ours is that one." He pointed, his arm out straight, at the bench where his people were. "He was my grandfather. He sold eels."

"Leave the lady be," his mother called across. "She's praying."

"What for?" the boy said.

"Come here and carry this."

The boy returned to his eels. I did not move, nor did my hand, while the family finished their jug and collected their baskets and their cloths and went past me in at the gate, the boy last, walking on the outsides of his feet to keep the petals on. The shadows of the tombs reached the road's far side. Whenever I touched the white plaster next to the door it was still giving back the sun's warmth, and the door stayed cool.

When my hand left it, the coolness stayed in my palm for some distance, and was gone by the gate. The rose woman was still on her step.

"Out bare and back bare," she said. "They'll think you've no dead at all."

"I have some," I said, "but not here."

I meant to sit on the bed only while I got my sandals off. I woke with one of them off and the other on, the light in the high window turned orange, and the noise of the street in the room with me.

My bag lay open on the floor, dropped there the first night, and the toe of the old wool sock stuck out from among my things. I touched it with a fingertip. Inside the wool were the cassette's hard edge and the two small hubs in its face, and I turned one of them through the knit, a quarter of the way round, and it turned.

Grata had her lamps lit along the counter, and as I came to the sill she took one look at my hem.

"You've been out among the dead," she said, "you've the red off the Nuceria road all up your hem, it's the reddest dust in Campania and it never comes out, and you've slept through your supper and most of mine. Your man's been back since the lamps were lit. He's on the roof with his tree and the boy, and the boy's telling the street about some dead fighter whose stone he's found out past the amphitheater, and your man's sitting there letting him. There's patience. Mine would have thrown a pot at him by now. Sit there. Eat that."

I took the bench at the front of her counter with the bowl she gave me, and emptied it, and did not know what had been in it, and it was good. Everywhere in the street the shops were putting their shutters up for the night, each board let into the groove of its sill with a knock of wood on stone, and the knocking came toward us from the crossing one shopfront after another, passed the cobbler's, where our boards still leaned against the inside wall because nobody had thought to put them up, passed Grata's lamps, and carried on toward the gate, while behind it the street filled with people going home to eat, and the noise of them came up after the knocking and filled the street between its walls and rose into the open windows over the shops, voices and feet and a girl on a balcony overhead singing the same two lines of a song to a baby, and then the same two lines, louder than the baby.
