I hated the midwife.

*The ninth hour*

She arrived in the dead heat of the day, while the paving burned up through a sandal sole and every shutter in the street was drawn half to. A boy came behind her with the birthing stool roped onto his back, its legs to the sky. She was short and very broad, in a brown tunic gone black under the arms, and at Grata's counter she stopped and drank off a whole beaker at one go before she looked round for me.

The water had come at noon. I was mending at my plank in the shop with a lamp in bits and the glue warming, and it ran down my legs into the cobbler's leftover scraps on the floor. Grata had been watching me all month from behind her pots. She saw me get to my feet, and knew, and had me out of the shop and through her own back door before I had wiped my hands.

"Not in yours," she said. "That bed's a disgrace, and the fountain's the nearest water, and your roof lets in, I've heard it. You'll have it in mine. There's a well in my court and a door on my room and nobody's old leather underfoot. Walk about. You'll want to sit, and you'll walk instead. I've sent for Statia. She's the best this side of Nuceria and she knows it, so don't tell her."

Her back room ran the depth of the house under a low ceiling, with a slit of window high in the court wall and a door beside it that stood open for the air. There was no air. Beyond the wall her brazier kept the stewpots going for the evening trade. Flies walked on the ceiling. Somebody had swept the beaten earth of the floor so often that it shone at the center like an old saddle.

"How long will it be?" I asked her.

"As long as it likes. You'll ask me that every hour and I'll tell you the same every hour, and somewhere near midnight you'll leave off asking and start telling me what you think of me, and that's when it's getting on. My first took a day. My second came while I was lacing a sandal, and that sandal's unlaced yet. You'd know if I lied, it's in your face, so I'll not." She gave my hip two brisk pats and was off to her pots.

Statia came in wiping her mouth on her wrist, and smiled at me, and that was how I met her teeth. The two at the front had grown crossways, the left laid over the right like a pair of shears half shut, and the one beside them was brown down to the gum. Where an eyetooth belonged there was a hole, and her breath found the hole whenever she said an *s*. In the Soranus I had read, midwives had long slim fingers and the nails cut close. Hers were short and square and pared to the quick, and I gave her that. He had nothing to say about teeth.

"Up," she said. "Show me how you go. To the wall. Back."

Grata's loom leaned in the farthest corner, a blanket half woven on it in red and brown stripes. It was a longer way off than it seemed, and the return was longer.

"Hm," said Statia.

She set her two palms on the belly and moved it about the way a woman at market tried a melon she meant to buy. Her palms were hot and dry. She pressed low, left side, right side, and something in there pressed back at her.

"A girl," she told Grata. "Carried wide. Wide's a girl."

"You said a boy for her at the fullery."

"She carried two. Nobody's right about two." She turned on me. "First?"

"Yes."

"You're no girl yourself, for a first. It'll be slow. Slow's not bad. Slow's only slow." She went on feeling. "Who've you got coming? Your mother? Sisters?"

"Nobody's coming."

"Not a soul?"

"She's got me," said Grata. "She's got the street. Half of it's stood at my counter since noon asking, and the other half's the fullery, and she'll be round, mark me, she can't keep off a birth. There'll be women enough. It's room you'll be short of."

Statia grunted and took her hands away.

Her boy had stood the stool just within the door, and I looked at it because it was something to look at. It was a heavy chair of dark wood with arms and a back, and the seat was cut away at the front in a crescent, so that a woman perched on the rim with nothing underneath. The arms were rubbed pale where hands had held them. At some point in its life one arm had come adrift of the seat and been put back with a peg and glue, and the glue had set in a brown run down the leg like candle drip. I could have cleaned it off in an evening, with a knife and some warm water.

Statia unpacked her bag onto Grata's chest in a row, and put her little knife at the end of it in a fish-skin sheath. She set it out the way I laid out a bench.

The pain came then, a slow, deep clench that began low in my back and wrapped round to the front, and I held the post of Grata's bed until it went.

Statia was pleased. "There. That's one."

Grata shut her counter at the tenth hour. She had kept it open right through the Saturnalia. The front boards went up with a clatter. A carter complained outside, and Grata told him there was wine at the Nola Gate and he could go and be robbed there.

Valens had the mules bedded by the eleventh hour. He stood in Grata's door and looked at me.

"It's begun," I said.

"Since when?"

"Noon."

"Felix said." He turned to Statia. "What's she want?"

"Nothing off you," said Statia.

"Water?"

"There's a well, and I've got arms."

"You'll be no use to anybody," Grata told him, "you'll have that face on you from now till morning, I've watched it on three husbands and one of them mine. Sit on my well and stay out of my road."

He did not go to the well. He crossed to me first and stood close, his arm against mine. Neither of us spoke, and in a while the next pain came, and I got both hands round his forearm and hung on it. It held like a beam. When the pain went he glanced at the marks my nails had left on him.

"Harder, if you want," he said. "That arm's carried lime since dawn."

* * *

*Lamp-lighting*

Grata lit the lamp on the shelf herself, her best, with two spouts, and talked to the goddess while it caught.

"Lucina," she said. "Juno Lucina. You know this house. You took three out of me in it and the youngest feet first, and I gave you a cake for each of them, so you know I pay. This one's not mine, she's Greek, but she's under my roof and she's in my bed, so she's mine tonight. A quick one and a whole one, and there's honey on the cake. Quick and whole and a boy, if she wants a boy, and there's a lamp in it for you as good as this one. Better. I'll go to the forum for it."

"Girl," said Statia.

"Then the lamp's for whole."

She came and drew the pins from my hair. They were bone, and she pulled them singly and dropped them in the fold of her skirt and spread the hair down my back with her fingers. It was wet at the neck. She untied the cord of my tunic and drew it out of its loops and threw it on the bed. After that she hunted the room for knots. She found one in the cord of the shutter and one in the strap of Statia's bag, and she undid the strap while Statia watched her, expressionless.

"There's one in your own belt," Statia said.

Grata undid her belt.

From the bag Statia took a little bundle in a rag, and unwrapped from it a brown stone the size of a walnut, smoothed all over and bound in a net of linen thread. She tied it to my left thigh, up near the groin, under the tunic, winding the thread twice round. When I moved, it rattled. There was a smaller stone loose inside it.

Pliny had a page on these. The eagle carried them up to her nest, he said, and without one she could not lay, and a woman in labor wore one on the same principle. Now it knocked against my leg every time I turned.

The pains came closer. Between them I paced Grata's room from loom to door, and got to know both ends.

"How many have you lost?" I asked Statia, at one of them.

She was rinsing her hands in the basin and did not stop. "Children or mothers?"

"Mothers."

"Some." She flicked her fingers dry onto the floor. "None yet this year."

"Statia," said Grata.

"She asked. I don't tell lies to the ones that ask. Go on to your wall, Greek, and leave the reckoning to me."

The wall had not moved.

Valens carried a jug from the well to the door, and Statia took it off him on the sill and sent him back to the well. The water was cold. In all Pompeii that evening it was the only cold thing, and I drank it standing, and it dropped into me like a stone into a cistern.

At the court door Grata's husband stood, lamp up. I had laid eyes on him perhaps a dozen times in all, and always on his way from one bench to another with a cup; he was a soft, sunburned man with a voice that carried better than he did. He was talking past me to Valens, who had settled on the curb of the well after all. Dice clicked in his fist as he went on.

"Down at Puteoli," he said. "On the fish quay, behind the tunny sheds. A Chaldean. Does the grain ships' stars before they sail. My brother's had him do his. Old as Saturn and mad with it, mutters gibberish to himself over it, not Greek, not Syrian, nothing you ever heard, and then he tells you your day in Greek like a schoolmaster. Cheap, though."

"What would I want with stars?" Valens asked.

"For the boy. You mark the hour he comes, and you take it down to the Chaldean, and he casts it. Everybody does."

"A girl," Statia called over.

"For the girl, then."

"It's not come yet," said Valens.

"Well, when it comes. Mark it, is all I'm saying. You'll want his stars read."

The pain took me then, round the back and through, and I braced on the jamb and leaned my weight into it. When it had gone the husband had gone too, and Grata was saying something about men who stood in doors with their mouths open.

"Mind my counter," she shouted after him. "You've leaned on it every night there is, you could find it blind."

* * *

*Bedtime*

The street put itself to bed. Shutters, a child grizzling and stopping, a cart at the corner with a load of something that clanked; after that, crickets.

The twins' mother came round from the fullery at the end of it, as Grata had promised, with one twin asleep on her collarbone and a sprig of something gray in her spare hand. She would not come in. She held the sprig out to Grata across the sill.

"Dittany," she said. "Cretan. It's the real one, I got it from a sailor's wife, you burn it under her. I burned it under me and look." She hitched the twin. "Two. Took no time."

"You took from one dawn to the next," said Grata. "I held your leg for it."

"It felt like no time."

"It felt like dawn to dawn to my leg." Grata took the dittany. "Go home, you'll wake that one, and if you wake that one you'll wake both, and then the whole street's up and him too, and he'll beat you for it and you'll beat him back on the next feast and nobody gets any sleep from now till the Vinalia."

The woman looked past her at me, propped on my feet by the loom.

"She's carrying wide," she said, delighted. "That's a girl."

Grata sent her home. She put the dittany on the coals of her brazier in the shop, and a sweetish smoke came round into the room and hung under the ceiling.

Statia ate the lentils. Grata's lentil pot sat inside the doorway, out of the way, and Statia lowered herself to the floor next to it and dipped in with three fingers, and talked with her mouth full.

"There's one near the water tower," she said, "calls herself a midwife. Lays them flat. On their backs, like a beetle. Then she wonders why it takes two days." She wiped her fingers on her tunic. "Lays them flat and goes home to her dinner. And her wool's off a black sheep, I know it for a fact. Black wool, at a birth." She dipped again. "Walk."

Passing the stool, I touched the run of glue down its leg. The run was hard and glassy, with a hair caught in it.

"Leave that be," said Statia.

"Somebody glued it too hot."

"My mother glued it. She had it off her mother, and that arm came off under a tanner's wife twice your size. It's held since before Nero. You can mend it when you've had as many on it as I have."

My legs had begun to shake at the knee between the pains, a small fine shaking that took no notice of me. The floor shone where I paced it.

In the court a man's feet crossed the paving, stopped at the well, returned. They halted at the door. They went off. Presently they were there again. The gate to the lane opened and shut, and much later it creaked, and the feet recrossed the court and stopped outside.

"Men out," said Statia. She rose and opened the door. "You. Off my door. Go and do something with your hands."

"I'm doing nothing at your door."

"You're doing it very loud." She shut it on him.

Through the planks he tried again. "Call me if there's anything to carry."

"Nothing wants carrying till it's here," Statia told the door. "After, you can carry it, and welcome, and see how you like it."

There was no air now at all. The lamp's two flames stood up straight on the shelf, and the flies had settled for the night on the ceiling. Grata wet a cloth from the jar and laid it across my nape, and it was warm before it settled. In my freezer at home there was a bag of peas I kept for the back of the neck in August; I had bought it for that purpose only, and never cooked a pea of it in four summers. The cloth slid down my back, and Grata caught it and wet it again.

The pain came, and returned, and my hands were on the wall, and this time I made a noise. It was low and long and I had no say in it.

"Good," said Statia, at my shoulder. "That's the right noise. Make that one."

* * *

*Midnight*

In the court a dry rough scraping kept on, and stopped, and began again.

Between two pains I asked for the door, and Statia let me have it. She held my elbow while I stood in it. The air out there was cooler by a little, and smelled of the well and of split pine from Grata's woodstack. Over the court the sky was thick with stars, and beyond the roofs a dog was barking at nothing, steadily, the way it had barked every night that summer.

A lamp sat on the well curb. Felix was next to it, hunched on the paving, nursing a second lamp in his lap. In the light stood the dun, tied short to the iron ring in Grata's wall. Her eyes were heavy and her lower lip hung loose. She was asleep on her feet.

Valens had her near fore up, tucked between his thighs, the sole of the hoof turned to the light. He had the curved knife. He took a thin paring from the edge of the wall, and it peeled off pale and curled, and dropped. Curls of horn lay everywhere round his feet, a drift of them, like the floor under a lathe. He laid the knife on the curb and drew the rasp across the hoof's rim, once and again, and ran his thumb round to feel what he had done. With the point of the knife he cleaned the cleft of the frog, gently, though it was clean, and blew into it softly, though there was no dust.

"He's done her four times," Felix whispered to me. "Every foot, four times round. Her feet were done at the Kalends. There's nothing left on her to take off. I told him."

"There's a flare on this one," said Valens, without lifting his head.

"There's no flare. He's taking off what isn't there." Felix shifted the lamp. "Worse than a cut, or not so bad? We didn't hear you, did you shout? Will you have it by cockcrow?"

I answered none of it.

Valens lowered the hoof to the stones and ran his hand down the back of the dun's leg from knee to heel. She woke, and blew, and set her weight down on it. He spoke to her too low for me, and then he moved round to the off fore and took that foot up, and she lifted it for him unasked and dozed off again.

The pain caught me there in the doorway. I gripped the doorpost and bent over it, and in the lamplight Valens dropped the hoof and came two steps toward me before Statia said, "Back to your mule," and he stopped where he was. He waited out the pain with me from there. Afterward he returned to the dun and found the foot again.

"How far?" he asked over the hoof.

"Statia says halfway. She said halfway at lamp-lighting."

"Then she's wrong one time or the other." The rasp went across. "She say anything else?"

"She says it's a girl."

"It isn't anything yet."

Felix yawned so wide his jaw clicked, and rested his head against the well. The lamp in his hands tipped and righted.

"Straight."

"It is straight. It's the ground's crooked." The flame steadied. "When can I see it?"

"When it's here."

"That midwife's eaten a whole pot of Grata's lentils, I watched her do it through the window. Does she eat at every birth? Do they have to give her supper? Two years I've slept within smell of Grata's lentils and never had a spoon, and I've asked."

"Ask her again."

"I'm asking you."

The next pain started in my feet and climbed. Statia's grip was on my arm, and the door was shutting, and the last thing out there was the rasp going again, slow, and the lamp jumping in Felix's fists as he jerked awake.

* * *

*Past midnight*

The stool.

Grata behind me, perched on the bed, her knees bracketing my back. Her arms under mine and her hands locked over my breastbone.

"Lucina. Lucina, you heard me."

Statia low in front of me. Her hands.

"Not yet. Noli. Not yet."

"I can't."

"You can. You are. Short breaths on it, like a dog in the sun. So."

"I want to stop."

"You'll stop when it's out."

Oil. Her hand, slick, and her voice going on in Oscan underneath it.

Somebody wiped my mouth.

Water poured into a basin from high up.

The rasp in the court. It stopped.

Statia's face rose into the lamplight. Her mouth was open. The left tooth lying over its neighbor. I fixed on it. The pain was everywhere and had no edges to it, and the tooth had edges. I held on to the tooth. I hated it. I hated it with the whole of me, and I held on.

"Now. With it. With it."

"Iam. Iam."

"Again."

The rasp, outside. Going. Going.

Wool over my face, wet, brown. A sleeve. Mrs. Dunphy wore brown, a brown cardigan gone thin at the elbows, and she was at the board with the chalk in her fingers, crying, the long division half written. Brown. The sleeve went.

Statia said something that was not Latin.

"Wait for it. Wait."

A cart rumbled past, streets off. Outside Felix was asking something, and Valens gave him a single word, and the asking stopped.

"He's coming, she says he's coming. Lucina, you old cow, you took my cake, you keep your word."

"Hush, you'll put her off."

"I'll put the goddess off, and welcome."

Grata's breath at my ear, in, out.

"Lucina. Two cakes. Two, and the lamp."

A sound left me that was nobody's I knew.

"There. There it is. I've the head. I've the head. Stop now. Stop, stop. Little breaths."

The rasp, outside, had stopped.

* * *

*Cockcrow*

A cock went off somewhere toward the forum. A second took it up from nearer, from some yard on our street, and a third farther down, and then they were all at it the length of the town, while the lamp burned low on the shelf and Grata held me up from behind.

Statia said, "Now," and I pushed with everything there was, and burned where he was coming, and then gave. He arrived in one rush, long and slippery and heavy, into her hands, and he was crying before she had him turned. It was a thin, cross, cracked sound, very close, and it filled the room to the ceiling and woke the flies.

Statia lifted her face to me over him and grinned. That came first, before him: her teeth in the lamplight, the crossed pair and the brown one, and below them in her two square hands a creature gray-blue and furious, streaked white and red, with his fists up by his ears and his mouth wide.

"Mas," she said. "Carried wide, the little liar."

Behind me Grata held me so tight there was no breath to be had. "A boy. Lucina, a boy. A cake and honey and a lamp, I'll have to find a lamp now. A boy."

Statia laid him on my belly while she tied the cord. She tied it with red wool twice over and cut beyond the tie with a little knife. He lay on me wet and hot and heavier than anything that size had any right to be, and kept on telling us all about it. I found his back with my hand. It rose and fell under my fingers, and his spine was all there, a row of small hard beads.

Statia took him up again, and spread a cloth on the floor at the stool's feet, and laid him down on it on his back.

"My man wears that to the Floralia," said Grata.

"It's a cloth now. Fetch the father."

Grata went to the door and opened it. Valens was right there. Behind him on the paving the rasp lay by the mule's hooves, and Felix curled asleep beside the well with his mouth agape.

He took one step in and stopped.

"Lift him," Statia said.

His gaze went from the child on the floor to me on the stool, and held.

"He's yours. Lift him up."

He crouched by the cloth. His hands went out and hung above the child, not touching. They were big and scored with old cuts. The child cried up at them.

"He'll not break," said Statia. "Here. Give me that one." She caught one of the hands by the wrist as if it were a tool she was lending him, and fitted it under the child's head, and spread the fingers. "That's the neck, that's the head. That holds him. Now this one." She pushed the other in under the small of the back, farther, until the child's bottom sat in his palm. "There. Up."

He rose with him too fast, in a single heave, and froze. He held the child away from himself on straight arms, high, level with his own face, with his elbows locked and his shoulders up round his ears, and he was not breathing. It was how a man held a jar he had caught just short of the stones, in the instant before he knew whether it was whole.

The child stopped crying. His eyes came open, or nearly, and looked at nothing.

"In," said Statia. "In to you. Bring him in."

Valens drew the child down and in against himself till he lay in the bend of one arm, the other palm spread over him.

"Whole?" he asked me.

"Whole," said Statia. "Look for yourself."

"Whole," I said.

"Now give him here. There's work yet. Sit, before you drop him."

He sank where he stood, onto the ground beside the stool with his back to Grata's wall, and did not give him back. The child lay on his forearm with its head in his palm. He studied the face, and then the hands, which had worked free of the cloth and were opening and shutting on the air, and then the face again.

"He's cold."

"He's wet," said Statia. "Give him here and he'll be neither."

In the end she had to unfasten the child from him, finger by finger.

My legs began to shake, and the stool rocked under me. Grata climbed down to the front of me and pinned my knees with her palms till it passed.

Outside the cocks were still at it.

* * *

*After the cocks*

The afterbirth came, and Statia held it to the lamp the way Grata judged fish, and was satisfied.

She washed him in warm water from the basin, quickly, holding him along her forearm with his head cupped in her palm, and he hated it and said so. She rubbed fine salt from a dish into every fold of him, to close his skin, and then oil, and then she bound him. She wound him in long bands of soft wool with his arms down by his sides and his legs straight, turn on turn, until he was a tight white parcel, red and furious at the top end, and she laid him beside me on Grata's bed, inside my arm.

I lay and watched him. He gazed back with gray eyes that had no use for anything yet.

I was tired and it was hot and I wanted water and nobody had brought any, so I waited.

He turned his face against me and gaped on my arm, and found nothing there, and complained. I pulled the neck of the tunic down.

"Not that," said Statia from the floor. "Not yet. That first milk's thick, it's no good to him, it's cheese, it'll sit in him and curdle. Honey on a finger for now. Grata knows a girl two streets over with milk to spare, she'll have him three days, and after that you."

"No."

"You'd kill him on his first day, would you?"

"Give me the honey if you like. He's having this."

"Greeks," said Statia, to Grata.

"Let her," Grata said. "She's mended every pot on this street with stuff she makes up as she goes, and none of them's come apart on me yet. Let her."

He didn't know the way of it, and I knew little more. He missed, and roared, and missed again, and then of a sudden he had it, and pulled, hard, with a strength out of all proportion, and his eyes closed. Statia watched, mouth pursed, and let the cheese go.

Valens appeared from the court with a cup. Nobody had sent him. It was from the well, cold, and he did not hand it to me, because both my arms were full; he brought the rim to my lips and tipped it for me, slowly, a sip and then another, and took it away when I had had enough, and stood holding the empty cup. A drop of it had fallen on the child's cheek. The child screwed up his face at it and kept his hold.

Grata brought a basin and washed my legs from the knee down, in water that had been warm an hour ago, and talked throughout.

"You did it quiet, for a Greek, I'll say that for you. Her from the fullery had the street out of bed with her two, and the aedile's men round to see who was killed, and you only the once, at the end, and that one was worth hearing, they'll have had that at the amphitheater. Now I've a lamp to find. The good ones are all at that thief behind the forum who wants a day's takings for a decent lamp, and he'll have them off me, I promised, she heard me." She wrung out the cloth. Her eyes were wet, and she was cross about it, and blamed the dittany.

Grata crossed her shop, took down one shutter board and stuck her head out, though there was not a soul to hear. "A boy," she said. "And the mother whole, and him big as a calf, and the baker can have that with his bread." She put the board up again.

Her husband followed her in from the shop with his lamp burned out and his tunic on inside out. He blinked at the bed, and then at Valens, who was on the sill again, empty-handed.

"Did you get the hour?"

"The cocks," said Valens.

"Which cock?"

"All of them."

The husband appeared to think that would do. He went to find his bed.

"And you," Grata told Valens, "can put that face away, you're stood there like somebody led you home a horse you never bid on and you can't find the halter. Go and sleep an hour on my bench. You'll sleep nowhere, I know it, but it's said."

Felix turned up at the door.

"Can I see him? Did he come at the cocks? Has he got a voice on him? I heard a voice."

"He's got a voice," Grata said. "Wash your face first, there's a stable on it."

He came anyway, as far as the bedpost, and looked at the parcel at my breast, and Grata had to call him twice before he heard her.

* * *

*First light*

Gray showed in the window slit, and the lamp looked yellow and foolish against it, and Grata pinched it out.

Statia went. She took her boy and her stool and the stone off my leg. On the sill she stopped to tie her bag strap again, a good hard knot, and then spoke to me.

"Round about midnight," she said, "you had a look on you like you'd have my eyes out with your thumbs."

"Only at midnight?"

"Don't flatter yourself. They all get it, about then. It's how I know it's going right." She hoisted the bag. "When they're fond of me at midnight, I start to worry."

Her boy went first with the stool, and she behind him, and I heard her in the shop telling Grata it had been wide regardless, and a boy was no excuse.

Valens had settled beside the bed, his back propped on the wall. The child was in his arm now.

Lying there, I had the top of the child's head in view in the early gray, the dark hair on it still stuck flat in whorls from the washing. Toward the brow there was a spot where the bones had not yet come together. They had been made in pieces and left open at the joins, so that they could ride over one another on the way out and spring back after, and the skin over the gap was so thin that it lifted and settled with his heart, if I kept still and watched. Nobody had to mend it.

"You'll not name him till the ninth day, like decent people," Grata told us both from the door, "and I'll hear it then along with the street and look surprised."

She went out. Her steps crossed the shop, and the shutter boards started coming off, one, and then another.

"The dun's feet," I said, once she had gone.

"Done."

"Felix says four times."

"Felix sleeps." He watched the child for a while. "She'll walk tender on the paving a day or two. It'll do her no harm."

"Were you afraid?"

He moved his thumb on the bands, where the child's chest was. "She stood on three legs from lamp-lighting on. She'll be stiff."

He spoke once more, low, to the child. "Lucius."

"Lucius," I said, and the child slept through us both.

* * *

*Morning*

I was mostly asleep. The street was at its work beyond the wall, and out front Grata was telling a carter he could have the news with his wine for the one copper, and none of it on credit. The light was full in the window slit. Down the lane the string clattered past for the gate with Felix shouting at it, and one mule's hooves went short and careful on the stones. Somebody had my right wrist.

It was Valens, on the bed beside me. The child slept against my left side. Valens had my hand palm down on his knee. He was working the watch's little crown a click at a time, and I felt each click in the small bones of my wrist. When it would turn no farther he tucked my hand under the sheet.
