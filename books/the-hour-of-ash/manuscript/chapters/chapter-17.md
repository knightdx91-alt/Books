The boards left their dust on my fingers. Fine and gray, faintly greasy, the dust of old wax in a shut room; under it the wood of the tag had been worn smooth by thumbs. The books lay flat in their pigeonholes, five and six to a hole. Each was a stack of boards bound along one side by a cord, and from every cord a wooden tag hung on a loop.

Each tag carried two names cut short, and a numeral. In a colony two names made a year.

Behind me the clerk was eating figs.

A fig cake sat on the table by his elbow, the kind the farm women pressed in a mold and sold off a cloth in the market: a brown brick with the shapes of the fruit still raised on its face. He dug them out singly with the blunt end of his stylus. When we arrived he was digging, and while Plautia gave him her name and her father's, and he was digging still. The gray one, then. We had wanted the other.

"When the governor came back," he said, "the colony's old books were carted over here, and nobody's sorted them since."

"Are they in this office?" said Plautia.

"They're in this office." He said it to the cake. "The old years are at the back, lady. Up the ladder."

"Then you will go up the ladder. I will sit here in the light until you are down again."

"There's no one but me in today to mind the door."

"I will mind your door."

"The boys race out on the Isthmus this morning, lady. There's not a soul left in Corinth to come through it."

"It will hardly tax me."

He found another fig. Plautia faced him across the table, on the bench, with her veil drawn forward and her hands in her lap, and then her hands were out of her lap and there was a bundle in a napkin on the table between them. It clinked faintly on the wood. The clerk raised one corner of the napkin with his stylus. Silver, a ladle and two spoons, the spoon handles ending in swans' heads. He let the corner fall back.

Her mother's. She had told me so at the brother's door in the dark, while the bearers were lifting her, in the voice she might have used to tell me the spoons were cold. The bearers had carried her through a city that had emptied itself for the games and barred its doors behind it: shutters down along every street, a fuller's vats standing full and untrodden, a dog asleep in the middle of the paving and not troubling to move for a litter, and at the forum's edge the fountain pouring into its basins with no one there to fill a jar.

The clerk wrapped the napkin round them twice and put it under the fig cake. He rose, taking the cake.

"The year?"

"The year my father gave me in marriage. Before the great fire at Rome. Find me the instrument of the dowry, with his seal on it, and bring it out here into the light, and I will read it with you."

"Lady." He took himself and his cake off to the store behind.

I heard the ladder take his weight. A rung creaked, and another, and high up something heavy slid along a shelf.

"Which year did you say?" His voice dropped to us from up near the roof, flat.

"A year before the fire," said Plautia.

"There's three books of it."

"Bring all three."

The ladder said what he thought of that.

I went on reading tags.

They ran left to right, and shelf by shelf downward, in years, and inside each year in the order of the numerals. *P. VETT. L. CANIN.*, Plautia had said through the litter's curtain that morning. Vettius and Caninius, the year Nero came. The first book of the year was the one, the magistrate's, the games and everything else he had signed. I found the year two holes down from where my hand was. *VETT. CANIN. II.* Then *III.* Then *IIII.* No first.

In its place lay a book with another pair of names on the tag, a year I had already passed.

In the basement of the museum, in the storerooms, a shelf told you more by its order than by any one label: a run of accession numbers going by in their sequence, year and lot and piece, and then a number out of its place, louder than anything written on it. A thing that was lost was nearly always only put back wrong.

I retraced the shelves to the passed year. There, between its own second and fourth books, sat a book whose tag said *VETT. CANIN. I.*

Nobody had taken it anywhere. A copyist would have had a long walk.

"Well?" said Plautia, low, from the table. Overhead the ladder creaked.

"It is here. In the wrong year."

"Put there by whom?"

"Somebody who wanted it lost and knew where to find it again."

"Be quick," said Plautia, "before he wants it."

I slid it free. It weighed more than I wanted, a dozen boards deep, each a finger thick, and the cord threaded the spine and was knotted at its head. The knot was buried in a lump of red wax with two seals pressed into it, a pair: a ship, and a head in a helmet. I propped the book on my hip and opened the front board.

Inside, the wood was hollowed into a shallow tray and the tray was filled with black wax, and the wax was crowded with a small, slanting hand that took no trouble for anybody. The beginning of the year. Oaths. A list of names. The hire of a bath. On the second board, a contract for road gravel.

The clerk said something in the store, to the ladder or the shelves.

I turned two boards together, then another pair. My hands were steady. My bladder was another matter. I had drunk a beaker of water in the brother's kitchen before dawn because Myrrhine had pressed it on me, and it had waited the length of the streets for this moment to want out.

The fifth board and the sixth closed on each other, written on their inner faces only, and a narrow strip of wood between them had been left raised at the edges so the two faces would not touch. I opened them.

The writing on these was larger. Somebody had taken pains for the emperor's year. A day. A magistrate's name in full. Then, near the top of the left face, the letters came one under another down the wax, and I had to read them again; the stroke for the *L* in that hand was barely a stroke at all.

*C. IVLIVS. VALENS.*

Under it the rest went on in the same hand: a lanista's name, the emperor's, the arena, and *liber*, and the witnesses, nine names, the last of them squeezed in where the space ran out.

The ladder creaked. He was coming down.

I closed the leaf, and the book over it, and slid the book back into its wrong place, with my hand still inside the front board, keeping the place.

The clerk emerged with three books stacked against his hip and the cake balanced on his free palm, and a cobweb across his hair. He lowered the books to the table in front of Plautia, and sat.

"It'll be the middle one. Dowries are the middle of a year. Everybody marries in the spring."

"I was married in the autumn."

"Then the last." He worked it free from beneath the others and untied nothing.

"The seal is my father's," said Plautia. "I know it from here. Read it to me."

"You read, lady."

"I can read. I wish to hear it. Every line, and the witnesses after, slowly, so that my woman can write down what I tell her to."

He freed the cord. He opened the boards and found the place with his stylus. With the same stylus he dug out a fig and ate it, and began.

"*Plautia, daughter of Sextus*," he read, chewing. "*Gave*." He swallowed. "*In the name of dowry*."

I took the knife out of the writing case.

It was at the bottom, beneath the pens and the letters, in a sheath of fish-skin: a little blade with a bone handle, for cutting pens. Myrrhine had packed the case three times over in the lamplight that morning, each time tighter, while Plautia was dressed in the next room. Between the packings she had marched me to and fro behind an empty chair to cure me of her mistress's hem. "Further back. Further. She gets down on the right, always, from a child; her nurse was left-handed, and nobody has ever told her otherwise." Then the case again. "The knife is her grandfather's," she had said, and put it in last, at the bottom, and laid the reeds on top. "Everything in this case comes home tonight. If it goes to anybody else, I shall know whose house."

"*A slave girl, Syrian, named Doris, aged eleven*," the clerk read. "*Valued at*." He named a figure and ate a fig.

"Again. The advocate has it otherwise," Plautia said.

He read it over, slower, with the fig in his cheek, as to a child who had asked twice.

I eased the book partway out of its hole again. The cord threaded every board at the spine, around and through. I put the blade to it below the boards, as far from the seals as it ran.

The cord was linen, twisted hard and waxed, and the knife was small. I sawed. Nothing. I sawed again and felt the twist begin to go, one strand and then the next, and the third, rasping like a nail drawn down a bedsheet.

"*Earrings, two pairs, gold, with drop pearls*." A fig. "*One bronze lampstand on three feet, a lion's paws*."

The cord parted with no warning, and the book slid in my hands and the edge of a board knocked against the shelf.

The clerk stopped reading.

"My woman has dropped her pen. She is clumsy," Plautia said. "I was warned. You were at the lion's paws."

He found his place again with the stylus. His head had not moved.

I worked the cut end upward through the holes with my fingers, through the twelfth board and the eleventh, upward, toward the front. It came stiffly. It had lain in those holes five years and taken their shape.

"Whose hand is it written in?" said Plautia.

"A clerk's." He turned the board. "*Witnesses*." He began on them.

Seventh board, sixth, fifth. The fifth and sixth parted from the rest together, the leaf, closed on itself. I put it into the case among Plautia's letters and laid the pens over it.

Now the cord needed threading down again through the six boards behind, hole by hole, and every hole fought it. Plautia's mother's name went by, and her mother's brother's, and a Gnaeus somebody. A drop of sweat crept down the inside of my arm and stopped at the gold on my wrist. I threaded the last hole. Two ends of cord stood out at the bottom of the book. I tied them in a reef knot, pulled it down hard, and pushed it in against the boards.

It showed. It was a second knot on a book that had one, small and pale and naked, with no wax on it, and a clean cut end of linen sticking up from it. Anybody who turned that book over would see it, and anybody who saw it would know. There was no half of that. It was done well or it was a botch, and it was a botch.

I returned the book to the wrong year, knot to the back.

"*Done at Corinth*," said the clerk, and gave the day.

"Is that seal my father's," said Plautia, "or his freedman's?"

The clerk tipped the book to the open door and peered at the wax. "Your father's."

"By what?"

"His freedman's had a chip out of the rim." He dug out a fig.

Feet hurried past the scaffold outside, and a boy in a short tunic was in the doorway, panting, the road's dust gray on his shins. His eyes traveled the room and settled on the clerk.

"From the legate Arrius Celer," he said. "He's outside, and he'll not come in under the poles. He wants Vettius's first book brought out to him, for the copyist."

The clerk sat a while with a fig on the end of his stylus.

"Now?"

"He's to be back at the governor's side before the men's race."

"Tell the legate," said Plautia, without turning her head, "that the lady Plautia is reading her father's seal, and will come out when she has read it."

The boy stared at her veil. "Lady." The doorway was empty again.

"He has chosen the same morning we chose," Plautia said to me, low, "and for the same reason. He will not walk under a ladder. Nor a builder's frame. He once walked the whole forum round, in the rain, sooner than pass under a painter's plank."

The clerk ate the fig. He left his stool for the shelves. His hand found the wrong year's hole at the first try; he drew the book from it and clamped it to his side, spine inward.

That morning, on the brother's roof, with the sky still gray, Plautia had stood at the parapet in her cloak and watched the procession go out along the street below to the eastern gate: the proconsul's chair, the lictors, the magistrates on their horses, the staff behind. She had pointed, once. A man in a white toga, very new, on a gray, with his head turned to the man beside him and laughing. A boy on a pony at his stirrup, in a tunic so clean it showed in that light.

"Is that the boy?" I had said.

"That is his tunic. I did not choose it." Plautia had stood until the street had emptied. Then she had gone in to be dressed.

"The lane door, lady," he told Plautia, in passing, "is by the oven, if you'd sooner not be kept. He'll keep you till noon." He bore it off to the colonnade.

Plautia stood up.

"I entered by the front," she said. "I will leave by it. Carry my case out to the lane, and wait there for the litter."

The litter was in the colonnade, where she had left it, a step from the man she meant. My way lay through the store, under the ladder, with the case against my side.

* * *

The oven was built onto the office's rear wall, and the wall itself was warm to the shoulder, and the lane in front of it smelled of hot brick and scorched bran. The step down had a hollow worn in it. Below it stood Valens, with a baker's peel in his hands and his forearms white with flour, which was the only disguise anybody had been able to think of for him, and it fooled nobody who had ever seen a baker.

The baker stood at the mouth of his oven, raking, his back turned. He was singing a song of endless verses and no tune.

Plautia's brother had seen to him. On the third evening after the plan the brother had come home from the forum and said only, "The baker will want a loaf's worth of trouble and no more," and had gone in to his dinner. What it cost the house nobody told me. The baker had a new leather apron that week, and greeted the house porter by name.

Valens read my face before he read the case.

"Here," I said.

I opened the case on the step and took the leaf from among the pens. He wiped one hand on his thigh, then the other. The flour stayed. He took the leaf by its edges, a hand to each.

"Open it," I said. "The wax will stand it. Do not touch the writing."

The boards parted between his hands.

It was dim in the lane, with the oven's glow on one side of us and a neighbor's wall close on the other, and the black wax gave back almost nothing. He tipped the leaf toward the oven. His mouth moved on the first word, and then it stopped moving, and he read the rest of it with his lips shut. He read all of it. He read the witnesses.

"Nine," he said.

"Nine names."

"Three of them are dead."

He shut it.

"And the book?" he said.

"A knot, where there should be none. Anybody who turns it over will see it."

"When?"

"Whenever somebody wants to look."

"He is in the colonnade," I said. "Celer. The clerk has taken the book out to him. With the knot in it. Plautia has gone out to him by the front."

"Alone."

"With her bearers."

"Her bearers are hired men off the Kenchreai road. They'd carry her off a cliff for him, if he paid."

The song broke off. The baker had turned round with his rake and was staring beyond us at the step, and I turned too.

The clerk stood in the back doorway. He had crossed the office for his bread, as he must have done each morning of his life at that hour, with a coin in his fingers ready for the baker; and he stood now on the worn step with his coin still raised, staring at what Valens held.

While the baker's fire slumped and settled in the oven, none of us moved.

The clerk stepped down to us. He had the cobweb still across his hair.

"That's out of the emperor's year," he said.

"It's mine."

Valens held it in his two floury hands, shut, chest-high, in plain view of the clerk, and he watched the clerk's hands. They were busy holding up a coin for the baker. The clerk's sandals were old, one strap mended in a different leather, and his jaw still worked at a fig.

The clerk held the coin out past us to the baker. "Two of the round ones."

The baker had the coin off him and turned to his shelf.

"He's not opened it," said the clerk to nobody, waiting. "He'll not open it. He has a copyist who'll keep it under his bed till everybody's forgotten it. He's paid the copyist a year ahead." He found a seed in a back tooth with his tongue. "You'd want to be gone before somebody decides to remember it."

"Today," said Valens.

"I'd not know about that." The baker put two loaves into his hands, hot, and he juggled them, one hand to the other. "My grandson runs with the boys today. My daughter's boy. Twelve. He'll have run by now." He squinted at the strip of sky between the roofs, as though the race might be up there. "I'd have liked to see him lose."

"Lose?" I said.

"His father thinks he'll run at Olympia. He's told the whole lane. If the boy loses today there'll be peace in my daughter's house till the grapes are in."

He took his loaves up the step, and the office had him again.

The baker returned to his oven and rested on his rake. "Two round ones each day since the Saturnalia," he said, "and the coin held up just so, and not one word to me about rain or sun. You're to stand there till noon, the brother said."

Valens said, "It's near enough noon."

My knees had gone loose. The step took me, with the case in my lap.

"He saw it," I said. "He saw your hands. He will walk out there with his bread and tell him."

"He'll eat his bread."

"How can you know that?"

"Look at him." Valens still faced the empty doorway. "He came out for bread with that man in the colonnade waiting on him. That's a man who's had enough of the day."

He undid his tunic at the neck and slid the leaf inside it, flat, against his chest, and pulled his belt tighter over it so that it rode under the cloth and could not slip. He laid his palm on it through the cloth.

The baker had turned away again and taken up his song where he had left it.

"Get down to Felix," Valens told me.

Before sunrise Felix had taken the mules down to Lechaion with the household's freedman: the dun on the front rope, the black, and the two others nobody had wanted at any price, with my bag and his own across the dun's withers, and the fig in its jar. He had gone protesting. "It's the boys' day," he had called at the gate, "the boys, and I'm to sit on a quay with mules? Who'll tell me who won? Do they cry it out at Lechaion when somebody wins?" and the answer was "Ask a sailor," and Felix had trudged off putting his questions to the black instead.

"Where are you going?" I said.

"Round."

"To him."

"She's got her face to him, with hired men round her and nobody at her shoulder. I walked at her back for two summers and two winters." He gave the baker his peel. The baker took it without stopping his song. "The ship's the one with the goose carved on her stern. Felix knows it. It goes out on the afternoon breeze, whoever's on it."

He untied the sacking from round his waist and dropped it by my feet.

"Valens," I said. "He knows your face."

"He can look at it, and leave her be."

He made for the colonnade with the flour still on his arms, and I stayed on the step through another of the baker's verses. I rose with Plautia's case and followed.

* * *

The lane let me out by the colonnade's last column, into shade and then a long slope of sun. The colonnade ran away from me along the forum's whole south side, two rows of columns deep, with doors behind them, all shut for the games but one.

In front of that one stood the scaffold. Men had been patching the stucco on two columns where the earthquakes had cracked it, and they had built up round them a frame of fir poles lashed with rawhide, planks laid across at the height of a man's head and again above that, a lime bucket left on the upper planks with its rope coiled on top. The lashings had been put on wet and had dried tight as iron. A mule could have stood on it.

The clerk was under it, in his doorway, eating his bread.

Plautia stood a few paces out from him, under the roof, veiled, her litter grounded behind her and its four bearers sitting on the step in a row. Valens had got there first. He had walked in under the planks and stopped at her back, half a pace to her left, with the flour on his arms.

Beyond the scaffold's end the forum paving was white in the sun, and on the paving stood Celer.

He was the man I had watched from the roof: the toga, brilliantly new, with the dust of the Isthmus road along the bottom of it in a brown band a hand deep. Close to, he was older than his laughing had been, and very carefully shaved, and his ears jutted from his head like the handles of a cup. His litter waited behind him with its curtains looped up. One slave held a sunshade over him and had let it slip. Another, the boy who had run in to the clerk, stood at his elbow with Vettius's book hugged to his chest in both arms, spine outward.

Behind the litter stood two other men. One was a stranger. The other had a seam of new skin above his left ear, pink and puckered, where nobody had stitched it.

I stopped at Plautia's litter, by the bearers, with the case.

"There," Celer was saying. "I told the lady Plautia. I said, he'll be about somewhere close, he always was, and here you are." His voice was pleasant and unhurried, the voice of a man telling a story to listeners who had begged for it. "Lupus. Free air's put flesh on you."

"Legate," said Valens.

"Oh, not that. We were never that, were we, you and I? Come out where I can see you properly." He waved at the sunlight beside him. "No? You'll forgive me not coming in. I don't go under those."

The man with the seam had found me. He touched two fingers to the place above his ear.

"Mistress," he said, pleasantly.

"The lady's come to read her own dowry," Celer went on, "and I've told her she ought to have sent to me for it. I'd have had it copied out fair for her, in a good hand, and sent round to her brother's with a slave. We'd have done that, wouldn't we, Onesimus?"

"Lady didn't ask," the clerk said, his mouth full of bread.

"I hope you found it all in order," Celer said to her. "Your father's people were always so correct."

"I found his seal," said Plautia. "It is the one mark in all that book that is his. Did he eat, this morning, before you left him?"

The question carried over the scaffold's shadow into the sun, short and level.

"Like a wolf." Celer smiled at her straight away, as if she had given him a present. "Bread and honey, and most of my bread besides. He's with my steward at the rail, in the new tunic, and he's been told he may shout as loud as he likes, and the Corinthian boy's the favorite, so he'll shout himself hoarse."

"He will be hoarse already. He shouts before anybody has told him he may, and he has done so since he could stand, and tonight his nurse will give him honey in warm water, too hot, and he will not drink it."

"His nurse knows her business," said Celer. "I'm never in the nursery. Come out tomorrow and sit with us, why don't you? Right at the governor's back, where everybody can see how well we all get on. He'd like that."

"Will you let me speak to him?"

"Of course," said Celer. "Afterward. When all this has been tidied away."

He let that lie in the sun, and looked past her, and past Valens, to me.

"And this will be Mara."

My name, in his mouth, in that pleasant voice. It had come to him from the road below Delphi, with that ear.

"I write for the lady." My Latin was stiff, and too quick.

"So I hear. In a Latin they must teach very carefully at Syracuse." He let that go by, smiling. "My man tells me you carry something heavy under your dress, too, that shines. I'd like to see it one day. And she's given you the gold off her arm." His eyes rested on the band at my wrist. "She would. She always gave away what was given her. Didn't you, Plautia? One loved her for it."

Under the veil, Plautia's chin came up a finger's breadth.

Celer turned back to Valens. He came a step nearer, and another, to the very end of the scaffold, until the toes of his good shoes were on the edge of the shadow the poles threw on the paving, and stopped there. One hand lifted, open and easy, as it must have reached for the shoulders and forearms of a thousand men in that forum, to take a fellow by the elbow and walk him a pace or two aside and tell him something for his own good; and it hung there in the sun, a long arm's length short of Valens's arm, with the planks and the rawhide and the coiled rope over the space between them; and the shadow of a pole lay across his fingers; and he looked at his hand there, in the bar of shade, as though somebody else had put it out, and drew it in.

"You were the best money I ever laid out, Lupus," he said. "Two years at her back, and never a scratch on the lady."

"You hired me," said Valens.

"Did I?" said Celer, kindly. "Well. We'll let the lawyers argue that. We could make all of this very easy, you and I. Ships leave Lechaion every day now the sea's open. If a man crossed to Italy and settled, and grew old in the sun with a vineyard and somebody to write his letters for him, I can't see why anybody in Achaea would ever need to say his name again. My steward put up some notices, last year. Stewards will. Notices come down."

Valens stood with his floured hands hanging at his sides. Under the flour on his tunic, where the leaf rode, the cloth sat flat and square if you knew to look for it. I knew. Celer was looking at his face.

"And the paper in Rome," said Valens.

"Papers." Celer shook out a fold of the toga where it had creased over his arm. "There's a bill of sale with the lanista's mark on it, and there's a line in a magistrate's book, and one says one thing and one says the other, and a judge goes to sleep after his lunch and wakes up and chooses." He put out his hand again, sideways, and laid it flat on the book in his slave's arms, and patted it. "Papers are no more than that. You know that well enough. You never needed them on your mountain."

The clerk took another bite of his bread.

"Her brother's paying my road," said Valens. "To Rome."

The bearers on the step had left off their talk. Among the temples a donkey was complaining about something, and far beyond it, past the city, past the walls and the long road and the trees, not a sound reached us of the boys running.

Celer smiled at him.

"Then we'll see each other in Rome," he said. "All of us. I'm told it's very fine in the spring." He glanced at the sun's height over the temple roofs. "And if I'm not at the governor's elbow before the men run, he'll think I've died, and give my seat to somebody he likes better. Onesimus."

"Legate."

"Shut up your door and go out and see your boy. Go on. Nobody's going to rob the proconsul this morning."

"He'll have run."

"Then go and hear who won it. You'll want my ring on something, for the book?"

"Not today, legate."

"Splendid man." He was already turning to his litter. "Come and see me after the games, Lupus. Come to the house, at the second hour, any morning. Bring Mara." He settled himself into the cushions, and the slave with the sunshade lost it altogether and had to run after it. "We'll all dine. Plautia, my mother asks after you."

Plautia said, "Let her go on asking."

The bearers lifted. The shortest way from where he sat to the street he wanted lay straight down the colonnade, in the shade, under six paces of planking that would have carried a loaded cart. His men carried him round instead, through the white sun of the forum, round the scaffold's end and in again under the roof beyond it, the boy jogging beside them; and the book went with him, lighter by two boards, while behind the curtains he talked to somebody about the heat.

Celer's seamed man went last. Where the colonnade turned, he looked round once.

The clerk finished his bread. He fetched his cake from inside, with a key on a thong. He pulled the door to, locked it, and tried it. He eyed us under his scaffold like a bench somebody had left in his way.

"If anybody asks me tonight," he said, "the book left here for the copyist, and I was at the Isthmus by noon. You were never in."

"The spoons were my mother's," Plautia told him.

"My wife will have them on a cloth in the market by the Kalends."

"Tell her the swans are Sicyonian work, and she is not to let them go for less than that."

He dug a fig out of the cake with his thumb, now the stylus was locked inside, and ate it. "If you hear who won the boys, send me word."

"I hope he lost," I said.

"Gods hear you."

He left under the columns, by the eastern road.

* * *

Plautia stood where she was until the litter had left the forum by its farthest corner. Then she faced us, and put back her veil, and her face under it was perfectly dry and very white.

"When the praetor names our day, a letter will come from this house," she said to Valens. "Where will the letter find you?"

"Puteoli. After that, I can't say."

"Puteoli, then, and after that the gods." She looked at the flour on his arms. "You came out under the poles."

"You were by yourself."

"I have been by myself in front of that man since I was sixteen, Valens, and I have generally managed to stand up. And you told him Rome. To his face, in the forum, with his men behind him."

"That was where he was standing."

She put out her hand for the case.

I gave it to her. "There is a knife in the bottom of it," I said, "under everything. It is a good knife. It cut the cord."

Plautia opened the case, and moved the pens aside with a fingertip, and found the knife in its fish-skin. She shut the case again on her letters and gave me the knife.

"It belongs with whoever knows what it is for."

"Myrrhine said it was your grandfather's."

"Myrrhine will find you," Plautia said. "She finds everything. You will write to me from Italy, in Latin, so that my brother cannot read it over my shoulder; his Latin is worse than yours, and he will not ask for help with it. Will you?"

"I will write."

"Did you stand on my hem?"

"Twice. In the street, in the dark."

"Good," she said, "she will be content," and the bearers rose together, and she climbed into the litter from the right, the side Myrrhine had shown me, and her curtain stayed down.

I pushed the knife into my belt.

"What was it she gave you?" said Valens.

"Her grandfather's knife."

"Her woman will be on the quay before us." He looked once round the empty forum. "Walk."

We left the colonnade for the sun.

"When will he open it?" I said.

"He's at the Isthmus till dark. Then he goes home and eats. Then he wants his book."

"And then?"

"By then we're a day out to sea, wind willing."

"The three who are dead," I said. "Who were they?"

"The doctor at the school. He sewed me up for six years. An oil-seller who had the stall by the gate. A trainer who had me from a boy." He walked on some paces. "The other six are strangers to me."

The forum was a long white floor with nobody on it but statues and pigeons, and a woman asleep against the base of an emperor with her basket in her lap. At its north side the ground fell away to the harbor road, and at the head of it stood the arch, with a bronze chariot on top of it and the horses' gilding gone green along their bellies.

Celer's man, the one with the ear, sat on the rim of the fountain basin next to the arch, in the shade, with his feet in the water.

He saw us and lifted one hand, friendly, as a neighbor might from his own doorstep.

"Keep walking. Don't look at him."

"He is watching which road."

"Let him watch it. It's the only road."

We passed beneath the arch, and him, so near I heard the water move round his ankles. My hands had begun to shake. I put them into the folds of Myrrhine's dress, and we went on down the first broad step, and the street took us.

The road to Lechaion fell from the city in long steps, broad as a room, with colonnades along both sides and shops behind the columns, and up those steps from the harbor poured the whole of the morning's ship. They came in a crowd, everybody talking: Italians in traveling hats, a family with a goat, a priest with a box on a slave's head, and in the middle of them, taller than everyone round them by a head, six or seven athletes in a knot round their trainer. They were young, enormous, oiled for the road, with their strigils and flasks hanging off rings at their belts and clinking as they climbed. The biggest had ears like knots of dough. The trainer was a little bald man who climbed the steps backward ahead of them, talking, and missed no footing that I saw.

Boys worked the crowd with pine. They had armfuls of it, cut that morning, the green twists of it plaited into crowns. "The god's pine! The winners' pine! Wear what the winners wear!" One pushed a crown at the athletes and was cuffed, cheerfully, by the trainer. Another danced down a step ahead of me. "Crown, mistress? For your man? Put it on him and they'll think he won something!"

"He has," I said, and the boy dived into the crowd after somebody richer.

By the following column a man had a tray of new silver slung from a strap and was shouting it. "Judaea taken! The emperor's new money, from Rome, for the triumph! See her weep!" On every coin on the tray a woman sat in the dust beneath a palm with her head in her hand, and a soldier stood over her, and the edges of the coins were still sharp from the die. A sailor paid for one and stowed it in his belt.

A sausage-man had set up his brazier against a column at the turn of the steps. The coils on his grill spat and smoked, filling the colonnade with the smell of fat and cumin. He banged his tongs on the grill. "Lucanian, all of them! Not for the athletes, the athletes aren't let have them, their trainers say so! Everybody else!"

We pushed down against all of it. Shoulders knocked mine. A hat brim caught my cheek. Valens went first and the crowd opened in front of him without anybody seeming to decide to, and shut behind me before I was through.

The trainer saw Valens coming and stopped dead, so that the nearest of his boxers walked into his back.

"Murmillo," he said, in the Greek of Alexandria, to Valens's chest. "You come down a stair like one. Whose school?"

"I lead mules." Valens went by him.

"Mules!" The trainer turned to his boxers, delighted, walking backward again. "He leads mules, he says. Did you hear him? Mules!"

At the turn by the sausage-man I stopped.

"Valens."

He was back beside me at once, searching the crowd above us. "Him?"

"I have to make water."

He stared.

"I have had to since the ladder. There is a latrine." I pointed. "Behind those shops. There was a sign."

There was. Inside, a marble seat ran round three walls, cut with a row of holes, and water ran in a channel at our feet, and a stick with a sponge on it stood in a pot at the entrance; and on the bench, already, a fat man in a festival wreath and two of the athletes, elbow to elbow, discussing the javelin. None of them so much as glanced at me. I took the last hole along and tried to think of nothing, and the water in the channel ran by, and the fat man explained to the athletes about the javelin. He broke off and called along the bench to me.

"Mistress. Would you say a man throws farther with the wreath on him, or off?"

"Off."

"You see," he told the athletes, and went on. I finished, and it was slow, and then it was over.

Valens was at the door with a crown of pine in his fist.

"For Felix," he said. "He missed the boys."

"Now? You bought it now?"

"He'll ask who won." He scanned the climb behind us. "We'll not know. He can have this."

The fountain man had vanished from the climb behind us, or was hidden in it. That meant nothing; the steps were all people.

The city ended at the bottom of the road, at an old wall nobody had mended and a gate nobody kept, and beyond it the way lay flat and straight through market gardens to the sea. The sun was a little past its height. A cart crawled along in front of us, going our way, and in the distance the harbor and its moles and a thin brown smudge of masts.

"His hand," I said. "At the edge of the shade. You saw it?"

"I saw where his feet stopped."

On my face, the air moved. It came inland off the water, cool, a breath of it and then a steadier push, smelling of salt and weed and pitch, and it lifted the hair off my neck.

Valens felt it too. He lifted his face into it.

"That's the breeze," he said.

"Felix will be on the quay with the mules," I said, "telling the master how to load them."

"He'll be asking him who won."

We ran.

I ran in a borrowed dress with the hem bunched in my fist and the camera on its strap under the cloth hammering my ribs. My sandals slapped. We overtook the cart. Valens ran beside me, not in front, one hand spread on his chest over the leaf under his tunic and the pine crown in the other, shedding needles on the road. My breath went short, then raw.

"Will they wait?" I said.

"No."

A dog burst from a garden gate and ran with us for a stretch, delighted, and dropped off. The wind blew harder into our faces, and the gardens went by on both sides, beans and cabbages and a man with a hoe who straightened to watch us pass. The smudge at the end of the road was masts now, each one separate, and hulls under them, and the long arm of the mole. At the harbor, beyond the last of the gardens, a sail was going up.
