"Have you seen his back?" Until then she had talked in Greek, a Greek so finely made that I lost it a clause at a time: the rain, and her brother's roof, which leaked over the north colonnade, and a regret that his household had made me no better welcome in all these weeks, in sentences that went on taking new parts into themselves long after I had found the verb and lost hold of its subject. The question was short and plain, and all of it reached me.

"Yes," I said.

I had had my palms on the ridges of it most nights since July. The heat went up my neck into my ears, and she watched it go.

"Good," said Plautia.

She sat by the brazier in a chair with a high curved back, her hands folded one into the other, and nothing of her moved but her eyes. She had fewer years on her than the woman I had made up for myself in the dark: thirty, or a little under, her hair dressed close to the head and pinned there, her face kept from the sun all its life. Her left earlobe had been torn through once, by an earring dragged or worn too heavy, and had grown back in two soft points with a notch between them. She wore no earrings. The room held a chest, her chair, an empty loom under the window, no web on its beam, and the stool where her maid Myrrhine sat shelling lupins into her skirt; and all round the walls, at the height a woman could reach, bronze hooks with nothing hanging from them.

I had lived under her brother's roof since the first of the autumn rains, and until that morning I had seen her three times, each time from behind: crossing the court under a cloak that Myrrhine held up to keep the rain off her, going out at the porch, climbing to these rooms. In the first week a dish of honey cakes had been sent down to the rooms over the stable for the wife of Valens, and I had given it to Felix, who ate every cake and asked when the next dish was due. If Valens had lain with her, then all of his telling by the stream had been a story, and he could tell me whatever he liked. If he had not, no woman alive sent honey cakes to a muleteer's wife. Every night, lying beside him, I went round it and came out where I had gone in.

"Have you?" I said.

She answered in Greek again, and at length, and I lost her at the second bend of it, somewhere between a lawyer's fee and a difficulty about witnesses, and found her only at the name of a month.

"Lady," I said, "my Greek is from Phocis, and there is not much of it. When it runs, I lose it."

Plautia's hands came apart.

"Then we will try another," she said, in Latin. "My brother has it from your husband that you were brought up at Syracuse, among people who kept their Latin as some families keep a tomb, swept, in good repair, and visited on the proper days, and that you can read it, and Greek besides, and write both. Can you?"

It was book Latin, laid down slowly, the verb held back for the end, and each clause was mine before the next arrived.

"I can," I said. Mine came out stiff, each ending sounded to its last letter. "I have spoken it to almost nobody."

"You are speaking it to me. Not as anybody speaks it in Syracuse."

"No."

"My brother spent a year there as a boy, with an uncle who salted tunny," said Plautia. "Speak Greek in front of him."

"You sent cakes."

"You did not eat them."

"The boy ate them."

"All of them, the cook tells me, and then the honey off the dish with his thumb." She did not smile, quite. "I have wished to see you since you came. I did not send for you, because I supposed you would find some reason to climb my stair and look at me before the winter was out, as anybody would, and you have found none; and now the winter is half gone, and my brother is at Patrae for ten days about the suit, and my patience has run out before my pride. You have been looking at the hooks."

"Yes."

"My son was seven at the vintage." It came in the same voice as the rest. "He is in his father's house, where the law says he must be. Twice in three years I have had sight of him: once at the theater, from the women's benches at the top, and once in the street, being taken to the baths, before anybody thought to turn him down another one. At the Saturnalia I sent him a ball of painted leather, red and green, wrapped in a cloth, and the porter at that house took it in at the gate." Outside the shutter the rain poured off the eaves in a rope. "Would a porter keep a child's ball?"

"A porter would be asked where he got it."

"So I tell myself." She looked at the brazier. "Your husband wants a leaf out of a book in the proconsul's record office. My brother wants the same leaf, for the suit, and I believe the man I was married to has thought of it as well. He is back among the proconsul's people this year, now that Corinth has a proconsul again to be among. Since the emperor took back from this province what Nero had given it, the colony's magistrates have had their books kept in that office beside the governor's own; the men who keep them are the proconsul's freedmen, and my son's father has been kind to each of them all this winter. My brother's freedman asked at the door before the Saturnalia and was told the office was shut for the holiday. He asked after it and was told that the book he wanted was out with a copyist."

"Copied by whom?"

"He is a freedman," said Plautia. "The clerk was smiling, and he went away."

"Can your brother not go to the proconsul himself?"

"He can, and he has. The proconsul received him with great kindness, and asked after my health, and after my son's, and said that he would look into the matter of the copying as soon as the year's business allowed, and my brother came home and broke a cup."

"And the proconsul?"

"Dined that evening with my former husband. Our cook has a cousin in that kitchen."

"What made you send for me?"

"Because you read." Her eyes left the coals. "And because I wished to see you. The second came first."

"To see what?"

"What he had chosen," said Plautia, "when at last he chose anything."

I had nothing to say to that in any language.

"Everybody who climbs that stair wants something of mine," she said. "My brother's advocate wants my mother's silver for his fee, and says so in a very pretty way. The cook wants my old cloak for his daughter, and does not say so, and looks at it. My brother wants me married again by the summer to a man in Sicyon with a vineyard and a cough. And you?"

"The leaf," I said. "For him."

"That is his want. And yours?"

"I have known you a morning. There is nothing of yours I want."

"Then you are the first," said Plautia. "You are lying."

Myrrhine got to her feet with her skirt full of skins and shook them onto the coals, and the room filled with the smell of their burning.

* * *

We left the grazing on the last fair day of it, when the turf in the hollow had burned yellow and the evening cold rose from it sooner each night. Mnason had gone down the gorge the week before, his ewes in front of him and his cheese pots slung on a donkey, to his son's wife and her counting.

Valens led the mules on through Delphi by daylight, by the road the town knew him on, to his yard below it. I left them at the cliff foot and sat out the afternoon above the stadium in a fold of rock, with Felix's spare cloak round me, while the shadow of Parnassus crept across the olives and the plain and settled at last on the gulf. When it touched the water there were lamps in Kirrha.

When the town had gone in to its suppers I took the stadium path down in the darkness, skirting the theater and the god's shut house. A dog joined me from among the treasuries and walked at my side to the precinct gate, and stretched across it there, its duty done. The lane was black. I had it by its walls, by the step halfway along that had turned my ankle when I was new, and by Nikaso's door, which had never shut flush and laid a blade of light on the paving.

Kallia's gate stood open the width of a hand. She was in the gap holding the lamp low, a sandal in her other fist, studying the stone of the step; as I reached her, something small and yellow went along it with its tail curled up over its back. She brought the sandal down on it, twice, and kicked the remains into the lane.

"There's its brother in the woodstack somewhere," she said.

Kallia held the gate for me, and barred it behind.

The pomegranate had its fruit on, split on the branch, the seeds gone brown inside where the wasps had been at them. A lodger's cloak was hung over the rail above, and up there somebody coughed in his sleep, with long gaps between, an old man's cough. Kallia set the lamp on the woodstack.

"Did he bring them all down?"

"All but two. He sold two at the bottom of the gorge, to a farmer with olives to bring in."

"The boy's going?"

"Felix is coming with us."

"When's the boat?"

"In two days, from Kirrha, if the wind holds. Across to Lechaion, and the road up to Corinth."

She put her foot in the sandal and worked the heel down.

"He fed you, up there?"

"Barley bread out of the ashes. Milk. Greens the herdsman boiled."

"Burnt underneath, the bread."

"Every time."

"His mother never showed him a griddle," Kallia said, "or he never looked."

"I have your obol still," I said. "For the sheet. I never went to Euthydikos's people for it, and then it was the mountain. At Corinth there are ships for the east, out of the other harbor. I will buy a sheet there and write it, and find somebody going to Syria."

"Pay him when he's aboard," Kallia said. "Not before."

I unpinned the himation. Through the summer it had been my cloak by day and my blanket at night; it had been through thorn, scree and Mnason's fold with me, and showed it. I folded it lengthwise and then across, the darned corner inside, and gave it to her past the lamp.

Kallia looked at it. She took it from me by its folds, as she would have taken a pile of a lodger's bedding, and carried it into her own room, and the lamp with it, and the court was left to the dark. Her shutter stood a crack open on the court. The light stayed in it.

I found the storeroom by the feel of its jamb. Inside, the mat was in its corner, and somebody had beaten it since the spring; the dust was gone from it, and it smelled of the sun. I lay down in my chiton with my bag under my head, and slept.

* * *

I wrote Kallia's line in Plautia's room four mornings after that first one, on a sheet of papyrus from a stall at the top of the Lechaion road, with ink the stallholder ground for me in a shell while I watched. I wrote it out twice because the first one blotted and the second one was not better.

Plautia had sent down for me two more mornings running. Both times she talked Latin to me for an hour about very little: the price of charcoal in a wet winter, the proconsul's wife's cough, a mosaic man her brother had hired who laid his little stones drunk. In between she put one plain question to me each time, never the same one.

"Do you sleep, in a stable?"

"Better than in a lodging house. Mules do not talk in their sleep."

"Were you afraid of him, at the beginning?"

"Of his size. For about an hour."

"And after the hour?"

"After the hour he was rude to me about a woodstack."

She had returned to the charcoal after that. Today she watched me cut the reed.

"Who is it to?"

"A woman in Delphi, to her son. He serves in Syria."

"What is in it?"

"The news of her lane."

"Nothing of herself."

"Nothing."

"Does he write to her?"

"He has written once," I said, "in more than two years."

Plautia looked at the sheet a while. "When his letter came, did she weep?"

"She swept a floor."

"What is she to you?"

"She found me on the step of a spring at night, and took me home, and gave me her husband's brother for a father, and the clerk wrote it down." I blew on the ink. "She went a whole winter without my name."

"What did she ask you?"

"Whether I could carry water."

"Can you?"

"Now."

Plautia put her hands out to the coals. Her sleeve slid from her left wrist, and there was a band of gold round it, plain, the breadth of a fingertip, closed so that its closing was hidden from me. In the lab I had held bracelets by the trayful, in cotton gloves: a hinge at one edge and a pin at the other, the pin a wire run through the knuckles of the hinge from top to bottom, and the knuckles always showing, however good the work, because a hinge was a thing that could not be hidden. On this one I found neither. The gold went round her wrist like a ring round a finger.

"How is it closed?" I said. "I can find no hinge. I have never met a hinge that kept itself hidden."

Plautia turned the band round her wrist with a fingertip until a line showed in the gold, a seam as fine as a hair; she opened it along the seam, and it lay in her palm, and she held the palm out to me.

"No," I said. "Lady, I only—"

"You admired it." The band went onto the table beside the sheet, clear of the wet ink, and she stood. "You will forgive me. The smoke has got into my eyes."

She passed the curtain into the next room.

The curtain was heavy and dull red, and it hung still behind her. Through it, after some time, her sound reached me. It was quiet and regular and it went on: a sound held in, then loosed, then held once more, and a long one, and round again to the start.

Myrrhine rose from her stool, came to the table, and eyed the bracelet without touching it.

"A drachma," she said, in Greek. "I'll give you a drachma for it, which is a good price for a thing you never paid for."

"It is not for sale."

"Everything she gives away is for sale again by morning." She meant no malice; it was a fact of the house, like the leaking colonnade. "I had her mother's cloak back off a fuller's wife at the Saturnalia, and her good shoes off a girl at the baths. Two drachmas."

"No."

She returned to her stool and her lupins, and split one with her thumbnail and ate it, and watched the curtain.

I took the bracelet up. It was heavier than it looked, and soft, as the old gold always was; a fingernail would have marked it. Near the clasp, on the outer face, just where the band would have lain across the inside of her wrist, the gold carried a small crescent of dents, and the opposite edge another: two marks above, two below, set close, with a gap in the middle of each pair. Over the years they had worn smooth at their edges. At work I would have measured them and drawn them and written them up on a card, and I sat in that room in Corinth holding it and did none of it.

Someone had cut his teeth on it.

Behind the curtain the sound had stopped. I went in.

It was a small room with a bed in it, and a window high up with its shutter closed, and a lamp on a stand. She was sitting on the edge of the bed with her hands folded as they had been in the chair, and her face was wet and perfectly composed.

"You will think me very badly governed," she said.

"I saw the marks."

She looked at the gold in my hand, not at me.

"He had two teeth at the bottom by midsummer," she said, "and two at the top by the vintage, and he would not take the breast unless that arm was across him, so that I wore it day and night for most of a year, and his mother told everyone who dined with us that I nursed my son like a fuller's wife. Which was true." Her hands stayed where they were. "Will you wear it?"

"Yes."

"On the left. It was made for the left."

I put it on. I found the seam with my thumbnail, and the gold opened along it and shut again round my wrist with a click, and after that the seam was gone from under my nail.

She put out one finger and touched the marks, and let it rest there.

* * *

Kallia's lamp was still alight when I woke.

From my mat I had it in the open doorway: a small flame on the sill of Kallia's window, blue at the root, standing in a court that was still more dark than gray. The himation lay folded on my bag inside the door, where my hand would find it before my feet found the floor. I opened it out across my lap.

All summer my pin had pierced the cloth at one spot on the left shoulder, and the weave there had parted round it into a hole I could have pushed a fingertip into, with a fringe of broken threads I had meant every week to mend. The hole was gone. Kallia had closed it into a round eye, working a gray thread about it in turns laid so close that no cloth showed between them, and had run a second thread through the weave all round, to keep the hole from going any further. The hem was turned where the scree and the thorn had frayed it, from one corner to the other, and stitched down. The stitches were small and even and all alike. Toward the second corner they grew a fraction longer, and then, for the last hand's breadth, small again.

I put the pin through the eye, and it slid home without catching.

I took my bag and left the storeroom. The top of the court was gray and the bottom still dark, and Kallia was in the lean-to, her back to the court, raking last night's embers forward under the pot. She came out carrying the broom. I stood aside on the paving, and she swept the storeroom step where I had just come over it: quick strokes, outward, the grit off my sandals skittering away past my feet. Two strokes more along the sill, jamb to jamb. She took the broom back to the lean-to and hung it from its nail beside the fire, and went to the jar for water, and did not go near the stair.

On the gallery the old man coughed and shifted under his blanket.

The light came the way it came every morning into that court, downward: first the ridge tiles of the gallery, then the coping opposite, then the highest leaves of the pomegranate, and the flame on the sill went from being the brightest thing in the court to being only one of the things in it. I lifted the camera out of my clothes by its strap.

I opened the lens wide and turned the dial down to the slowest speed I could hold steady, and set my shoulder against the pomegranate's trunk. In the finder the window was a square of dark with a crack of light beside it where the shutter was ajar. On the sill below it stood the lamp: a round belly of brown clay, a nozzle black to the lip, the flame standing up off it, and above it a long tongue of soot climbing the plaster of the reveal to the lintel, broadening to a hand's width at the top, one night's burning laid over years of others.

I held my breath and pressed the release.

The shutter clacked, louder in that court than anything since the scorpion, the mirror clapping up and back behind the glass. Kallia was facing me from the jar with the dipper in her hand. She looked at the thing at my face, and then past it, at her lamp, with the look she gave a jar when a lodger had been standing too near it. The lamp was unchanged.

She crossed to the sill, wet her fingertips at her mouth, and pinched the flame out. A wisp went up along the soot and was gone before it reached the lintel.

* * *

The rain held off for three days at the end of that month, and on the second of them Plautia set out for the baths.

I sat on the porch bench with the dog, which had decided by then that I belonged to the porch and lay with its chin across my foot. Myrrhine came out first with the bundle of towels and the oil flask on its strap, then two of her brother's slaves in their cloaks, then Plautia, veiled, holding the hem of her cloak clear of the step's puddle.

At that same moment Valens appeared out of the lane by the stables with a sack of barley on his shoulder, Felix behind him with another.

He stopped short of the step. He let the sack slide to the paving, and before it had settled he had searched the street with his eyes, both ways: the corner behind him, the fuller's door opposite with its vats steaming in the cold, the turning beyond, the line of the roofs. He stood aside against the house wall, a little to the right of the step, facing the turning.

Plautia had stopped on the step when he stopped. She waited there, with her hem in her hand, until he had looked, and until he had moved; then she stepped down and passed him on his left, so that he was at her back, and walked on with Myrrhine and the slaves toward the end of the street. At the turning she moved her head, not far, to find him.

He was where he had been, by the house wall, the sack at his feet.

She was gone round the turning. He watched the empty turning a while, then hefted the sack again and climbed the step, and saw me, and let the sack drop again. He came to the bench and looked at my face. Behind him the street might have been a wall.

"Your feet are wet."

"The dog is lying on the dry one."

"Is that gold?" Felix had climbed the step behind him and dropped his own sack on top of Valens's. "On your arm. Is it real? Where'd you get it? If you bite it you can tell; if it's real your teeth go in. Give it here, I'll show you."

"Someone has done that already," I said, and kept the arm in my lap.

Valens looked at the band, not long. "She gave you that."

"Two days ago."

"She does that. Give it to her woman tomorrow, early, and say nothing to her about it. She'll be sorry by dark."

"She told me to wear it. On the left."

He studied my face, and then the band, the band for longer.

"Wear it, then," he said.

"Is it heavy?" said Felix. "How heavy? What would it buy? Would it buy a mule?"

Valens turned to Felix. "Take the sacks round. Fill the dog's bowl; it's foul."

Felix gripped a sack and did not lift it. "The man at the stable says there's fights at the amphitheater after the games. Proper ones, with a lanista from Patrae and a Gaul that's killed four. He says the Corinthians built it in a hollow so the noise goes up and the whole forum hears it." Felix glanced past the band to Valens. "Is it the one? Where you got the sword?"

"There's only the one."

"Will you show me where you stood? Not in it. Only the gate."

"The sacks," said Valens.

Felix took one sack and dragged the other, and the dog went with him for the bowl's sake.

"Will you take him?" I said. "Only to the gate."

"He'd want in."

"Would that be so bad?"

"He'd stay in."

"Where have you been all morning? The barley is sold two doors down."

"Near the forum." He sat next to me on the bench, which in that house he had not done before, his forearms laid along his thighs. "The proconsul's office is at the end of the long colonnade, across the forum from the old temple. Two doors. One onto the colonnade, with a porter on a stool and a slave asleep at his feet. One at the back, onto a lane, with a baker's oven built against it and a step down."

"You went to see it."

"I went for barley." He watched the lane end of the street, where the fuller's boy was carrying out a vat of something that steamed. "The porter on the colonnade is the proconsul's. The baker's not."

"How do you know the baker's not?"

"He sold me bread. He asked was I from the games at Nemea, and I said no, and he said I had the look, and gave me back too much."

"Did you give it back?"

"I bought another loaf with it."

He had the loaf inside his tunic, and he took it out and broke it, and handed me half, warm still in the middle. The dog came back without Felix and sat in front of us and watched the bread.

That afternoon I climbed Plautia's stair without being sent for.

She was alone. Myrrhine was in the kitchen seeing to the lamps, and Plautia sat in the high-backed chair with her hands empty, doing no work, with the shutter open a hand's breadth on a pewter sky. Her own wrist was bare. She saw mine before she saw my face.

"I have come for nothing," I said.

"Then sit."

I took Myrrhine's stool. The lupin skins were still in a heap under it, and I moved them with my foot.

"What is he called?" I said. "Your son."

"Marcus. For his father's father, who held the consulship two months in a bad year." She looked at the gray gap of sky. "He has his father's ears, which stand out, and my mother's temper, which does not, and before he was four he could say the Muses, all nine, in order, and could not be got to say the Graces at all, because he said they were only girls."

"Our stable boy is the same."

"Your boy has a mule's manners and two good hands. I watched him at the well." She was quiet for a little. "Does he laugh? Your husband."

"Not often."

"I walked in front of him for two years and never heard it."

"In the summer a bed fell in under us," I said, "in a herdsman's hut on the mountain. It was the herdsman's own bed, and the fork at its head split, and we went to the floor with the fleeces on top of us. He laughed then. He could not stop for some while."

Plautia sat quite still. Then a sound broke from her, short, surprised; she glanced at her own lap as though she had dropped it there, and it broke from her again, and that time she let it.

"And the herdsman?" she said at last.

"He is at his son's for the winter. He will find out in the spring."

"Come up tomorrow," said Plautia. "Come up when you like. Myrrhine will complain, and you will not listen to her."

* * *

We went over the gulf two days after Kallia's lamp, in a boat that carried oil jars from Kirrha to Lechaion and anything else that would pay: a broad black coaster with a patched sail, and a master who chewed mastic and spat it into the sea when the taste went. Our mules were tied in the waist between the jars, heads short to a rail, straw kicked down under their hooves, and the fig rode roped to the foot of the mast in its jar.

Halfway across, the mules were sick, as far as mules could be.

They had no way to bring it up. They stood with their legs braced wide against the roll and the sweat coming out dark on their necks and flanks, and groaned, all together, a long low sound like furniture being dragged across a floor upstairs. The dun hung her lip and let it hang. The black kept trying to lie down, folding at the knees and catching herself, and every time she started, Valens got a shoulder under her neck and talked her up again.

Felix was sick into the sea with great industry and no breakfast.

"Why don't they spew?" He straightened from the rail, his face gone white as whey. "Wouldn't it help them? Does it hurt worse, if you can't? Is that why they're making that noise? Would a horse?" And he was at the rail again.

"They can't," said Valens. "It stays in them."

"All of it?"

"Hold the dun's head up. If she goes down on this she'll not get up till Lechaion."

I held the dun's head up. She gave the whole hot weight of it to me, groaning into my himation, and her ears flattened and lifted with every roll. The drugstore at Twentieth and Walnut had sold bands for seasickness, elastic, with a plastic bead sewn in to press on the inside of the wrist; Gloria's Ray swore by them, and had been sick in a pair from Lewes into Cape May. The dun groaned again. I rubbed the flat place between her eyes with my knuckles, and the master watched me do it over his mastic and went aft to tell the boy at the steering oar something about foreigners.

"If a mule dies on my deck," the master called forward to Valens, "you pay for the deck to be scrubbed, and the jars it falls against, and the god for the trouble."

"She won't die."

The wind went down with the sun, as it did on that water, and we lay all night off a low coast I never saw, rolling on a long swell with the sail brailed up and the jars knocking. Sometime after the middle of the night I woke on the deck to a scraping and a grunt, and a hoof striking wood, and Valens's voice in the dark, low.

"The light."

I felt in the bag for it and thumbed it on.

Down among the jars lay the black. She had folded at last and gone over onto her side, her legs against the rail, and they would not come under her. In the light were the white of her eye and the sweat on her shoulder, and Valens in the straw at her head with her halter rope round his fist. It was a poor brown light, browner than in the cave, and it shrank as I held it on them.

"What's that?" Felix had raised himself on an elbow on the coil of rope he slept on. "What is it? Where's the flame? Why doesn't it smoke? Is it hers? Can I hold it?"

"Get round her quarters," Valens said. "Don't push. Take the tail when I say, and pull her toward you."

"What's it burning?"

"Felix."

He went round her quarters. Valens worked a sack in under her shoulder, and spoke to her in Latin, and when he said, Felix took the tail and threw himself back on it, and the black heaved. Her forelegs came under her first, and she sat up in the straw like a dog, trembling, with her hind end still down; then she got those under her too, and stood. Her ribs heaved. By then the light was a yellow smudge on her flank the breadth of three fingers, and I turned it off while it still had something left.

"Does it go back on?" said Felix, in the dark.

"Go to sleep," said Valens.

Felix lay down again on his rope. Valens stayed by the black, and presently he spoke across her, low.

"How much is left in it?"

"Not much. Some."

"Don't spend it on mules."

"You asked for it."

"I did." I heard him settle into the straw between the black and the jars, where she could not go over again without going over onto him.

When the gray came, the coast was there, and the long low line of Lechaion's moles, and over all of it, rising from the flat land behind the town, the great rock of the citadel with the first sun on its top and a wall running along its crest, gone gold. Felix was at the rail with his hands wrapped round each other.

"What's on the top of it?"

"A temple," I said. "Aphrodite's."

"Is it gold? Do they let boys up?"

"It's a long climb," said Valens, "for a boy who's been sick all day."

"Is that the town, though? Is that Corinth? Which way's the ship road, that they haul them over on?"

"East of the harbor," said Valens. "Along there."

Felix stared that way for some time.

"I'd sooner not look at it," he said, and sat on the deck with his back to the rail.

From Lechaion the road ran dead straight over the flat land to the city, between market gardens and the grave plots of Corinthians, the citadel never nearer however far we walked, and we reached the gate past noon, the string strung out behind us and Felix asleep on the dun. Plautia's brother kept a porter on a stool in his porch and a dog on a chain, which rose and announced us to everyone in the lane.

The porter asked who we were. Valens gave his name, and the name of the freedman who had come to Kirrha for him.

"And her?"

"My wife," said Valens.

Never before in my hearing had he said it to a stranger, to a man with a stool and a tablet, who might have put it in writing and did not. The dog pushed its wet nose into my hand and kept up its snuffling while the porter settled that we were no concern of his. Beyond him, at the back of the court, a woman was climbing an outside stair, a maid behind her holding a cloak over her against the drizzle, and she kept her face to the stair.

* * *

"At the Isthmia," Plautia said, "the proconsul goes out to the god's precinct on the Isthmus with his whole staff, and the magistrates who give the games go out with him, and that half of Corinth which is neither racing nor selling goes out after them to watch the half that is, for as many days as there is racing; and the boy's father, who has not missed a festival at which a proconsul might see him since he first had a beard to be seen in, will sit in the second row behind the governor's chair in a new toga, laughing a breath behind the governor." It was late, the shutters were barred, and her brother was home from Patrae and asleep in his own rooms. "The proconsul's office keeps one clerk through the festival, to sit on its door."

"Which clerk?" asked Valens.

He stood just inside the door with his back to the wall by the jamb, which gave him the whole room and the stair behind him. He had not sat in that room. Twice she had asked him to sit, and twice he had said that he would stand.

"My brother's freedman says it will be the young one, who drinks, or the gray-haired one, who does not, and that both of them are fond of money." She had a cup of hot wine in her hands and did not drink it. "I will go in myself, on the second morning, at the hour the boys run, when the office is emptiest. I will have a reason for being there that no clerk can refuse a woman of my family without writing his refusal down, and a purse that he will not wish to write down at all, and my woman with me to carry my writing case. My woman reads."

"I carry your case," said Myrrhine, from the chest by the wall, in Greek. She had followed that much.

"Not this time."

"She doesn't know where the flask goes. She doesn't know which side you get down from a litter. She'll stand on your hem."

"Then you will show her where the flask goes, and which side, and she will stand off my hem." Myrrhine split a lupin with great force and said nothing more.

"What reason will you give the porter?" I said.

"My dowry. The instrument of it was entered in the colony's books when I was married, with my father's seal on it, and those books went into that office with all the others. I have the right to read it, and to have it read to me, and to have a copy made with the advocate standing by. A clerk who tells a daughter of my father that she may not see her own dowry will have to say why, in writing, to a proconsul who once dined at my father's table."

"And while he is fetching it?"

"While he is fetching it," said Plautia, "my woman looks along his shelves."

"You'll be known," Valens said.

"By whom? Every man in Corinth who knows my face will be at the Isthmus, looking at boys."

"Your son," I said.

"Will be there as well, I am told, in the second row, in a new tunic, beside his father."

"You could go out to the Isthmus instead, and see him."

"From the women's benches at the top, over the heads of half Corinth, while one race is run. I have done that." The cup went to the floor by her chair. "This is the suit. The suit is the one thing that man wants of me that I have not already given him, and I will not be at the top of the benches while it walks out of a back door. And on the day after the boys' race, when he rides in from the Isthmus with the dust on him, you will be on a ship out of Lechaion, and he may look for you in the archive, and the theater, and the baths."

Valens shifted where he stood. "I'll be by the baker's oven."

"Where every slave of that house buys its bread."

"The baker gives too much back."

"You will be at the Isthmus with your mules," said Plautia, "selling them at the horse fair to whoever asks, where twenty men who sat on the benches the day you got your sword may stare at you all they please and tell each other they have seen Lupus, and none of them will think to walk a morning back to Corinth and find out what his wife is doing."

Valens said, "No."

"I have not finished."

"I'll be at the back door."

Their eyes met across the room. It was the first such look I had seen pass between them: the look of two people carrying a weight from either end, each waiting for the other to tire. Plautia let her end go.

"We will speak of the door when my brother has spoken to the baker," she said.

"The book," Valens said.

"You cannot have the book, Valens." She gave his name its weight, every sound of it, as she gave every word. "It is the book of the magistrate who sat at those games, the whole of his year's business, and it is boards, not a roll; there is no clerk awake or asleep who would not miss it gone, and you could not carry it across a forum under your cloak and not look like a man carrying a door."

"What holds the boards?" I said. "A cord, or rings?"

"A cord. Through holes at the back edge, and knotted, and the knot sealed."

"With his seal?"

"His, and his colleague's."

"A cord can be cut," I said, "and tied again. A seal is wax."

Plautia regarded me over the coals.

"Can you put a seal back so that nobody sees?"

"Not so that nobody sees," I said. "So that it slips past a drinker, in a doorway's light, with somebody talking at him. A sober clerk who is looking would find it."

"Then we will hope for the young one." She turned to Valens. "You have no use for the book. You want two of its boards. The leaf with your name on it is near the front, because the emperor's games were early in that year, and it is two boards that close on each other with the writing inside; once the cord is cut they come away together and go into a writing case like any other pair. It will say Gaius Iulius Valens, freedman of Gaius, set free by his master before the magistrate of the games and before the emperor, with the witnesses' names under it, and the day."

I had been married to a man in that house for three months, by the porter's account and the cook's, and I had had one name for him.

"Iulius," I said.

"The lanista's," said Valens. "I had it with the sword."

"I heard you give all of it in that atrium," Plautia said, "your hand on the household altar, the day you were sworn to me. You said the Gaius as though somebody might take it back."

"It was new," said Valens.

"When is the suit heard?" I said.

"In Rome, before a judge the praetor gives us, on a day the praetor finds for it. The advocate says two years from this spring. My brother says one. The praetor has said nothing."

"I said I'd stand up," said Valens. "I'll stand up."

Plautia inclined her head to him, very slightly, the small bow a woman of her kind gave a porter who offered to carry what she meant to carry herself.

Down in the court somebody shouted for him, high and cracked.

"Valens! The dun's out of her halter! She's in the cook's cabbages, and he's after her with a ladle!"

It was Felix, below in the court with a lamp.

Valens went down. His tread left the stair; in a while a door banged in the stable yard, and at the porch the dog gave one bark and stopped.

Plautia waited until the court was still. She left the cup where it stood, and when she spoke again it was in a Latin plain enough for the stableboy, with each word laid down on its own. She asked it of me, and not of the coals: when he had his leaf, and his ship, and me, what was there left in Rome that would bring him to stand up before a court and tell it the truth about her?
