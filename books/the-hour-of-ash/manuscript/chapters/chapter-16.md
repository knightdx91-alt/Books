"Have you seen his back?" Until then she had talked in Greek, a Greek so finely made that I lost it a clause at a time: something about the rain, and her brother's roof, which let it in along the colonnade, and a regret that his household had made me no better welcome since the autumn, in sentences that went on taking new parts into themselves long after I had found the verb and let go of the subject. The question was short and plain, and all of it reached me.

"Yes," I said.

I had had my palms on the ridges of it most nights since July. The heat went up my neck into my ears, and she watched it go.

"Good," said Plautia.

She sat by the brazier in a chair with a high curved back, her hands one inside the other in her lap, and nothing of her moved but her eyes. She was younger than the woman I had made up for myself in the dark: thirty, or a little under, her hair dressed close to the head and pinned there, her face kept out of the sun all its life. Her left earlobe had been torn through once, by an earring dragged or worn too heavy, and had grown back in two soft points with a notch between them. She wore no earrings. The room held a loom against the far wall with no web on it, a chest, her chair, and the stool where her maid Myrrhine sat shelling lupins into her lap; and all round the walls, at the height a woman could reach, bronze hooks with nothing hanging from them.

I had been in her brother's house since before the rains, and until that morning I had seen her three times, each time from behind: crossing the court under a cloak that Myrrhine held over her head, going out at the porch, going up the stair. In the first week a dish of honey cakes had come down to the rooms over the stable for the wife of Valens, and I had given it to Felix, who ate every cake and wanted to know when the next dish was coming. If Valens had lain with her, then all of his telling by the stream had been a story, and he could tell me anything at all. If he had not, no woman alive sent honey cakes to a muleteer's wife. Every night, lying beside him, I went round it and came out at the same place.

"Have you?" I said.

She answered in Greek again, and at length, and I lost her at the second turn, somewhere between her brother's advocate and a difficulty about witnesses, and found her only at the name of a month.

"Lady," I said, "my Greek is from Phocis, and there is not much of it. When it runs, I lose it."

Plautia's hands came apart in her lap.

"Then we will try another," she said, in Latin. "My brother has it from your husband that you were brought up at Syracuse, among people who kept their Latin as some families keep a tomb, swept, in good repair, and visited on the proper days, and that you can read it, and Greek besides, and write both. Can you?"

It was book Latin, laid down slowly, the verb held back for the end, and each clause was mine before the next arrived.

"I can," I said. Mine came out stiff, with every ending in its place. "I have spoken it to almost nobody."

"You are speaking it to me. Not as anybody speaks it in Syracuse."

"No."

"My brother spent a year there as a boy, with an uncle who salted tunny," said Plautia. "Speak Greek in front of him."

"You sent cakes."

"You did not eat them."

"The boy ate them."

"All of them, the cook tells me, and then the honey off the dish with his thumb." She did not smile, quite. "I have wanted to see you since the autumn. I did not send for you, because I supposed you would find some reason to come up my stair and look at me before the winter was out, as anybody would, and you have found none; and now the winter is half gone, and my brother is at Patrae for ten days about the suit, and I have come to the end of my patience before I came to the end of my pride. You have been looking at the hooks."

"Yes."

"My son was seven at the vintage." She said it in the same voice as the rest. "He is in my former husband's house, where the law says he must be. I have seen him twice in three years: once at the theater, from the women's benches at the top, and once in the street, being taken to the baths, before anybody thought to turn him down another one. At the Saturnalia I sent him a ball of painted leather, red and green, wrapped in a cloth, and the porter at that house took it in at the gate." Outside the shutter the rain came off the eaves in a rope. "Would a porter keep a child's ball?"

"A porter would be asked where he got it."

"So I tell myself." She looked at the brazier. "Your husband wants a leaf out of a book in the proconsul's record office. My brother wants the same leaf, for the suit, and I believe my former husband has thought of it as well. He has found his way back onto the proconsul's staff this year, now that there is a proconsul again to find his way back to. Since the emperor took back from this province what Nero gave it, the books of the colony's magistrates have been kept in that office along with the governor's own, and the men who keep them are the proconsul's freedmen, and my former husband has been kind to every one of them since the autumn. My brother's freedman asked at the door before the Saturnalia and was told the office was shut for the holiday. He asked after it and was told that the book he wanted had been sent out to be copied."

"Copied by whom?"

"He did not ask. He is a freedman," said Plautia, "and the clerk was smiling."

"Can your brother not go to the proconsul himself?"

"He can, and he has. The proconsul received him with great kindness, and asked after my health, and after my son's, and said that he would look into the matter of the copying as soon as the year's business allowed, and my brother came home and broke a cup."

"And the proconsul?"

"Dined that evening with my former husband. Our cook has a cousin in that kitchen."

"Why did you send for me?"

"Because you read." Her eyes came back from the coals. "And because I wished to see you. The second came first."

"What did you wish to see?"

"What he had chosen," said Plautia, "when at last he chose anything."

I had nothing to say to that in any language.

"Everybody who comes up that stair wants something of mine," she said. "My brother's advocate wants my mother's silver for his fee, and says so in a very pretty way. The cook wants my old cloak for his daughter, and does not say so, and looks at it. My brother wants me married again by the summer to a man in Sicyon with a vineyard and a cough. What do you want?"

"The leaf," I said. "For him."

"That is his want. What is yours?"

"I do not know you well enough to want anything of yours."

"Then you are the first," said Plautia, "and I do not believe you."

Myrrhine stood up with her lap full of skins and tipped them onto the coals all at once, and the room filled with the smell of their burning.

* * *

The string came down off the grazing on the last fair day of it, when the turf in the hollow had burned yellow and the cold came up out of it earlier every evening. Mnason had gone down the gorge a week before us with his ewes in front of him and his cheese pots slung on a donkey, and his son's wife waiting at the bottom to count his mouthfuls.

Below the cliffs Valens took the mules on through Delphi in daylight, by the road the town knew him on, to his yard on the far side of it. I left them at the foot of the climb and sat out the afternoon above the stadium in a fold of rock, with Felix's spare cloak round me, while the shadow of the mountain went out over the olives and the plain and lay down at last on the gulf. By the time it reached the water there were lamps in Kirrha.

When the town had gone in to its suppers I came down the stadium path in the dark, past the theater and the god's shut house. A dog came out from somewhere among the treasuries and walked beside me as far as the precinct gate, and lay down across it there, its duty done. The lane was black. I knew it by its walls, by the step halfway along that had turned my ankle in my first week, and by Nikaso's door, which had never shut flush and let a blade of light out across the paving.

Kallia's gate stood open the width of a hand. She was in the gap with the lamp held low in one fist and a sandal in the other, looking at the stone of the step, and as I came up something small and yellow went along it with its tail curled up over its back. She brought the sandal down on it, and again, and scraped it off the sole into the lane with her toe.

"There's its brother in the woodstack somewhere," she said.

She held the gate for me, and barred it behind.

The pomegranate had its fruit on, split on the branch, the seeds gone brown inside where the wasps had been at them. A lodger's cloak hung over the gallery rail, and from up there somebody coughed in his sleep, with long gaps between, an old man's cough. Kallia stood the lamp on the woodstack and turned the sandal up to look at its sole.

"Did he bring them all down?"

"All but two. He sold two at the bottom of the gorge to a man who wanted them for his olives."

"The boy's going?"

"Felix is coming with us."

"When's the boat?"

"The day after tomorrow, from Kirrha, if the wind holds. Across to Lechaion, and up the road to Corinth."

She put the sandal on, and worked her heel down into it.

"He fed you, up there?"

"Barley bread out of the ashes. Milk. Greens the herdsman boiled."

"Burnt underneath, the bread."

"Every time."

"His mother never showed him a griddle," Kallia said, "or he never looked."

"I have your obol still," I said. "For the sheet. I never went up to the priest's house for it, and then it was the mountain. At Corinth there are ships for the east, out of the other harbor. I will buy a sheet there and write it, and find somebody going to Syria."

"Pay him when he's aboard," Kallia said. "Not before."

I unpinned the himation. All summer it had been my cloak by day and my blanket at night; it had been through thorn, scree and Mnason's fold with me, and showed it. I folded it the long way and across, with the darned corner inside, and gave it to her over the lamp.

Kallia looked at it. She took it from me by its folds, as she would have taken a pile of a lodger's bedding, and carried it into her own room, and the lamp with it, and the court went dark from wall to wall. Her shutter stood a crack open on the court. The light stayed in it.

I found the storeroom door by the feel of its jamb. Inside, the mat lay where it had always lain, and somebody had beaten it since the spring; the dust was out of it, and it smelled of the sun. I lay down on it in my chiton with my bag under my head, and slept.

* * *

I wrote Kallia's line in Plautia's room four days after the first time, on a sheet of papyrus from a stall at the top of the Lechaion road, with ink the stallholder ground for me in a shell while I waited. I wrote it out twice because the first one blotted and the second one was not better.

Plautia had sent down for me the morning after that first morning, and the morning after that. Both times she talked Latin to me for an hour about nothing in particular: the price of charcoal in a wet winter, the proconsul's wife's cough, a mosaic man her brother had hired who laid his little stones drunk. In between she put one plain question to me each time, never the same one.

"Do you sleep, in a stable?"

"Better than in a lodging house. Mules do not talk in their sleep."

"Were you afraid of him, at the beginning?"

"Of his size. For about an hour."

"And after the hour?"

"After the hour he was rude to me about a woodstack."

She had gone back to the charcoal after that. Today she watched me cut the reed.

"Who is it to?"

"A woman in Delphi, to her son. He serves in Syria."

"What does she tell him?"

"The news of her lane."

"Nothing of herself."

"Nothing."

"Does he write to her?"

"He has written once," I said, "in more than two years."

Plautia looked at the sheet a while. "When his letter came, did she weep?"

"She swept a floor."

"What is she to you?"

"She found me on the step of a spring at night, and took me home, and gave me her husband's brother for a father, and the clerk wrote it down." I blew on the ink. "In a whole winter she did not ask me my name."

"What did she ask you?"

"Whether I could carry water."

"Can you?"

"Now."

Plautia put her hands out to the coals. Her sleeve fell back from the left wrist, and there was a band of gold round it, plain, as broad as the top joint of my thumb, closed so that I could not see how. In the lab I had held bracelets by the trayful, in cotton gloves: hinged on one side and pinned on the other, the pin a wire run down through the knuckles of the hinge, and the knuckles always showing, however good the work, because a hinge was a thing that could not be hidden. On this one I could find neither. The gold went round her wrist like a ring round a finger.

"How is it closed?" I said. "I cannot see the hinge. I have never met a hinge I could not see."

Plautia drew the band round her wrist with two fingers until a line showed in the gold, a seam as fine as a hair; she opened it along the seam, and it lay in her palm, and she held the palm out to me.

"No," I said. "Lady, I only—"

"You admired it." She put it down on the table, beside the sheet and clear of the wet ink, and stood. "You will forgive me. The smoke has got into my eyes."

She went through the curtain into the next room.

The curtain was heavy and dull red, and it hung still behind her. Through it, after a while, I heard her. It was quiet and regular and it went on: a sound held in, and let go, and held in again, and then a long one, and the same again from the beginning.

Myrrhine got up off her stool and came to the table and looked down at the bracelet without touching it.

"A drachma," she said, in Greek. "I'll give you a drachma for it, which is a good price for a thing you never paid for."

"It is not for sale."

"Everything she gives away is for sale by the next morning." She said it without malice, as she might have told me where the roof let the rain in. "I had her mother's cloak back off a fuller's wife at the Saturnalia, and her good shoes off a girl at the baths. Two drachmas."

"No."

She went back to her stool and her lupins, and split one with her thumbnail and ate it, and watched the curtain.

I took the bracelet up. It was heavier than it looked, and soft, as the old gold always was; a fingernail would have marked it. Near the clasp, on the outer face, at the place where the band would have lain across the inside of her wrist, there was a little crescent of dents in the gold, and on the opposite edge another: two small marks above, two below, set close, with a gap in the middle of each pair. Over the years they had worn smooth at their edges. At work I would have measured them and drawn them and written them up on a card, and I sat in that room in Corinth with the thing in my hand and did none of it.

Somebody had cut his teeth on it.

Behind the curtain the sound had stopped. I went in.

It was a small room with a bed in it, and a window high up with its shutter closed, and a lamp on a stand. She was sitting on the edge of the bed with her hands one inside the other in her lap, exactly as she sat in the chair, and her face was wet and perfectly composed.

"You will think me very badly governed," she said.

"I saw the marks."

She looked at the bracelet in my hand, not at me.

"He had two teeth at the bottom by midsummer and two at the top by the vintage," she said, "and he would not take the breast unless that arm was across him, so that I wore it day and night for most of a year, and my former husband's mother told everyone who dined with us that I nursed my son like a fuller's wife. Which was true." Her hands stayed where they were. "Will you wear it?"

"Yes."

"On the left. It was made for the left."

I put it on. I found the seam with my thumbnail, and the gold opened along it and closed again round my wrist with a small sound, and then I could not find the seam at all.

She put out one finger and laid it on the marks, and left it there.

* * *

The lamp was still burning when I woke.

I could see it from my mat, through the storeroom door: a small flame on the sill of Kallia's window, blue at the root, standing in a court that was still more dark than gray. The himation lay folded on my bag inside the door, where my hand would find it before my feet found the floor. I opened it out across my lap.

All summer my pin had gone through the cloth in the same place on the left shoulder, and the weave there had parted round it into a hole I could have put a finger through, with a fringe of broken threads I had meant every week to do something about. The hole was gone. Kallia had closed it into a round eye, working a gray thread round and round it with the turns laid so close that the cloth did not show between them, and had run a second thread through the weave all round, to keep the hole from going any further. The hem was turned where the scree and the thorn had frayed it, from one corner to the other, and stitched down. The stitches were small and even and all alike. Toward the second corner they grew a little longer, and then, for the last hand's breadth, they were small again.

I put the pin through the eye, and it went through without catching.

When I came out with my bag the top of the court was gray and the bottom still dark, and Kallia was in the lean-to with her back to me, raking last night's embers forward under the pot. She came out with the broom. I stood aside on the paving, and she swept the storeroom step where I had just come over it, in short strokes, outward, the grit off my sandals going across the stone and away past my feet into the court. Two strokes more along the sill, from one jamb to the other. She carried the broom back to the lean-to and hung it on its peg by the hearth, and went to the jar for water, and did not go up the stair.

On the gallery the old man coughed and turned over in his blanket.

The light came the way it came every morning into that court, from the top: first the ridge tiles of the gallery, then the top of the far wall, then the upper leaves of the pomegranate, and the flame on the sill went from being the brightest thing in the court to being only one of the things in it. I took the camera out from inside my chiton.

I opened the lens as far as it would go and turned the dial down to the slowest speed I could hold steady, and set my shoulder against the pomegranate's trunk. In the finder the window was a square of dark with a crack of light down one side where the shutter stood open. On the sill below it stood the lamp: a round belly of brown clay, a nozzle black to the lip, the flame standing up off it, and above it a long tongue of soot going up the plaster of the reveal to the lintel, wider at the top than my hand, one night's burning laid over a great many others.

I held my breath and pressed the release.

The shutter went, louder in that court than anything since the scorpion, and the mirror slapped up and down behind the glass. Kallia had turned from the jar with the dipper in her hand. She looked at the thing at my face, and then past it, at her lamp, as she might have looked at a jar a lodger had been standing too near. The lamp was as it had been.

She crossed to the sill, wet her finger and thumb at her mouth, and pinched the flame out. A thread of smoke went up along the soot and was gone before it reached the lintel.

* * *

The rain held off for three days at the end of that month, and on the second of them Plautia went out to the baths.

I was on the bench in the porch with the dog, which had decided by then that I was a part of the porch and lay with its chin across my foot. Myrrhine came out first with the bundle of towels and the oil flask on its strap, then two of the brother's men in their cloaks, then Plautia, veiled, with the hem of her cloak held clear of the wet step in one hand.

At the same moment Valens came round the corner from the stable lane with a sack of barley on his shoulder, and Felix behind him with another.

He stopped short of the step. He let the sack down off his shoulder onto the paving, and before it had settled he had been over the street with his eyes, up and down: the corner he had come round, the fuller's door across the way with its vats steaming in the cold, the far corner, the line of the roofs. He stood aside against the house wall, a little to the right of the step, and faced up the street toward the far corner.

Plautia had stopped on the step when he stopped. She waited there, with her hem in her hand, until he had looked, and until he had moved; then she came down into the street and went past him on his left, so that he was at her back, and walked on with Myrrhine and the men toward the corner. At the corner she turned her head, not far, to see where he was.

He was where he had been, against the wall, with the sack at his feet.

She went round the corner. He stood watching the empty corner for a while, then hefted the sack again and came up the step, and saw me, and set the sack down a second time. He came and stood in front of the bench, and looked at my face. Behind him the street might have been a wall.

"Your feet are wet."

"The dog is lying on the dry one."

"Is that gold?" Felix had come up the step after him and put his own sack down on top of Valens's. "On your arm. Is it real? Where'd you get it? If you bite it you can tell; if it's real your teeth go in. Give it here, I'll show you."

"Somebody has done that already," I said, and kept the arm in my lap.

Valens looked at the band, not long. "She gave you that."

"Two days ago."

"She does that. Give it to her woman tomorrow, early, and say nothing to her about it. She'll want it back by dark."

"She told me to wear it. On the left."

He looked at me, and at the bracelet again, for longer.

"Wear it, then," he said.

"Is it heavy?" said Felix. "How heavy? What would it buy? Would it buy a mule?"

"Take the sacks round," Valens told him. "And fill the dog's bowl. It's foul."

Felix took hold of a sack and did not lift it. "The man at the stable says there's fights at the amphitheater after the games. Proper ones, with a lanista from Patrae and a Gaul that's killed four. He says the Corinthians built it in a hollow so the noise goes up and you can hear it in the forum." He looked at the bracelet, and at Valens. "Is it the one? Where you got the sword?"

"There's only the one."

"Will you show me where you stood? Not in it. Only the gate."

"The sacks," said Valens.

Felix took one sack and dragged the other, and the dog went with him for the bowl's sake.

"Will you take him?" I said. "Only to the gate."

"He'd want to go in."

"Would that be so bad?"

"He'd want to go in," said Valens, "and then he'd want to stay in."

"Where did you go?" I said. "The barley is sold two doors down."

"By the forum." He sat down on the bench beside me, which in that house he had not done before, with his forearms on his thighs. "The proconsul's office is at the far end of the long colonnade, behind the old temple. Two doors. One onto the colonnade, with a porter on a stool and a slave asleep at his feet. One at the back, onto a lane, with a baker's oven against the wall next to it and a step down."

"You went to look at it."

"I went for barley." He looked at the lane end of the street, where the fuller's boy was carrying out a vat of something that steamed. "The porter on the colonnade is the proconsul's. The baker's not."

"How do you know the baker's not?"

"He sold me bread," said Valens, "and asked was I from the games at Nemea, and I said no, and he said I had the look, and gave me back too much."

"Did you give it back?"

"I bought another loaf with it."

He had the loaf inside his tunic, and he took it out and broke it, and gave me half, warm still in the middle. The dog came back without Felix and sat in front of us and watched the bread.

That afternoon I went up Plautia's stair without being sent for.

* * *

We crossed the gulf two days after Kallia's lamp, in a boat that carried oil jars from Kirrha to Lechaion and anything else that would pay, a broad black coaster with a patched sail and a master who chewed mastic and spat it over the side when it lost its taste. The mules stood in the waist between the jars, their heads tied short to a rail and straw kicked down under their hooves, and the fig rode lashed to the foot of the mast in its sawn-off jar.

By the middle of the gulf the mules were sick.

The mules could not be sick the way a man could. They stood with their legs braced wide against the roll and the sweat coming out dark on their necks and flanks, and groaned, all together, a long low sound like furniture being dragged across a floor upstairs. The dun hung her lip and let it hang. The black kept trying to lie down, folding at the knees and catching herself, and each time Valens got a shoulder under her neck and talked her up again.

Felix was sick over the side with great industry and no breakfast.

"Why don't they spew?" He came up off the rail with his face the color of whey. "Wouldn't it help them? Does it hurt worse, if you can't? Is that why they're making that noise? Would a horse?" And he was over the rail again.

"They can't," said Valens. "It stays in them."

"All of it?"

"Hold the dun's head up. If she goes down on this she'll not get up till Lechaion."

I held the dun's head up. She put the whole hot weight of it against my chest and groaned into my himation, and her ears went flat and came up again with every roll. The drugstore on Walnut Street sold bands for seasickness, elastic, with a plastic bead sewn in to press on the inside of the wrist; Gloria's Ray swore by them, and had been sick in a pair all the way to Cape May. The dun groaned again. I rubbed the flat place between her eyes with my knuckles, and the master watched me do it over his mastic and went aft to tell the boy at the steering oar something about foreigners.

"If one of those dies on my deck," the master called forward to Valens, "you pay for the deck to be scrubbed, and the jars it falls against, and the god for the trouble."

"She won't die," said Valens.

The wind went down with the sun, as it did on that water, and we lay all night off a low coast I never saw, rolling on a long swell with the sail brailed up and the jars knocking. Sometime after the middle of the night I woke on the deck to a scraping and a grunt, and a hoof striking wood, and Valens's voice in the dark, low.

"The light."

I found it in the bag by feel and slid the switch under my thumb.

The black was down. She had folded at last in the dark between the jars and gone over onto her side, with her legs against the rail, and she could not get them under her. The beam showed me the white of her eye and the sweat on her shoulder, and Valens down in the straw at her head with her halter rope round his fist. It was a poor brown beam, yellower than it had been on the mountain, and shrinking while I held it.

"What's that?" Felix was up on one elbow on the coil of rope he had been sleeping on. "What is it? Where's the flame? Why doesn't it smoke? Is it hers? Can I hold it?"

"Get round her quarters," Valens said. "Don't push. Take the tail when I say, and pull her toward you."

"What's it burning?"

"Felix."

He went round her quarters. Valens got a sack under the black's shoulder and worked it in, and spoke to her in Latin, and when he said, Felix took the tail and threw himself back on it, and the black heaved. Her forelegs came under her first, and she sat up in the straw like a dog, trembling, with her hind end still down; then she got those under her too, and stood. Her sides went in and out. By then the beam was a smudge of yellow on her flank, no wider than my palm, and I switched it off before it could go out of its own accord.

"Does it go back on?" said Felix, in the dark.

"Go to sleep," said Valens.

Felix lay down again on his rope. Valens stayed by the black, and after a while he spoke across her, low.

"How much is left in it?"

"Not much. Some."

"Don't spend it on mules."

"You asked for it."

"I did," said Valens, and I heard him lie down in the straw between the black and the jars, where she could not go over again without going over onto him.

When the gray came, the coast was there, and the long low line of Lechaion's moles, and above everything, standing up out of the plain behind the town, the great rock of the citadel with the first sun on its top and a wall running along its crest, gone gold. Felix stood at the rail with his hands wrapped round each other.

"What's on the top of it?"

"A temple," I said. "Aphrodite's."

"Is it gold? Do they let boys up?"

"It's a long climb," said Valens, "for a boy who's been over the side all day."

"Is that the town, though? Is that Corinth? Where's the road, where they drag the ships across?"

"East of the harbor," said Valens. "Along there."

Felix looked along there for some time.

"I don't want to see it," he said, and sat down on the deck with his back to the rail.

* * *

"At the Isthmia," Plautia said, "the proconsul goes out to the god's precinct on the Isthmus with his whole staff, and the magistrates who give the games go out with him, and that half of Corinth which is neither racing nor selling goes out after them to watch the half that is, for as many days as there is racing; and my former husband, who has not missed a festival at which a proconsul might see him since he was old enough to be seen, will be in the second row behind the governor's chair in a new toga, laughing a little after the governor laughs." It was late, and the shutters were barred, and her brother was back from Patrae and asleep across the court. "The record office keeps one clerk through the festival, to sit on its door."

"Which clerk?" said Valens.

He stood inside the door with his back to the wall beside it, where he could see the whole room and the stair behind him. He had not sat in that room. He had been asked to, twice, and had said that he would stand.

"My brother's freedman says it will be the young one, who drinks, or the old one, who does not, and that both of them are fond of money." She had a cup of hot wine in her hands and did not drink it. "I shall go in myself, on the morning of the second day, at the hour the boys run, when the office is emptiest. I shall have a reason for being there that no clerk can refuse a woman of my family without writing his refusal down, and a purse that he will not wish to write down at all, and my woman with me to carry my writing case." She looked at me. "My woman reads."

"I carry your case," said Myrrhine, from the chest by the wall, in Greek. She had followed that much.

"Not this time."

"She doesn't know where the flask goes. She doesn't know which side you get down from a litter. She'll stand on your hem."

"Then you will show her where the flask goes," said Plautia, "and which side, and she will stand off my hem," and Myrrhine split a lupin with great force and said nothing more.

"What reason will you give at the door?" I said.

"My dowry. The instrument of it was entered in the colony's books when I was married, with my father's seal on it, and those books went into that office with all the others. I have the right to read it, and to have it read to me, and to have a copy made with my brother's advocate standing by. A clerk who tells a daughter of my father that she may not see her own dowry will have to say why, in writing, to a proconsul who once dined at my father's table."

"And while he is fetching it?"

"While he is fetching it," said Plautia, "my woman looks along his shelves."

"You'll be known," Valens said.

"By whom? Every man in Corinth who knows my face will be at the Isthmus, looking at boys."

"Your son," I said.

"Will be there as well, I am told, in the second row, in a new tunic, beside his father."

"You could go out to the Isthmus instead, and see him."

"From the women's benches at the top, over the heads of the whole of Corinth, for as long as a race takes to run. I have done that." She put the cup down on the floor by her chair. "This is the suit. The suit is the one thing my former husband wants of me that I have not already given him, and I will not be at the top of the benches while it walks out of a back door. And on the morning after the boys' race, when my former husband comes back from the Isthmus with the dust of it on him, you will be on a ship out of Lechaion, and he may look for you in the archive, and in the theater, and in the baths."

"I'll be at the back door," Valens said. "By the oven."

"Where every slave of my former husband's house buys its bread."

"The baker gives too much back."

"You will be at the Isthmus with your mules," said Plautia, "selling them at the horse fair to anyone who asks, where twenty men who were on the benches the day you got your sword may look at you as long as they please and tell each other they have seen Lupus, and not one of them will think to walk a morning back to Corinth to see what his wife is doing."

"No," said Valens.

"I have not finished."

"I'll be at the back door."

They looked at each other across the room, the first time I had seen them do it, and it was the look of two people at either end of a heavy thing that neither would put down first. Plautia let her end go.

"We will speak of the door when my brother has spoken to the baker," she said.

"I want the book," Valens said.

"You cannot have the book, Valens." She said his name as she said everything, every sound of it given its weight. "It is the book of the magistrate who sat at those games, the whole of his year's business, and it is boards, not a roll; there is no clerk awake or asleep who would not miss it gone, and you could not carry it across a forum under your cloak without looking like a man carrying a door."

"What holds the boards?" I said. "A cord, or rings?"

"A cord. Through holes at the back edge, and knotted, and the knot sealed."

"With his seal?"

"His, and his colleague's."

"A cord can be cut and knotted again," I said. "A seal is wax."

Plautia looked at me over the brazier.

"Can you put a seal back so that it is not seen?"

"Not so that it is not seen," I said. "So that it is not seen by a man who drinks, by the light of a doorway, with somebody talking to him. By a man who is looking for it, no."

"Then we will hope it is the young one." She turned to Valens. "You do not want the book. You want two of its boards. The leaf with your name on it is near the front, because the emperor's games were early in that year, and it is two boards that close on each other with the writing inside, and once the cord is cut they come away together and go into a writing case like any other pair. It will say Gaius Iulius Valens, freedman of Gaius, set free by his master before the magistrates of the colony and the emperor, at the games, and the names of the witnesses under it, and the day."

I had been married to a man in that house for three months, by the porter's account and the cook's, and I had had one name for him.

"Iulius," I said.

"The lanista's," said Valens. "I had it with the sword."

"I heard you give all of it," Plautia said, "in my former husband's atrium, with your hand on his household altar, the day you came to walk behind me. You said the Gaius as though it might be taken back."

"It was new," said Valens.

"When is the suit heard?" I said.

"In Rome, before a judge the praetor gives us, on a day the praetor finds for it. My brother's advocate says two years from this spring. My brother says one. The praetor has said nothing at all."

"I said I'd stand up," said Valens. "I'll stand up."

Plautia inclined her head to him, very slightly, as she might have to a man who had offered to carry something for her that she had already decided to carry herself.

Down in the court somebody called his name, high and cracked.

"Valens! The dun's out of her halter! She's in the cook's cabbages, and he's after her with a ladle, and she's kicked the lamp over!"

It was Felix, at the foot of the stair.

Valens went down. His tread went off the stair and across the court toward the stable, and the dog barked once at the porch and was quiet.

Plautia waited until the court was still. She took her cup up off the floor and held it without drinking, and when she spoke again it was in a Latin plain enough for the stableboy, every word set down by itself. She asked it of me, and not of the coals, and she watched my face the whole time she was asking: when he had his leaf, and his ship, and me, what was there left in Rome that would bring him to stand up before a court and tell it the truth about her?
