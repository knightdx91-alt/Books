The morning on which Apollo would first answer anybody that year came in under a frost so hard that Kallia's jars rang on the paving at the spring, and the water from the lion-head spouts smoked in the dark. The priests were higher up, at the rock basin, and they were more noise than shape: water slapped onto bare skin, a man praying through his teeth, and another man telling him the god had heard enough and to get his tunic on before he froze in it, while a torch went along above them and stopped. Down at the lions it was Kallia and me, and the cold, and the long noise of the water.

I had not had her to myself in three days. All that week the house had been full to the eaves. Thessalians slept in the court in their cloaks with their feet to the ashes, an Achaean snored a yard from my mat, and every hour of daylight had somebody in it who wanted bread, or hot water, or the way to the stadium. The donkey man's story had reached Kallia the morning following, through this spout: a stranger robbed and dead under the terraces, and Valens's string come on him with the oil. She had heard it out, and kept it to herself, and gone on with the pots.

I held the first jar to the spout, and while it filled I told her the rest.

I told it in the order it had been given to me. The man on the road who had known Valens from the sand; the knife; the ditch. What I had done with the thing of my father's that I carried inside my chiton, which I called only that, a heavy thing from my bag. Then the fall in the cleft, and what Valens had told me standing in it, from the sword they had sent out to him on the sand at Corinth to the painted notice by our colonnade, as near his words as I could keep them; they were plain words, and they kept. I left out his hands, which were his.

Kallia filled her own jar at the lion beside mine while I talked. Halfway through it a Thessalian came yawning down to the basin with a jug, and I stopped; Kallia sent him to the far trough with four words, and I went on when the dark had him again. At the man's death she spat, quickly, into the fold of her dress at the breast, and kept her eyes on the water and listened. At the notice she said, "Five hundred," very low, and at the wife she shifted her jar along the step with her foot. When I had come to the end of it she put the pad on her head.

"The girl that dressed the wife's hair," she said. "What became of her?"

"He did not say."

"Sold on, or sent out to his farm." She swung the jar onto the pad and found its balance one-handed. "A girl who's said that sort of thing in an atrium doesn't stay in the house after to do the hair. Bring him to the court after; the Thessalians will be up at the sanctuary till dark, and there's a pot of lentils wants eating."

I got my own jar up and went after her. The lane was still black, and the frost on every wall-top had caught what little gray there was in the sky, so that we walked home between two white lines.

"The floors will keep," she said, at our own gate. "Go up once it's day. Be home ahead of the Thessalians, or you'll be on the stair half the night with their dinners."

By the time the sun was on the cliffs the Sacred Way was solid with men.

They went up between the treasuries with wreaths of laurel on their heads, the leaves still green and the wool hanging down behind in white tails, each with his question learned by heart or folded in his belt, and an animal or a cake to go with it. A Thessalian family had a black ram that would not climb, and three sons pushing it. A man off one of the islands was selling dried figs by the handful from a basket on his hip and swearing to each new face that they were Chian. Under the Way the almonds on the terraces were all in full flower now, white, every tree together, and the smell reached the road thin and bitter, like the inside of a stone.

The women stood along the lower edge of the terrace below the temple, where the rods had put them. I found a place on the wall from which the altar and the steps were both in view, and kept well away from either.

While the terrace was still in shadow two priests brought a woman up the ramp between them. She was old, and veiled to the eyes in white wool, with a sprig of laurel in her hand. They had her by the elbows, a man to each arm, and went at the pace of her slowest foot. At the foot of the temple they halted on every step while she brought her second foot up beside the first. The crowd on the ramp made a lane for them unasked.

"That's her," a woman along the wall said to her neighbor. "She'll be going down now."

The doors took the three of them in.

The light crept down the cliff face while I waited, a gold edge sliding toward us over the gray. It found the temple roof first, then the gilt shields along the front, shield by shield, so that they lit in a row like windows down a street at dusk; last of all it reached the columns and the altar, where a priest was already feeding the fire with split pine, and the first smoke went straight up in the still air until a draft from the gorge found it and laid it over toward the theater.

Felix pushed out of the press at the ramp with the goat on her rope. She had been at the yard since sundown yesterday, as Valens had said she would be, and Felix had walked her up while it was dark behind a handful of barley; now she had a fillet of white wool tied between her horns, which he had bought for her at the foot of the Way with an obol of his own. He had washed his face for the god and missed his neck.

"That's him," he said, before he had even reached the wall. "Over there, with the Thessalians. The one from Elateia, with the ears, look at his ears. He knocked a man down at the gymnasium yesterday that had a head like a jar, and the man didn't get up till they threw a bucket on him. Would he show me his hands, do you think, if I asked him? After?"

The Elateian wore his laurel on top of ears like two folded pancakes, and was praying very hard over a cake.

"After," I said.

The cakes were sold at a stall below the altar, honey and meal, each pressed flat and stamped, on trays with a laurel leaf burned into the wood of the rim, and the stall-keeper sucking his fingers between sales. Valens stood below the altar steps with Praxias and a cake. He had a wreath on too, and the leaves sat on his cropped head like something another man had put there while he slept. The scrape on his cheekbone had crusted brown. On his left forearm, where the olive stick had landed, the welt had spread into a bruise that was purple at its heart and yellow toward the elbow, and that hand's knuckles had scabbed over, all four. He held his shoulders carefully, all of a piece, and when he wanted to see behind him he brought his whole body round, hips and all.

Praxias had left his quail at home, but his left arm kept the crook of it anyway; now and then his fingers went over nothing there, smoothing.

I paid him first, at the wall, out of the rag, in coins from the ford, into his hand. He weighed the silver in his palm and found it enough. He glanced at what the road had done to Valens, and back at the coins, which were plainly the only part of the morning that concerned him.

"Even money she stands like a post," he told me, of the goat. "They do, in a frost. A frost-morning goat will take a spring's worth on her head and think about her dinner."

"If she does?"

"Next month. And then you pay again, Syracusan," he said, and liked the sound of that.

I had the Advil in my belt, down to its last two. Before Valens could follow him up the steps I tipped both into my palm where my himation hid them, and offered them low between us, where nobody else could see what they were.

"For the shoulders," I said. "Take them with water, and inside an hour you could turn your head without your hips."

He did not touch the two small white things in my palm.

"After," he said. "I want my head, in there."

I put them back in the bottle. He was watching the crowd past my shoulder while I did it: the ramp, the steps, the faces at the wall.

"Have you seen them?" I said.

"Not yet." His eyes went on to the wall's end. "If you see the one you hit, send the boy to me in the line. Stay among the women."

"And the boy?"

"The boy does as he's told."

The altar was the great one the Chians had paid for, dark stone with a lip of white marble along it, gone brown with old fat where the fire was. The priest at it was a thin man with his himation pinned high on one shoulder. A boy stood by him with a bronze jug beaded with cold. Felix led the goat up, and Valens had the rope from him on the last step. The priest spoke to the god in a voice the noise of the terrace took away, tipped the jug, and let a thin stream run down between her horns and over her face.

She stood. Water dripped from her ears and her beard, and she shut her eyes against it.

He poured again, along her neck this time. One ear flicked. That was all.

The women round me had stopped talking. Below the steps Praxias was telling the nearest man that he had said so.

The priest spoke short to the boy, and the boy went at a run. He came back under a full jar, from wherever they kept the water, and the priest lifted it off his shoulder and emptied the whole of it along her back, from the fillet to the tail, in one long cold sheet.

She shook. It began in her hooves and climbed through all four legs and went through her body to the end of her tail, and her whole hide jumped with it, and the water flew off her onto Valens and the priest and the boy. The priest told the terrace in a loud voice that the god was receiving, as if somebody might want to argue the point.

It was the most comforting thing I had seen in Delphi. The god could be helped along. It took a jar.

The priest's knife was quick, and Valens kept his grip on the horns until it was over. Felix stood at the side of the altar and watched all of it, the cut and the blood going into the dish and the boy beginning on the skin, in silence. Later he was over among the Thessalians by the Elateian, studying his hands.

Valens went with Praxias into the line of wreathed men at the foot of the temple ramp, and the line closed behind them.

* * *

He told it in Kallia's court, well on in the afternoon, with a bowl of her lentils going cold on his knee.

For once the court was empty, the lodgers all still up at the sanctuary, eating their sacrifices or arguing over what they had been given, and the house had gone quiet, after a week of being loud, with the lodgers' blankets hung airing on the gallery in the sun. Kallia had put him on the stool by the woodstack with the wall at his back and set the bowl in his hands, and returned to the lean-to, from which she could hear every word.

Felix had the bottom step of the stair, and I had the hearthstone.

"Praxias called his name at the door," Valens said. "Praxias son of Kleon, of Delphi, for the house of Kallia. He gave it out twice, the second for the Thessalians' benefit."

"They wrote it down?"

"A priest at a table wrote it. Beard cut square." He ate a spoonful of the lentils. "He put his nose a hand from mine to make me out. He was slow about it, and he knew the face."

"He puts his nose that close to everything," I said. "He could not find the join on his own grandfather's incense stand with a lamp held to it."

"Euthydikos," Kallia said, inside the lean-to.

"He asked was it the mender. The Syracusan of Kallia's who did the stand." Valens scraped at the bowl. "I told him yes. He said it over as he wrote: Maira, of Syracuse, of the house of Kallia, a kinswoman. He asked the carrier's name after that, and I gave him Felix."

"Me? In the god's book?" He was up. "In the priest's own writing? Where did he put me, under her? Under Praxias? Was it wax he wrote it on, or the other, the—"

"Wax," Valens said.

"What did it look like?"

"Small."

Felix stood with that. He sat down again on the stair, slowly for him, and asked nothing more. His ears had gone red to the tips.

"There's writing on the wall in the porch," Valens said. "Big. Men stop at it and move their lips."

"What's it for?" said Felix.

"Know yourself," I said. "And another. Nothing too much."

Felix waited for the rest of it. "That's all? On a god's house? My mother could have told them that." He stopped, surprised at himself, and scowled at the pomegranate.

"After the writing, the doors," Valens said. "Inside it's dark. You come in out of the sun and for a while there's nothing; there's the smoke. They burn laurel on a hearth in there, and barley on top of the laurel, and it gets in your eyes. The floor slopes to the back, not much, but a man with bad feet would feel it. It's polished in a path where they walk, and on the right-hand side the stone's darker, where men have stood to wait."

"Darker with what?" I said.

"Their feet." He set the bowl on the stones by his foot. "There's one door. I looked for another. There's a bench along that side, and a man with a rod at either end. You sit on it and wait your turn, and nobody looks at anybody. There was a Boeotian next to me wouldn't stop praying. There's a chair by the wall too, iron, old, that nobody sits in. The Boeotian went to sit in it while he waited, and the rods moved him."

I knew what that chair was. A poet had sat in it to sing for the god, five hundred years ago, and they had kept it for him ever since against his return, and the poet had been a Boeotian.

"Was he angry?" I said.

"He said a chair was a chair."

"Nothing else? In the whole room?"

"Smoke. Men."

"The stone," I said. "Rounded on top, about so high, carved over with a kind of net. It must be in there."

"There's a stone."

"What was it like?"

"Stone color," he said. "It had wool on it, tied over in a net, and it came to my waist. Somebody had hung a wreath on it. A man was posted beside it watching the bench from start to finish, and I didn't care for him, so I watched him." The bowl by his foot was empty. "He had a cough."

I would have given a year of my life for ten minutes in that room with a lamp, and the most famous stone in the world had reached me as a man with a cough standing next to it. On the stair, Felix would have liked to hear more about the cough.

"Where was the woman?" I said.

"At the back there's a step down, and a screen in front of it, wood, with a cloth over. She's down there where nobody sees her. They call you off the bench, and you stand at the screen, on the spot they give you, and the priest stands between you and it, sideways, so he can watch both. He reads the question off the wax, at the screen, in an ordinary voice. When he got to Syracuse the Boeotian stopped praying to say ask a ship. After that you wait."

"For how long?"

"The Boeotian started up again at my back. He'd had his already: a boundary stone his brother moved. The god told him to plant olives down the boundary and let the trees argue it." He was facing the wall across the court. "She talked, in the end, from down under the floor, a woman, not loud. It went on a while, and then it broke off partway, and stayed broken off. The men on the bench started to look at each other. Then it went on again, quicker, and stopped for good."

"What did she sound like?"

"Hoarse. Higher than I'd have put her." He thought. "Tired."

"Did you hear her own words, or only the voice?"

"Some. Not to keep." He touched the crust on his cheekbone, found it there, and took his hand away. "There was a word in it I didn't know, not Greek, and she gave it twice, slower at the end."

"What word?"

"I didn't keep it. It wasn't in what he gave me."

"Try."

"It's gone," Valens said.

"Surely the priest heard it."

"He had his hand up as long as she talked." Valens lifted his scarred hand, flat, the palm out toward the woodstack, about level with his ear, and held it so. "Like that, to the screen. When she finished he put it down and turned to me and said it."

"In her words."

"In his. Hers were fewer; that much was plain. He listened to all of hers, and after it he gave me his. At the end he asked had I anything to put to the god on my own account, while I was at the screen, seeing the goat had shaken."

"Had you?"

"I told him the goat was hers."

"Did anybody stop you, coming out?"

"Praxias, in the porch. He wanted it for the quail ring; there's men there lay money on what the god tells strangers, and a Syracusan's worth something to them. I told him it was hers."

* * *

Outside, the morning went on without him.

The line at the foot of the ramp moved by fits, and in between not at all, and those who had stood in it since first light began on the corners of their cakes. Delphians had gone in first. After them a man with a rod appeared on the ramp and called the next lot from the head of it by their city, and the men of that city rose off the steps where they had been sitting, all together, and gathered up their cakes and their questions and went, and the rest shifted along. The rod came out again and called Phocians. Valens and Praxias were well behind the Phocians, behind Achaeans and Locrians and a whole ship of Coans who had paid to go ahead of somebody.

The women talked about their questions, having no other use for them until the men came out. Next to me a woman from Amphissa had sent her husband in about a well. "North of the house, or south," she told us. "South is where his mother planted her fig."

"North," her other neighbor said. "Always north. The south dries out."

"He'll put it so the god says south," she said. "To spite me. You'll see." She turned to me. "And yours? What's yours about?"

"A road."

"Roads are easy," she said, comforted on my account. "He likes a road. Which is yours, in the line?"

"The tall one. With the bruise on his face."

She found him and gave him some thought. "Is he yours?"

"He is carrying for me."

"I said that about mine," she said, "and look where it got me."

My feet were cold and the wall was cold and I stayed on it. I let myself have it then, for the length of the line. A road: a cleft behind the mountain, a cave I had missed, a day in the summer when the shut rock would breathe. I would go through it the way I had gone in, crawling, in the dark, and come out into cold air off the plateau with a road below it that had a white line painted down its middle, and there would be the bus, and a phone in the café at Delphi that took a card. In my apartment the heat would be off, and I would turn it on and stand over the vent in the hall in my socks until it ran warm, and eat whatever was in the freezer, standing, with a fork.

Toward midday the slave who shelled beans on the stool by Euthydikos's bay tree came along below the wall, looking up into each woman's face in turn, and stopped below mine.

"You're Kallia's," he said. "The mender."

"Yes."

"The bowl wants doing when the god's finished for the year. The one with the lead in it. You're to come up when you're sent for." He had a look at me, and at the women round me, who were all listening. "You'll be sent for."

"The lead has held it this long," I said. "Another year will do it no harm."

He went on to wherever his errand took him next. It was a season's work, if I wanted it, cutting the smith's lead out of that bowl without marking the bronze, and I wanted none of it. Before the bowl's turn came I meant to be gone.

Below where we stood a man had a board of clay ears on a trestle, for anybody who wanted the god to hear him better: little pink ears with a hole at the top for hanging, all of them from a single mold. They were all left ears. I checked the board twice over to be sure, and there was not a right ear on it, and somebody had given each one a dab of red paint inside, and I was asking myself whether he had lost the right-hand mold or never owned one, or whether there was some reason a god should only care to be spoken to on the left, when Felix vaulted the wall beside me.

He went straight between the women and down the bank and into the crowd at the foot of the ramp. I saw what he had seen a breath after he did: a man standing among the Phocians with a strip of linen wound about his head, covering an ear, and a dark stain on the linen.

I scrambled down the bank behind him.

When I reached them Felix had hold of the man's cloak. He was a head taller than Felix and very young, a boy with his first beard, and he stared down at Felix with his jaw hanging. The stain on the linen was wine.

"Fell off his donkey," said the friend beside him, delighted, with an arm round his neck. "Riding in from the plain. Drunk as a Boeotian, and singing."

"I'm a Phocian," said the one in the linen, and one of the rods came along to see what the noise was, and Felix dropped the cloak and was at my side before it arrived, admiring the sky.

"I took him for the one you hit," he said, as we made for the wall again. "I'd have had him."

"Blood goes brown when it dries," I said.

"I never said about your father's silver thing," he said. "To anybody. The Elateian asked me yesterday at the gymnasium what the man was hit with. He'd heard about the man at the ford. A stone, I told him." He was quiet for three steps. "It wasn't a stone, was it?"

"It was heavy," I said.

Felix vanished again before long, and returned with his right hand held stiff before him like something borrowed.

"He let me," he said. "The Elateian. Feel. No, feel there, on the knuckles; that's from Thebes, that knuckle killed the man. It's harder than wood." He held it away from his side until we had our places on the terrace again. "He said I'm too thin. He said come back when I've eaten."

My neighbor's husband came out and walked straight past her to the men he had climbed with. He told them first, all three; they laughed and clapped him on the back, and afterward he came over to the wall and told her it was to be south. She heard him out.

"North, then," she said to me, when he had rejoined his friends. "I'll dig it at night, and tell him the god changed his mind."

Valens came out past noon, alone. Praxias had gone off up past the theater toward the stadium and the quail ring with his wreath still on. Valens came under the terrace with his wreath in his hand. At the wall he searched the women's faces, and the men's behind them, and came to a stand before me.

"Well?" I said.

"Not here."

"Is it bad?"

"At Kallia's." He had gone on down the Way, and I went after him with Felix at my heels.

"He goes like this when a mule dies," Felix told me, low, at the second hairpin.

"Has one died?"

"Not this year. Not that he's told me."

Valens was silent the length of the Way, past the treasuries and the bronze bull and the stalls at the gate, and the farther we went in silence, the surer I was of what it was. If the god had given me a road, a breath on the terrace would have held it. A road could be said in front of anybody. What wanted a roof and a wall round it was the other answer: that there was no road; the hole I had come in by was the only one and it was shut; that I was a Syracusan now, and the god had looked at the registrar's tablet and agreed with it.

By the lions I had it settled. I was almost calm.

* * *

When he had told that much, he got to his feet.

He had not been asked to stand. Out in the center of the court, beyond the shade of the gallery, he set his feet apart under him with his weight on both of them evenly and his hands open at his sides, and fixed on the wall above the woodstack. The words came in one breath, with the stops in them where the priest must have put them, in a voice that was new to me.

"The road home lies under the mountain of vines by the western sea, where the dead go out of the city, at the door painted on a dead man's house."

He stood on a little longer, listening for more, and when there was none he sat again.

The hair had stood up along both my arms under the wool, to the shoulder, and it would not lie down again. Over the woodstack the wall was bare but for a lizard, flat in the sun with its toes splayed.

It scanned. Most of it went in the measure of Homer, the long line, and the last part hung off the end of it like a suitcase roped onto the roof of a car that was already full. The priest was in it as plainly as the god.

"Somebody was paid to paint that door." Kallia had not come out from her pot. "Somebody's sons paid him, and argued the price."

"That's the whole of it," said Valens. "That's how he gave it."

"Didn't the priest say more?" Felix said, on the stair. "You were in there two men's turns."

"After, he said she was a Syracusan, wasn't she, it was on the wax, so it was plain enough. Her own mountain, he said. The one that burns behind her city, with the vines up its sides. He said that was a fine answer for a Syracusan and I was to tell her so. I'm telling you."

The priest had read Syracuse off his own wax and found my mountain for me, the one that smoked behind my invented home, and the god had very kindly agreed with the clerk and Kallia and the whole of the lie. Except that Etna stood above the eastern sea, the one Syracuse looked out on, and the god, in the priest's verse, had said west. The priest held a lamp to things in full sun, and I did not suppose he had ever looked at a map.

"Did the priest give you anything about when?" I said. "A month, a year. Anything."

"No."

"West of what? West of here? Everything is west of here."

"Is your country west?" Valens said.

"Very far west. Farther than any of this."

"Where do they put the dead, there?"

"In fields," I said. "With fences round them, and the grass kept short, and a stone with the name on it for each. In the towns, some of them, among the houses."

"Inside the walls?"

"There are no walls."

"Where does the town stop, then?"

"Where a board by the road says it does," I said.

"Italy's west," said Felix, who had left the stair. "What if it's Italy? They've the schools at Capua in Italy, the big ones, a sailor at Kirrha says, with the iron gate, and the fighters eat meat every day and they've a doctor all to themselves. They take boys if they're quick. If it's Italy, can I come?"

Valens rose and took his bowl to the lean-to, and put it in Kallia's hands.

"The string's had no water since daybreak," he told Felix, on his way back.

Felix looked from him to me and back, and went. He went backward as far as the gate, and from the gate he ran.

Kallia scraped at the pot. "The boats go west at the new moon."

I had seen a tomb's painted door once, or a photograph of one, thrown six feet wide in our kitchen one September night, when my father had his carousel out and a bedsheet tacked across the china for a screen: the rear wall of a chamber at Tarquinia, cut into the soft rock under a hill, with a false door on the plaster in dark red, shut, and a mourner at each side of it, palm to his forehead. He had clicked the carousel round to Volterra while my question about what lay behind it was half out, and the fan in the projector had gone on breathing out its smell of hot dust in the dark room. "Every road out of every town in Italy has tombs along it," I said. "That is where they bury, outside the gate, beside the road out, so that anyone going anywhere goes past the dead. Tombs with their doors painted on. In Etruria they painted whole rooms under the hills, and doors on the back walls of the rooms, and Etruria is by a western sea, with vines on its hills." I heard myself and kept on. "Or Sicily after all, or Massalia, or Spain. There is a mountain with vines on it beside every sea the Romans have ever reached. It is not an address. It is a description of the empire."

"It's what he said."

"I know it is."

"Then it's something."

"It is a riddle," I said. "A riddle is nothing. I might walk ten years with it and find a plaster door every other mile."

He had his eyes on me, the bruised side of his face to the sun.

"You'll try the cave again," he said.

"Tomorrow."

"It's shut."

"I want to see it shut for myself."

The court was quiet.

"Only the mountain part," I said. "Say just that piece again, from its beginning."

He began, and broke off. He began over at the very first word, and came forward through it at the same pace, every stop in its old place, as far as the sea; there he stopped himself, and his hand lifted an inch from his knee, the way you would check a man walking beside you.

"You cannot start it in the middle," I said.

"A trainer at the school called the forms. Eight or ten cuts at a breath, in a string, and you answered him with a practice blade of wood, cut for cut, in his order, out in the sun. Get one wrong and you started again from the first. You don't go in at the fourth. I can't take it from the sea onward. I'd have to start at the beginning."

"Do not," I told him. "It is in my head."

It was. I had it word for word, first to last, in his voice and in the priest's measure, and it was as much use to me as a bus ticket with the town torn off.

"Why his name?" I said. "At the table."

That took him a while, or whether to answer it did. "The boy wanted it written down somewhere."

"Yours?"

"Mine's written. At Corinth."

Kallia left the lean-to with the pot on her hip and the sand in her other hand, and stood over him.

"You'll not go down that road tonight," she said.

"The string, though."

"The boy can sleep with the string. He'd sooner. You'll sleep here."

"Your house is full to the roof."

"There's floor," said Kallia, and took the pot to the jar to scour it.

* * *

The jug boy was at the gate while Kallia was still scouring the pot.

I knew him for the boy from the altar, who had run for the jar. The front of his white tunic was dark with spilled water, and he had a cloth-covered basket hooked over one arm; he stopped at the gatepost. He took in Valens on the stool and Kallia at the jar, and saved his longest look for me.

"The Syracusan," he said. "The woman that asked about a road. Theano wants her."

"Who is Theano?" I said.

"The god's woman," said Kallia. "Up the street, almost to the end of it. She'll have you carry her water up while you're there. She does that."

The boy shifted the basket on his arm. "Now. She said now, before her knees set."

Valens stood.

"Her by herself," he told Valens, and the rest came fast, to Valens and not to me. I caught "no men" in it, and "muleteer", and something about the crows.

"She'll not eat her," Kallia told Valens.

Valens did not sit down again. He left the court before we did and halted at the corner where the lane met the street. There he set his shoulders to the wall with the whole climb of the street in front of him, to the head of the town and every gate along it; he was standing so, still, when the boy and I came by him on our way to the top.

"The house with the crows on it," he said as I passed, watching the street above. "Just under the top."

Supposing she wanted money, I climbed. Most things in that town did, and the god's woman would want more than most, for something the god had already said. The street was empty of men, who were all still at the sanctuary or asleep after it, and the women had thrown their doors open to the sun, so that I climbed past a row of rooms laid open like cupboards: a woman carding wool on her step, a girl plucking a hen into a basket, an old fellow asleep upright against a wine jar with his chin on his chest and a kitten in his lap. The boy walked in the middle of the street, well clear of the eaves. Every so often he lifted a corner of the cloth to make sure of whatever was under it.

"Forty-one jars," he told me. "Since the dark. Spring to altar and back, and the jug besides. Your goat took a jug and a jar. Most take the one jug." He hitched the basket higher. "The Thessalians' ram took two jars, and then it ran."

"Did the god receive it?"

"He received it after they caught it."

It was the house next to the highest. I knew it before the boy stopped. A squared stone was set at the door, cut by some mason long ago and gone round at the corners with being sat on, and along the ridge of the roof above it a dozen crows waited in a row, hunched against the cold, beaks to the wind.

She was on the stone, old, in a dark himation that had been good once, with a farmer's hands, square, the nails broken short, and a face the sun had burned brown and crumpled under the eyes. Under the hem her feet were bare on the stone, broad, cracked at the heels, feet that had walked behind a plow.

"Give it here," she said to the boy, and he gave her the basket and was off downhill, glad to. She took the cloth off, and it was barley, the parched kind. A laurel leaf was burned into the rim of the basket, small and brown.

She flung a fistful over the ruts, and the crows dropped from the ridge in a black slither.

"Look at that," she said. "Robbers. The minute a woman sits down." Another fistful. "Get off, you, you've had yours. You're the Syracusan."

"Yes."

"Syracusan," said Theano, as flat as a price for a lame ewe, and left it at that. "Sit down; my neck won't go back that far. Not there. That's the cat's." She waited until I was on her doorstep, below her, and went on slowly, taking her breath where she could get it. "Seven steps down into that hole, and seven to climb again, and the priests stand about at the top with their hands in their sleeves, chattering of the god's breath, and not a man of them will carry a stool down after me. They've stools; they've a stool for the square beard, so he can sit and scratch at his wax. I asked them for one at the vintage. They said they'd put it to the council. The council! For a stool! And meanwhile down I go on these and up I come on these, all morning, with a goat's worth of waiting at the top before every one. My husband dragged this stone here for me before he died, so I'd have somewhere to sit outside my own house, and it's the best thing he ever did, and he did it with two oxen and a lever and a deal of cursing, and he'd have carried a stool down a hole for me, he would, and held my arm on the steps." She pressed her knees with the heels of her hands. "Listen to them. You can hear them from there, can't you?"

I could not hear them.

"Which is worse for them," I said, "the steps down or the steps up?"

"Down," said Theano, to the crows. "Up, I can lean on the wall. Fourteen years I've had that god, since my husband went. He's no worse than any man you work for: he wants you there before it's light, and he's never satisfied with the goat, and he takes the credit. When I started there were three of us to take turns, and my turn came one month in three, and between turns I was home milking. Now there's only me, every month he sits, and nobody milks, and the ewes are my sister's son's, and he sells the cheese at the stadium and keeps the price of it."

Behind her the door stood open on one room, swept, with a loom against the end wall and a half-woven blanket on it, brown and white in stripes, and a cat on the hearth asleep in the warm ash with its paws tucked under it. Nothing in it was the god's.

The bottle had ridden in my belt all day. I shook the two pills out of it and held them up to her where she sat on her husband's stone.

"What's that?"

"For the knees. They take the ache down, for half a day. Swallow them whole, with water, and do not chew them."

She looked at them, and took them off my palm with two fingers. A cup stood at the stone's foot. She put both pills in her mouth at once, drank from the cup and swallowed, wiped her mouth on the back of her wrist, and threw another fistful of barley at the crows.

"That's my own barley," she said. "Off my own field, below the stadium. I wouldn't give them a grain of his."

"Why give them any?"

"Because they come." She shook the last husks off her fingers. "They were up on that ridge the day my husband went out of this door feet first, the whole row of them, and not a crow of them made a sound, which the mourners never managed. A thing that'll sit quiet at a burying can have my barley."

The brand on the basket was by her foot. I left it there.

"That big one that stood up for you," she said. "At the screen. You hear them from down there, every man of them, shifting about over your head like a mule in a stall, scraping, changing feet, and there was a Boeotian this morning prayed the length of my talking. Yours stood. I'd have said nobody was there, except the floor gave when he stepped to it. The rest of them, I can tell you what they ate before they came."

"He is careful where he stands," I said.

"They put a mountain in it," said Theano. "And a sea, and a painted tomb, and something to tap your foot to. They always do; whatever I bring up from down there, they take it and put shoes on it. He said it short and he said it plain. Nobody pays to travel down from Thessaly for short and plain."

"My carrier heard a word in it that was not Greek. Twice, he told me. You gave it twice."

"Not Greek, so not the god's, says the square beard. The smoke talking, or the woman down the hole. The god's been everywhere; he gets about more than they do."

"What was it he said? Down there, before they dressed it."

"That's not what you climbed my street for."

"You sent for me," I said. "Why?"

"Because my knees hurt," said Theano, "and I wanted to tell somebody who hadn't heard it. Ask."

It was in my mouth already, the thing I had wanted since the rock shut on me, and all morning at the wall on the terrace it had sat there, behind the road.

"When?" I said. "On which day is it open? The road."

"That's not mine to give you." She dusted her palms together. "Or it was never given me. Which of those it is, is mine to know."

"I can pay. There is silver."

"Keep your silver. Whatever's paid on this hill goes to the priests, and I get the steps."

"Was there a day in it? Was there one, and they cut it?"

"You've had your answer to that," said Theano, watching the crows. "Ask the other."

I had one question left.

"Which city?"

Theano shifted on the stone, and grunted with it. "Pompeii."
