I had scraped the floor of the crack down to bare rock as far in as my arm would reach, and now I knelt at the bend with Kallia's ash shovel, chopping at a crust of earth that had frozen into plates. Each plate came up with a skin of rime on its underside. Below it the dirt was black and gritty and packed hard, and cold under my palm.

Everything in there was cold. In May this rock had let the cold out as one thread, dry, with an edge to it, while the walls around it stayed damp and slow. Now the whole mountain had turned to cold: the rock, the air, the iron of the shovel, which had taken the skin off the pad of my thumb some while ago. Nothing could have come through that crack that I would have known from the rest.

I dug anyway. The shovel was meant for ashes, with a blade the width of my palm, and every time it struck the rock underneath it gave a flat little note that ran off along the hall behind me and answered itself, late.

The torch spat. Its light jumped up the face of the slab and dropped again.

"Your fingers are white," Valens said.

"They're fine."

That was English, and he let it pass. He was standing on the flowstone a little below me with his feet set across two of its ridges, holding the pine up and off to one side so that his own shadow fell away from where I was working. He had stood like that since we came in. When a torch burned down he lit the next from it and ground the stub out under his boot, and went on standing. Behind him my duffel leaned on the slab, zipped, the strap laid ready over it.

I pressed my hand flat in the bottom of the bend. The tips of my fingers went white and then stopped hurting, which was worse. Nothing moved across them.

"What hour is it?"

"The light's off the mouth. Past the middle of the day."

"The hour. On the watch."

He found it in the pouch at his belt and did not give it to me. He let me see it in the torchlight, lying on his palm, its steel gone dull with the cold and the band hanging down between his fingers, and he kept his thumb on the end of the band.

Ten past one, or near it; I had set the hands on my second day at Kallia's by the shortest shadow of her gatepost.

In May the cold had come at twenty minutes short of two. It was nothing to go on, and I had nothing else.

"Wait until the long hand is at the eight," I said.

"How long is that?"

"From Kallia's gate to the spring and back. With a full jar."

"Most of a pine, then."

I bent to the shovel again.

"There's snow coming over the ridge," he said behind me. "I saw it from the mouth. We're off the stair before it comes, or we're up here till morning. There's nothing up here for the mules."

"Take the mules and go down. Leave me one of the pines. I will come after you."

"You came up that stair holding the gray's tail."

"I shall not need to come down."

He was quiet so long that I heard the drip, far back in the hall, falling into its basin.

"You won't get over the top in this," he said.

"I said so yesterday. I am not crossing anything."

"You're coming down with me. I'm paid for the down as well as the up."

"Give me until the long hand stands at the eight. Only that. Stand there and hold the light and give me so much, and if nothing happens, I will come down with you, and I will not say one word between here and the widow's gate. And you may have the shovel also."

"It's not your shovel."

"It is the widow's. She shall have it back."

"You brought the bag in," he said. "Into a hole in a rock."

"Yes."

"It goes back down on the other mule, with you on the gray. I'm not carrying it up here twice."

The plate under the blade came away whole, broad as a dinner plate, and under it there was rock, the same gray rock, with a little water standing in a pit of it under a lid of ice. I broke the ice with a knuckle. Water. Only water, and cold, and my own breath going white over it in the torchlight.

"Two more pines after this one," Valens said. "Then down, whatever your hole's doing."

* * *

The afternoon before, he had brought a string of mules up Kallia's lane, loaded to the ears with split oak, and stopped them short of the gate, and walked on by himself, looking at the ground.

The ground was ice from the gate to the turn, and the ice was mine. Twice a day for most of a month I had carried water up the lane on my hip, because I still could not do it on my head, and every step slopped a little onto the stones; since the nights had turned, the slops had stopped drying and begun to freeze, one coat on another, like the glaze on a cheap plate.

My plan at the spring had not lasted the first day. I had the hearth cleared and two jars in before light, as I had meant to, and Kallia was waiting for me in the court with the broom. After the court there were the floors; after the floors the pots the Thebans left, and a Theban who wanted his cloak beaten, and then it was dark. On the fourth morning I reached the top of the town with the duffel before it came to me that I had no idea of the way. I'd come into the town by the long road round, and between the plain and Delphi stood only cliff. I went back down and did the floors.

Since then the snow had come a little lower on Parnassus every night. I watched it at the lions while the second jar filled, first the summits and then the shoulders and then the rim of the cliffs above the sanctuary, white along its edge like a glass dipped in sugar. I had spoken no Greek aloud since the guide on the Sacred Way. Kallia gave me orders my hands could understand without me, and I followed them, and whatever got away from me got away in English, and Kallia worked on through it.

He walked back to his mules. I was in the gateway with the empty jar on my hip.

"Ashes," he said. "For the ice. Before they come up."

A woman in a gateway with a jar was whoever you asked for ashes, so I went and got them. The pail stood by the hearth where I left it every morning, half full, with Kallia's little iron shovel upright in it. He took the pail from me at the gate and went up the lane throwing the ashes in low, flat fans, so that they fell where a hoof would come down and nowhere else. A scar the length of my finger ran over the back of his right hand. Whoever closed it had been in a hurry and set the stitches too far apart, and it had healed in a ladder, every rung raised and white.

In the ice storm of January '94 the man in the ground-floor apartment on Larchwood had appeared at six in the morning in his bathrobe and poured a whole bag of cat litter down the front steps. In March he moved out. Nobody in the building ever learned his name.

He brought the mules up singly. They were big, dark, dusty animals with the oak lashed onto wooden packsaddles, and each one stopped at the edge of the ice, put a forefoot on the ashes, and considered. The last was a gray who would not consider anything. He leaned on her shoulder and said something into her ear, low and level, in Latin. *Ambula, asini filia.* Walk on, daughter of a donkey.

It was the plain truth about her, and I laughed.

He looked across her neck at me, then down at the ice under her feet. The gray walked on.

Kallia was in the gateway behind me.

"Not in my court, Valens," she said. "Unload in the lane."

She had put her back against the trunk of the pomegranate, with her arms crooked out from her sides, like a crossing guard who had lost her sign.

"She had that tree to the height of her head last winter," she said.

"That was the brown."

"Then she can stand in the lane and think about the brown."

So we unloaded in the lane. Kallia pulled a split off the first load and smelled the cut end of it.

"Wet."

"Split this month," he said. "It'll burn."

"It'll spit. The last lot spat a coal on a Theban's cloak, and I paid for the cloak."

"That was pine. This is oak."

"Then it'll spit oak." She pointed the split at me. "You. Under the stair. Bark up."

I carried. He unlashed the loads and handed the splits down to me in armfuls, and I built the stack under the outside stair with the ends laid crosswise, two this way and two that, so that it would stand on its own and not lean on Kallia's wall, which had enough to hold up already. On my fourth trip he looked at the ends.

"Who showed you that?"

"Don't wait for an answer," Kallia said. "She doesn't talk."

He handed me the next armful.

"Five the load," he said to Kallia, when the last of it was in.

"Four. It's wet."

"Five. It's oak."

"Four now, and the fifth when I've seen it burn without spitting."

She paid him from a knot in the corner of her kerchief, and after that the two of them went at it in Phocian too fast for me to follow, until he gave her one coin back. I thought it was for the spitting. She tied the knot up again, more slowly than she had untied it.

"Is the sea shut yet?" she said.

"The last ship went out of Kirrha eight days ago."

"Did anything come in from the east before it went?"

"Oil. Wine from Crete. A man with dogs to sell."

"Letters."

"I don't carry letters."

"Nobody asked what you carry."

"Kallia!" A Theban hung off the gallery. "Are those lentils ever coming?"

"When the hearth comes," she shouted back, and went in to it, and I was alone in the lane with him and five empty mules.

He was working along the string, tightening girths. Above the roofs the cliffs had gone gray-blue and the snow along their rim had turned pink, and that was nearly all the day had left.

"You go up on the mountain," I said.

The sentence left me with all its breathings in, in the voice of a woman reading aloud to a seminar. The guide had made a whole square laugh at that voice, and I had promised myself on the spot that the town would never hear it again, and here it was, going out into the lane.

He finished the girth he was on. "For wood. Not high."

His Greek was as foreign as mine. He spoke it slowly and flatly, every ending in its place, a man setting things down one by one where he could find them later, and it was the first Greek in Delphi that came to me without my having to reach for it.

"Higher," I said. "Above the cliffs and over the plain. To the Korykian cave."

"Pan's cave. What's up there?"

"Where I came in."

He turned round from the mule then.

"The charcoal men had a story at the vintage," he said. "A goat boy up there saw a nymph walk out of Pan's cave in trousers and eat his cheese."

"He gave me the cheese."

"That's not how he tells it."

"He spat out mine. I gave him something to eat and he spat it out on the path."

"He tells that part." He took in my boots, which had carried me off the mountain in October and had not had a day off since. "So you walked over the mountain. In the autumn. Alone."

"From in there. Not over anything."

"And now you want to go back into it."

"Tomorrow."

"Not in snow." He moved on to the next mule. "Look at it."

I had been looking at it for a month.

"The stair's ice from the second turn," he said. "The plain's snow. Nobody's up there now but the charcoal men, and they're coming down."

"How long is it to the cave?"

"In summer, a morning. In this, a morning and more."

"And down again?"

"Before dark, if you're quick in there. Why tomorrow? It'll be there in the spring."

"I will not."

Across the mule's back I could not tell what he made of that, and I did not explain it; I could not have in any language.

"I will go up without you," I said.

"You'll go off the stair where the ice starts. The widow carries her own water again. In the spring somebody goes up to bring down what's left of you."

"The danger is mine."

"It's his. Whoever goes up in the spring."

"I can pay."

"With what?"

I had two taxi coins left in my jeans, and I offered him the bigger. He pinched it up off my palm and tipped it to the last of the day: brass, and the profile of a young man with the wind in his hair.

"Who's that?"

"A king. From farther away than you would believe."

"That buys nothing up there. Or down here." He returned the coin. Then he looked at my hands.

They had been soft at the spring a month before. Now every knuckle had split from the cold water, and a blister was healing on each palm where the jar handles rode. They were still not a working woman's hands, and he saw it, and he saw that I knew he had.

"Those never carried a jar till this month," he said.

"No."

"You talk a schoolmaster's Greek. You've got Latin." He meant the gray mule and my laughing. "You've got that." He meant the coin. "Who's coming after you?"

"No one is coming after me. In this whole country not one person knows my name. The widow has not asked it."

"A husband."

"I have no husband."

"A father, then."

The buckle was stiff with the cold. It took me two tries to work the tongue out of the hole, and then I had it off my wrist, and I passed it to him across the gray mule's withers, still warm on the inside of the band.

"This was my father's."

"You took it from him."

"He does not need it."

He was looking the watch over the way he had looked over the lane, all of it, the back first: the steel case and its scratches, the little crown on the side, the band with the three links gone. Then he turned it face up. The thin seconds hand was going round. He watched it make one full circuit, and his face stayed as it was.

"Something's going in it," he said.

"It tells the hours." I had no word for it except an old one. "A *hōrologion*."

He looked from the watch to the sky over the lane, where the pink had gone off the snow, and back to the watch.

"Where's the shadow?"

"It does not need one. It needs no sun and no water. It goes in the dark."

"By itself."

"If it is wound. Every morning." I pointed. "Here."

He tried it. His thumb covered the crown entirely, and the rest of his hand closed round the case to keep it still, and with great care, slowly, he turned the crown the wrong way. It gave the small loose click it gave when you went backward. He stopped to listen to the click. Then he turned it again, with the same care, the same way, as if the click were what it was for.

My father had wound it at the kitchen counter each morning of my childhood while the percolator went, talking all the while about something else.

I let him turn it twice more. Then I had my voice.

"Stop. The other way."

He stopped and let me at it, the watch lying flat in his palm, and I turned the crown forward for him, three turns, until I felt it tighten.

"That way. Until it will not go any more. Never past that."

He did it himself. Forward, the click was finer and closer together, and the crown pushed back against his thumb when the spring was full, and he felt it and stopped.

"How long does it go?"

"A day and a night. A little more."

"And if a man forgets?"

"It stops. You wind it, and it goes again."

He tried to put the band round his own wrist. It would not go halfway. The watch disappeared into the pouch at his belt, and he drew the string.

"Paid now," he said. "Not up there. Now."

"Yes."

"Before light, if the sky stays clear. If it snows in the night, we don't go. I take you to the cave and I bring you down. Nowhere else."

"And if I am not ready to come down?"

"You'll be ready when the pine's gone. Can you sit a mule?"

"I have never tried."

"You'll know tomorrow. The road over to Boeotia's under snow. All of it."

"I am not going over."

He was tying the last mule's lead into the tail of the one in front.

That night I did not sleep much. I lay under Kallia's blanket in the storeroom with the duffel beside me, packed, which was no trouble, since it had never been unpacked. Some time after midnight I got up and did the morning. I cleared the ashes by touch and swept the hearth, made the trip to the lions twice in the dark, and topped up the big jar so that nobody would be short when the house woke. I folded the blanket in four on the mat, hoisted the duffel, and took up the little shovel from the pail.

Kallia's voice reached me from the dark at the top of the outside stair.

"The shovel comes back."

I stood in the court with it. My Greek did not run to what I needed to say to her, so I thanked her for the blanket in English.

"Shut the gate behind you."

Valens was waiting at the turn of the lane with two mules, the gray and a dun, with nothing on their saddles but fodder sacks and a coil of rope and a bundle of pine splinters bound with withy. He took the duffel from me and weighed it on one hand.

"Everything," he said. It was not a question. He lashed it onto the dun. "Does the widow know you're going?"

"She knows the shovel is going."

We went through the sleeping town and out past the high corner of the sanctuary wall, where the dogs knew him and kept quiet, and onto a path that went at the cliff in short zigzags, cut steps and worn rock and loose stone. It was too dark to see the drop. I heard it in the way the mules' breathing came back off the rock. At the first turn he put the gray's tail into my hand.

"Hold that. She'll pull."

I climbed the cliff above Delphi in the dark being towed by a mule, and the mule thought nothing of the arrangement, and by the third turn neither did I. My nose ran and I used my sleeve and it ran again.

I talked. I had not meant to. The drop was on my left from the first turn to the last, and the tail was coarse and warm in my fist, and he could not understand one word, so I talked to his back, low.

"My name is Mara Reyes. I'm thirty-three. I mend broken things in a basement for money, and I'm good at it, which nobody here is ever going to find out. I have a cousin who keeps her husband's keys in the freezer door. I've got an apartment with a radiator that knocks all night in February, and the rent is due on the first, and I'm three months behind by now, I suppose, unless they've given it to somebody else. That watch in your pouch was my father's, and he came home when he said he would maybe twice in my whole life, and I've wound it every morning since January, which hasn't helped him or me. I have no idea what happens if it's shut. I've lain in the widow's storeroom every night for a month working it out, and the furthest I've got is the water. I'm getting better at the water. If that cave is open today I'll walk down to the hotel on the main street in Delphi and stand under a hot shower until it runs cold, and then the first bus out and the first plane to anywhere, and call Gloria collect from Kennedy and let her yell at me, and then the train home to Philadelphia, and my own kitchen, and never—"

"Philadelphia," Valens said.

I stopped talking. The gray stopped too and stood with her haunches against my knees.

"There's a Philadelphia in Asia," he said. "Past Sardis. A man I knew came from there."

"Farther," I said.

"Past Asia there's only Parthians."

"Past them."

He waited a little for the rest of it. When nothing came he clicked his tongue at the gray, and we went on up.

Two turns up, the ice began. He halted, got sacking out of a fodder bag and bound each of the mules' hooves in it, crouched in the path with the hoof between his knees, pulling the twine tight with his teeth. Ray put the snow tires on the Buick every Thanksgiving weekend whether it had snowed or not, and took them off on Easter Monday whether it had stopped or not, and drove on them all winter on the theory that the tires knew something.

"Step where she steps," Valens said. "Not where I step."

"Why not where you step?"

"She looks where she puts her feet."

Daylight arrived while we were on the last of the cliff, gray along the valley, and showed me the whole fall of the mountain below us, terraces and roofs and the sanctuary laid out down its slope like a model under glass, with the temple in the middle of it, very small. From up there the paint did not show. For the space of a breath it was nearly the gray one on the postcards; then the sun cleared the ridge and found the gilt on the roof.

"They paint it again when the snow's gone." Valens had stopped to let the mules blow. "The priests hang a man off the roof on a rope, with a pot of red."

"Who paints it?"

"The same man every year. He came off the corner at the last painting and broke his arm. They had him back up there in a month with the arm tied to his side."

"Is he any good?"

"He's cheap."

On a bend near the top he stopped and stood studying the path we had climbed. Far below us a man with a donkey was coming up the zigzags with a load on its back.

"He's been on the path since the town," Valens said.

"He has a donkey."

Valens watched him through two turns of the path, and then three. The mules shifted. I stood holding her tail with my fingers going dead, and the man below came up to a fork and turned off toward a thread of smoke on the slope, where somebody had a kiln going, and Valens went on up without a word about it.

"Who did you think it was?" I said.

"Somebody for you."

"I told you. There is no one."

"You told me."

Where the cliffs ended there was the plain, under snow, a hand deep where the wind had left it alone and up to the knee where it had drifted along the walls. The huts I had seen from above that first evening stood about in it with their brush roofs weighed down and not one of them smoking. The crust took my weight for a step and then let me through.

"Get up," he said, and put me on the gray, and walked at her head, reading the ground under the snow by the lie of the walls and the tops of the thornbushes. "Sit back. Hold the saddle, not her mouth. Let her find it."

I sat back. The gray found it.

"What's her name?"

"She hasn't got one."

"What do you call her?"

"The gray."

"And the one who ate Kallia's tree?"

"The brown." He kicked the snow off a stone with the side of his boot and looked at what was under it. "A name's for calling. They come when they see the barley bag."

"Why do you talk to them in Latin?"

"They came from Corinth. They don't know any Greek."

"Mules do not know any language."

"These know Latin."

"And you? Where are you from?"

"North."

We went on a while with nothing but the creak of the packsaddle and the crust breaking under him.

"What's in it that goes?" he said.

It took me a little while to understand that he meant the watch. Then I tried to tell him, in a Greek that had no words for any of it.

"Inside there is a strip of metal. Very thin. Rolled up on itself, round and round. When you turn the crown it rolls tighter."

"And when it's tight?"

"It wants to open again, the way a brooch pin wants to spring. There is a little wheel that goes back and forth, so, and lets it open by one tooth, and then the next, and no faster. Those teeth turn the hands."

I did the wheel for him with my hand flat on the gray's neck, rocking it.

"Who makes them?"

"Men in a country you have never heard of. Thousands of them."

"For hire?"

"For sale. Anyone can buy one."

"Anyone." He tried the crust ahead with his heel before he trusted it. "And the little wheel. What stops it rocking?"

"The spring running down. Dirt. Cold. Being dropped."

"I won't drop it."

By the pen under the firs I knew where I was. Its stones still stood upright on their edges, each propped on the next all round, with snow lying along the slant of every one. Somebody had tied the hurdle shut with a fresh withy since October.

"Snow by afternoon." Valens was looking at the sky over the summits, where a long bank of cloud was standing up behind the ridge.

The cave had not moved. Its arch was half full of snow, blown in under the lip and shaped there into a long smooth wave that ran back into the dark. In the dressed rock by the arch the letters were still red at the bottom of their strokes, and snow had settled in the strokes, white in red. The ledge below held only snow.

"What does it say?" he said.

"To the Nymphs. To Pan."

"That's what the goat boys say it says."

He tied the mules in the lee of the rock, broke a corner off a crust from his bag, and left it on the ledge.

"It's his house," he said.

"Pan's? You believe in him?"

"I pay him."

"That is not an answer."

"It's what I do." He was already crouched in the mouth with his back to the wind. "How long will you be in there?"

"Until it opens."

"Until what opens?"

I left that where it was.

He made fire with a steel and a flint and a lump of dried bracket fungus, striking until a spark caught and he could blow it red. It was slow work.

"Can you not go faster?"

"No."

When the first pine caught he stood up and came in behind me, and I took the slope of the floor and crossed the hall without waiting for him, by the great pale lump of stone with its shoulder shining, and climbed the flowstone, and the torchlight followed.

The slab was there. The crack was there. I stood the bag by the slab and worked my hand in between the two faces of rock and kept it there, palm to the black.

Rock. Air. Cold that was only cold.

I went down on my knees in the bottom of the crack, and began to dig.

"What's in there?" he said.

"Nothing yet."

* * *

The last pine but one was burning when I gave up digging. Nothing was left at the bend to dig; I had it down to rock on every side. I pushed my fingers into the gap, flat, as deep as they would reach.

"Please," I said to the rock, in English. "I did the water this morning. I did the floors every day for a month. I did everything anybody asked me. Please."

The rock was cold. The air did not move.

"The hour," I said.

He showed me. Five to two.

I looked at it until his fingers closed over it. My fingers came free of the gap and I stood up. My knees had stopped feeling the rock some time before, and when I stood they gave a little and then held. I set my hand on the slab. Cold. I hit it. I hit it with the side of my fist and then with the heel of my hand, and the skin came off the heel, and I hit it again.

The noise I made had no word in it.

It went down the hall and struck the far wall and came back, and the cave went on giving it back after I had stopped, smaller and farther, from places I could not see.

After it, the drip, and the torch hissing.

Valens laughed.

It was short and surprised, and ran on briefly after. It was not a large laugh. Nothing in it was aimed at me. I had been laughed at on the Sacred Way, in front of the whole square, and I knew the difference even in the dark with my hand bleeding.

"Good," he said.

He dug into the front of his tunic and came up with a round of flat bread, broke it in two across his knee, and gave me half.

It was warm. He had carried it the whole climb inside his clothes, against his skin, and it was exactly the temperature of a person.

I ate it. It was barley, mostly, coarse, and whoever made it had salted it with conviction, and the crust had a scorch across one side where it had sat too near somebody's coals. I ate it standing in the bottom of the crack with my bleeding hand held away from it, and he ate his half watching the torch.

"Why did you laugh?" I said.

He finished what was in his mouth first.

"All day you were careful with it," he said. "With the mountain. With the hole. You put your hand in slow and kept it still. Then you hit it."

"And that was funny."

"It was the first thing you did up here I understood."

"And before? What did you think I was doing with the shovel?"

"Digging up something you'd hidden."

"Hidden what?"

"Silver. Whatever you ran with. People who run bury it, and come back for it later."

"Had I any silver, you would have had silver."

"You'd have kept the silver," he said, "and paid me in the watch."

"I am not a thief."

"I didn't say thief." He brushed the crumbs off his knee into his palm and ate them. "I was at the turn of the lane before you came out to me. I heard you at the spring, twice, in the dark. A thief doesn't fill the widow's jar before she goes."

I had no answer to that, in either language. When the bread was gone I could have eaten the same again, which after a month of Kallia's lentils was nothing new.

"Eat the crumbs," he said. "You'll want them on the stair."

He lit the last pine from the stub of the old one and held it while I got into the duffel's strap. I left the shovel lying in the crack. He went back for it.

At the mouth the snow had come. It was falling through the arch in the gray light, big wet flakes, so thick that everything below the lip was gone, and the tracks we had made coming up were already going soft at the edges. The mules stood in the lee of the rock with snow on their backs. The crust on the ledge wore a cap of it.

He put me on the gray and lashed the bag onto the dun and went at her head, and we went down into the white.

On the plain there was only snow and wind and the gray's ears ahead of me, the flakes settling between them. The huts were gone. We were under the firs before I saw them. The light went off the snow very slowly, like milk being poured off cream.

"What were you digging for?" he said, somewhere on the plain.

"Cold. I was looking for cold. A wind, out of the rock."

The gray went a dozen steps before he answered. "It's cold all over this mountain."

"I know that."

"Wind all over it."

"I know."

The gray dropped her head, blew, and shook the snow off her ears all over me.

"Who were you meeting up there?" he said.

"No one. It was not a who."

We came in among the firs, where the wind couldn't reach, and the snow fell straight down between the trunks, very quiet.

"Where do you sleep?" he said.

"Under the widow's stairs. With her jars."

"Dry?"

"Dry."

"Does she hit you?"

"No."

"She feeds you."

"Lentils."

"Stay where you are. She'll keep you while you work."

"Why did you bring me up," I said, "if you thought somebody was coming after me?"

"The watch."

"Only that?"

"It's a good watch."

"Sell it back to me. When I have found silver. Real silver."

"You paid it to go up. You went up."

"It was my father's."

"You said he didn't need it."

I had said that. The gray picked her way down between the trunks with me on her back, and I held on to the front of the packsaddle with both hands.

"Then wind it," I said. "Every morning."

"Forward. Till it stops, and not past."

At the top of the cliff path there was a red glow off to the left, low in the snow, and a smell of smoke that was sour where woodsmoke would have been sweet. The man with the donkey had his kiln there, a long hump of earth and turf with smoke seeping from every seam, and he sat beside it on an upturned basket with a sack across his head and shoulders. The donkey stood in its lee with its eyes shut.

"Mule-man!" he called. "Come and warm yourselves. She won't mind." He meant the kiln.

Valens would have gone by. I watched him decide it. But the gray had already turned for the warmth, and I was on the gray.

"Nine days she's been at it," the man said, "and now this." A flake hissed where it landed on the turf. He got up and stamped a seam shut where a tongue of flame had shown in it. "Easy, girl. Hold it in." He raked a few chestnuts out of the edge of the embers with a stick and pushed them across the snow to me with his foot. "Eat. They're split already."

They burned my fingers, and I ate them anyway. He talked fast, in the mountain Greek, and I lost half his words and none of Valens's answers.

"Did you get any of the god's ox?" The rest ran on past me. I caught the number four in it, and the council, and a grievance about meat.

"I was at Kirrha."

"So you ate what we ate. Smoke." He peered up at me in the glow. "That's her. The nymph. My cousin's boy saw her step out of Pan's cave at the vintage."

"She's the widow Kallia's," Valens said. He had crouched at the other mule's forefeet to strip off the wet sacking and bind on fresh. "Down by the spring."

"Since when has Kallia got anybody?"

"Since the vintage."

"In trousers?"

"She came off a ship."

The next part he said slowly, and to me. "The boy says you ate his cheese and paid him in a cake made of hay."

"It was oats," I said.

"It's all hay to a boy." He saw my hand then. "And what's she done to that?"

"She hit Pan," said Valens.

The sack across the man's shoulders shook, and the donkey opened its eyes and shut them again.

"Sleep here by her," he said. "You'll not get down in the dark."

"The widow wants her shovel," Valens said, and stood, and gave me the mule's tail again.

Below us the whole valley had sunk into gray, with a few lamps showing far down in it where the town was. Going down was worse than coming up. The mule did not pull; she sat back on her haunches and let herself down from step to step, and twice my boots went out from under me on the ice and her tail held me up. Valens went in front with the second mule, sideways to the slope, talking to both of them in Latin, low, about nothing I could hear.

"He'll have you all over the town by the next market," he said, when the glow of the kiln was out of sight. He kept his back to me. "So I'll tell you what I'd tell them, if they asked me. You came down off the top in the autumn, by yourself, in trousers, so the road wouldn't take you for a woman. Your hands never worked. You read the letters on the rock without spelling them out. You've got Latin. You had a thing on your wrist no smith in Achaea could make, and you won't give a name, yours or your people's. That's a big house. Corinth, or Patrae. Somebody there wants you back, a father or a husband, and whoever he is, he can pay men to ask. When they come up this road, they'll ask who took you up there."

It was the most he had said all day, and he had said it in order, like a man giving evidence, and most of it was true, and the whole of it was wrong.

"It is a good story," I said. "It is a better story than mine. If I told you where I came from—"

"Don't."

"You would think me mad."

"Don't tell me where. I don't want it in my head when they come."

"You have not asked my name either."

"No."

"Where did you learn Latin, then?"

"On the road."

"Latin is not learned on this road."

The sanctuary dogs let us by. The lamps of the town were close now, yellow in the doorways, with smoke lying low over the roofs and the smell of somebody's supper in it. At the first houses he stopped the mules in the shadow of a wall and went round the dun, unlashing my bag.

He gave me the duffel. Then he gave me the shovel.

"Men will come asking," he said. "You went up by yourself. You never had a watch."
