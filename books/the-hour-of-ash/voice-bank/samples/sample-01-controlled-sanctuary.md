The temple of Apollo was painted. I had known that the way one knows things from a lecture hall; I had written a seminar paper on ancient polychromy and been proud of its footnotes. None of the footnotes had prepared me for the red. It ran along the cornice in a band the colour of a fresh cut, and above it the pediment figures stood out in blue and ochre, their eyes outlined in black and their hair gilded, so that the late sun caught them and threw itself back down at the pilgrims queuing on the ramp. A man beside me was selling sausages from a tray slung around his neck. The smell of fat and fennel came and went with the wind off the valley.

I stood on the Sacred Way with my feet aching inside my boots, and I thought, with real shame, that it was ugly.

Ugly was the wrong word, and I knew it before I had finished thinking it; loud was nearer. Every few yards along the road stood a base with nothing on it. Somebody had cut the bronzes off at the ankles. I could see the stumps of the lead fixings, still bright where the chisel had gone in, and pale rectangles on the marble where the weather had not had time to grey them. Nero had come through with his carts three summers before. The conservator in me wanted a soft brush and a notebook. The rest of me wanted to sit down on one of those empty plinths and weep, and I did neither, because a temple slave with a rod had begun to take an interest in my trousers.
