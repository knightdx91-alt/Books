He said it to the window, in English, the way you would talk to a dog you had owned a long time.

"Well, Jesus H. Christ."

I had not heard English spoken by anyone but myself in five years. It came out of an old man in a Chaldean's robe with a Philadelphia flatness to the vowels, and my knees went, and I sat down on his floor among the wax tablets and began to laugh and could not stop, and somewhere in the laughing I was crying, and he got down beside me with a great deal of trouble and patted my shoulder very fast, the way men of his generation patted women who were crying, as if they might go off.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Hey. You're okay. What year?"

"Ninety-eight."

"Nineteen ninety-eight." He sat back on his heels. "Holy cow. Is Nixon dead? No, don't tell me, I don't care, tell me about the Phillies. No. Don't tell me that either." He was turning a battered Zippo over and over in his ink-stained fingers. It was empty; I could smell that it had been filled with lamp oil at some point and had not liked it. "You came through the cave. The Corycian. You had my notebook. So somebody finally sold the house."

It was not a question, so I did not answer it.

"It'll take you back," he said. "That's the one thing I'd bet the farm on. Late summer, seventy-nine. I've been sitting on that number since the quake."
