The bed in the shepherd's hut was a frame of olive wood strung with rope, and it had been built for one small shepherd who did not move in his sleep. When Valens sat down on it, it said so. When I sat down beside him it said so again, louder, and tipped us together at the hip, and I laughed into his shoulder so hard I had to hold on to his tunic.

"It will hold," he said.

"It will not."

"I've slept on worse."

"With company?"

He did not answer that, which was an answer, and I stopped laughing. He undid the pin at my shoulder with the same patience he used on a knotted lead rope, and the wool slid down, and the cold air of the high pasture came in under the door and found my skin before he did. His hands were rough across the palm and very careful. He touched me as though he had been told the price of every part of me and meant to pay it.

I had been waiting for my own mind to start talking, the way it did about everything, and it did not. There was the rope creaking, and the smell of sheep and woodsmoke in the blanket, and his breath going ragged against my neck when I pulled him down; and there was the moment the bed gave up entirely on one side and we went with it, and neither of us laughed that time, and neither of us stopped.
