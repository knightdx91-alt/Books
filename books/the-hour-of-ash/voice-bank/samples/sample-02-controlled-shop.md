Irene Szabo had the face of a good leather handbag, creased in all the places that had been useful, and she unrolled the chart on the glass counter without ceremony, weighting the corners with a stapler, a brass bell and her own reading glasses. Sleet ticked against the shop window. Somewhere in the back a radiator knocked twice and gave up.

The paper was laid, not wove; I could see the chain lines when she tilted it toward the lamp, faint ladders running through the sheet. The coastline had been engraved and then washed by hand in a thin green that had gone the color of pond water at the folds. Someone had come along much later with a fountain pen and blue-black ink and written across the mountains above the Gulf of Corinth, in small capitals that sloped slightly uphill, a string of numbers and the word BIBLIOTHECA with a question mark after it. Beside the question mark was a little drawing of a doorway. It had been done carefully, with a ruler.

"Venetian," Mrs. Szabo said. "Sixteen-eighties. The coast is right. That's why I bought it." She looked at me over the top of the glasses she was not wearing. "You are not a dealer."

"No."

"Then you will pay what I ask," she said, and named a figure that would have been an insult to the map if it had been fake, and was a robbery if it was not.
