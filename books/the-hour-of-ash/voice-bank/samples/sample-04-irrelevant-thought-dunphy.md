The clerk wrote me down on a wax tablet with a bronze stylus whose tip had been sharpened so many times it was nearly gone. He did not look at me while he did it. He asked Kallia my name, and Kallia told him, and he asked Kallia where I was from, and Kallia said Syracuse in a voice that dared him to listen to my accent and say otherwise. He did not dare. He scratched it in, a woman of Kallia's household, and smoothed a word he had got wrong with the flat end of the stylus.

I thought of Mrs. Dunphy.

She had taught us for three weeks in the fourth grade while our real teacher was having her gallbladder out, and one afternoon in the middle of long division she had sat down at the desk and begun to cry without making any sound. She wore a green cardigan with one button missing. Thirty children bent over our workbooks and carried the remainders and did not look up, because we knew, the way children know such things, that looking would make it real. I never found out why she cried. She was gone the next Monday. I had not thought of her in years, and there was no reason on earth to think of her now, in a magistrate's office in Phocis with flies walking on the shutters.

"Done," the clerk said, to Kallia.
