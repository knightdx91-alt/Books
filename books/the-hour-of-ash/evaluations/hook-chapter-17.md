## Opening (Hook)

> The boards left their dust on my fingers. Fine and gray, faintly greasy, the dust of old wax in a shut room; under it the wood of
> the tag had been worn smooth by thumbs. […]
> Each tag carried two names cut short, and a numeral. In a colony two names made a year.
>
> Behind me the clerk was eating figs.

- **Type: IN MEDIAS RES (quiet, touch-grounded).** The search is already under way when the reader arrives, and the reader works
  out where they are from what they are given: boards, tags, consular years, a clerk at her back. The how-we-got-here comes later
  in pieces, as the ledger asks. The first sentence is the sensory anchor the type requires: dust on the fingers, 8 words, in the
  record office. That meets the ledger's `short-punch`.
- **Why not SENSORY IMMERSION.** The paragraph is tactile, but the hook is not the texture. It is the act of searching, set against
  the man behind her. The "wrong detail" in the field is not in the description. It is the clerk, and he gets his own paragraph.
  A reader holding Ch.16's plan knows what these hands are doing in this room. One clean type.
- **Why not QUESTION (barred).** Nothing in the first five sentences poses a gap the reader cannot square. "In a colony two names
  made a year" is an answer, Mara's register working, and the button is an action.
- **Score: 8/10.**
  - Information gap 2/3: which year, which book, why "we had wanted the other" clerk.
  - Emotional pull 2/3: the tension is carried by the reader, drawn from Ch.16's plan, and the fig-eater at her back turns the dust
    into a held breath. No emotion is named.
  - Voice 2/2: Mara's object-reader's attention (the worn tag, two names making a year), kept to one recognition as the writer
    warning requires. Credential asides 0.
  - Connection 2/2: there is no recap of Ch.16's question or the plan. The plan is simply happening.
- **Rewritten:** no.
- **Alternatives considered (not applied):**
  - CONTRAST: move `Behind me the clerk was eating figs.` (7w) to the top (est. 8, no gain). With no theft yet on the page, the
    contrast is not legible, the ledger's dust and wax are lost, and the anchor's button is spent in the first line.
  - IN MEDIAS RES, louder: open on the ladder (`The ladder creaked. He was coming down.`) (est. 7). That spends a later beat, and
    the line lies on the wrong side of the leaf's discovery.
  - TICKING CLOCK (still unused book-wide): a noon or the-boys-race marker as the punch (est. 6). The urgency would be manufactured
    in the first line, since the clock only becomes real when Celer's boy arrives. It breaks the ledger's wax-and-dust opening and
    Mara's register. Not forced.

## Ending (Pull)

> "Will they wait?" I said.
>
> "No."
>
> A dog burst from a garden gate and ran with us for a stretch, delighted, and dropped off. The wind blew harder into our faces, and
> the gardens went by on both sides, beans and cabbages and a man with a hoe who straightened to watch us pass. The smudge at the
> end of the road was masts now, each one separate, and hulls under them, and the long arm of the mole. At the harbor, beyond the
> last of the gardens, a sail was going up.

- **Type: CLIFFHANGER (action unfinished, quiet signature).** The chapter stops mid-run, before anyone reaches the quay. The stakes
  were set as story logic, not by the narrator: "It goes out on the afternoon breeze, whoever's on it." / "That's the breeze." /
  "Will they wait?" / "No." So the sail going up is a clock the reader can read for themselves. It is a 13-word close with no
  dialogue in the last paragraph, which is the ledger's `quiet`.
- **The energy compresses rather than winding down (anti-pattern 10 checked).** The paragraph narrows sentence by sentence: the
  dog is incidental, the wind is pressure, the masts come apart into separate masts and hulls, and then one sail moves. The last
  sentence is the shortest in the paragraph. The image is concrete, with no feeling named and no aphorism.
- **Honesty check.** Whose sail it is stays open on purpose. Ch.18's outline resolves the run substantially (the captain's
  condition, the ten-word wedding, the mule at the gangplank), not in one line. See the Ripple note for Ch.18.
- **No foreshadowing.** The sail is seen now, in past tense, at the moment of seeing. "No." is a character's answer about the
  present. style_check reports foreshadow 0.
- **Score: 8/10.**
  - Forward pull 3/3: the leaf is on his chest, the breeze has come, and a sail is going up. The Midnight Test passes cleanly.
  - Emotional charge 2/3: the charge comes through the body (the camera hammering her ribs, the breath going raw, his hand spread
    over the leaf) more than through Mara's inner life at the line itself.
  - Unpredictability 1/2: the race was set up by "the afternoon breeze", so the sail is the expected threat. The run itself is
    what the reader did not see coming after the latrine.
  - Promise keeping 2/2: the chapter pays the leaf, Celer in person and the escape by boredom, and opens the ship.
- **Rewritten:** no.
- **Alternatives considered (not applied):**
  - End on `We ran.` (est. 7). It stops before the clock the breeze set, and the run never becomes "unfinished".
  - End on `"Will they wait?" I said. / "No."` (est. 7). It turns the signature into `dialogue` and breaks the ledger's `quiet`.
    It also makes Valens's one bare "No." the scene button, a retired reflex.
  - DECISION POINT, ending on the pine crown (`"He'll ask who won."`) (est. 5). It settles into tenderness and lets the reader
    turn off the light.
