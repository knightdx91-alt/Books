## Opening (Hook)

> "Have you seen his back?" Until then she had talked in Greek, a Greek so finely made that I lost it a clause at a time: […]
> The question was short and plain, and all of it reached me.
>
> "Yes," I said.
>
> I had had my palms on the ridges of it most nights since July. The heat went up my neck into my ears, and she watched it go.
>
> "Good," said Plautia.

- **Type: QUESTION.** This is the outline's "Plautia's first blunt question", spoken in the first five words. The reader's brain
  stalls on it: a woman the reader has met only as "his sister", "She'll be in it", asks the new wife about the man's bare
  body. Either she has seen it, which is the jealousy Ch.15 left open, or she knows what is on it. Then "Good," closes the
  exchange on a verdict the reader cannot read yet.
- **Why not IN MEDIAS RES.** It arrives mid-conversation, but no action is under way, and the line works as a gap rather than
  as momentum. One clean type.
- **Answered in the chapter (honesty check) ✓.**
  - "Have you?" gets a refusal that neither denies nor admits ("Anything I have seen is a matter for the court").
  - The real answer comes by watching, as the outline asks: the porch at C3, where she waits for him to search the street and
    turns her head "to find him".
  - "What he had chosen … when at last he chose anything" pays the "Good," without anyone stating it.
- **The held second sentence is the chapter's comprehension rule in action.** The Greek is lost "a clause at a time", against
  the short plain question that "all of it reached me". It slows the hook on purpose and earns the turn into Latin. It is
  not throat-clearing, and cutting it would lose the rule the closing paragraph pays.
- **Score: 9/10.**
  - Information gap 3/3: whose back, why ask, why "Good".
  - Emotional pull 2/3: the blush "she watched it go" is physical, and her stillness makes it a contest. The emotion is
    mostly the reader's suspicion rather than Mara's.
  - Voice 2/2: Mara's linguist's ear ("found the verb and lost hold of its subject") and her plain "Yes".
  - Connection 2/2:
    - Ch.15's heat answers "most nights since July", and Ch.15's "She'll be in it." is the jealousy this opens on.
    - The ridges reach back to the scar under her palm in Ch.12.
    - There is no recap of the crossing or the arrival. The Parallel structure supplies those later (D1, D3).
- **Rewritten:** no.

  - CONTRAST, opening on `"My son was seven at the vintage."` set against the empty hooks (est. 7). That spends the son too

## Ending (Pull)

> Plautia waited until the court was still. She left the cup where it stood, and when she spoke again it was in a Latin plain
> enough for the stableboy, with each word laid down on its own. She asked it of me, and not of the coals: when he had his
> leaf, and his ship, and me, what was there left in Rome that would bring him to stand up before a court and tell it the truth
> about her?

- **Type: QUESTION PLANT.** It meets the ledger's question close and the writer warning on all three counts.
  - **Real.** The suit cannot be won without his testimony: the bargain in Ch.15 and the summons in Ch.21 ("Valens is the
    witness"). The outline gives Valens no stake in Rome once he has his leaf.
  - **Unanswered.** Mara has no line after it, and the chapter stops on the question mark.
  - **Not rhetorical.**
    - It is aimed at one person, "of me, and not of the coals".
    - It is specific: the leaf, the ship, "and me".
    - It comes straight after Valens's "I said I'd stand up. I'll stand up.", which it refuses to take at face value.
- **What makes it more than a plot doubt.** The subtext layer ("each is asking the other whether Valens loved her") reaches
  its last turn here. Plautia lists Mara among the things he will have and asks the wife to vouch for him. Mara knows, as the
  reader does, that her own aim is a door in Campania. Plautia cannot know that, so the question cuts deeper than she means.
- **Why reported speech works.** "a Latin plain enough for the stableboy, with each word laid down on its own" is Plautia
  making sure that this time Mara cannot lose any clause of it. That pays off the opening's Greek lost "a clause at a time".
  Direct speech would gain volume and lose that point.
- **Why it is not EMOTIONAL GUT PUNCH (barred).** The pull is a demand put to Mara, not an image that lands a feeling. No
  detail is held for the reader to ache over.
- **Bookend check (anti-pattern 5).** The chapter opens and closes on a blunt question from Plautia to Mara. The pairing
  carries meaning. The first question asks what is between his body and her (jealousy). The last asks what holds him to her
  cause (loyalty), and it comes after the friendship turn. It moves the relationship forward rather than returning to the
  start, so it stays.
- **Midnight Test: pass.** The next chapter is the theft the reader has just watched being planned. This question adds a
  second reason to read on: the reader now distrusts the promise the plan depends on.
- **No foreshadowing.** The question is a character's, asked now, about a future she cannot see. The narrator does not reach
  forward. style_check reports foreshadow 0.
- **Score: 8/10.**
  - Forward pull 2/3: a slow burn, paid in Ch.21–22. The Isthmia plan carries the immediate drive.
  - Emotional charge 2/3: her son is behind every word, and Mara is made the guarantor.
  - Unpredictability 2/2: "I'll stand up." reads as settled, and this reopens it.
  - Promise keeping 2/2: the chapter's three non-negotiables are delivered (the Isthmia plan, Kallia closed, the bracelet), and
    one new debt is opened.
- **Rewritten:** no.
- **Alternatives considered (not applied):**
  - The same QUESTION PLANT in direct speech (`"When he has his leaf, and his ship, and you," … "the truth about me?"`) (est. 8,
    no gain). It loses the plain-Latin payoff, and it risks the closing signature computing as `dialogue`.
  - DECISION POINT, ending on `"I said I'd stand up," said Valens. "I'll stand up."` (est. 6). It settles the thread and fails
    the Midnight Test.
  - CLIFFHANGER, ending on Felix's shout ("She's in the cook's cabbages") (est. 5). The next line resolves it, so it is a
    dishonest pull.
