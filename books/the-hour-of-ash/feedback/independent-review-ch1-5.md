# Independent read — Chapters 1–5 (2026-10-19)

A full read of the finalized text, separate from the pipeline's evaluator, at the author's request:
length, content, and whether the prose leans on metaphor ("the water was as still as glass").

## Verdict

The prose is plain, concrete and specific. It does not lean on metaphor, and the stock phrases of
machine-made fiction are absent. The real risk in these five chapters is pace for a romance reader,
plus two habits of rhythm and voice that no gate counts yet.

The gate allows 4 per 1,000 words; Outlander itself runs higher. Several of the counted hits are
literal measurements ("as high as my knee", "as broad as a coin"), so the true figure is lower.

Every simile is concrete and comes from Mara's own world: a coastline perforated "like the edge of a
stamp", mountains drawn "like a sheep seen from behind", a bag carried "like a tray of drinks", a dog
barking "like a car alarm on Baltimore Avenue at three in the morning", an unripe olive "like biting chalk
soaked in aspirin". None explains a feeling.

A scan for stock purple phrases ("still as glass", "shimmered", "washed over", "a wave of", "silence
hung", "breath caught", "tapestry", "bathed in", "the weight of") found **zero** hits. The implied
metaphors are rare and earned: "the coast that had cost a navy", the mouse and the watch "going about
their two small businesses in the dark".

## Content: strong

- **The scenes that matter land.** The father's voice on the answering machine and "Which one stops
  it?"; Szabo's haggle; the cold in the crack and the embarrassment instead of awe; a child spitting out
  a granola bar and naming her "Nymphē"; the painted temple she finds ugly; the looted statue bases;
  Kallia inspecting her teeth.
- **It is funny.** Szabo, the German with his daughter's windowsill of stones, the guide's rival, the
  four oxen.
- **The research holds up.** Spot-checked: Saturday 14 March 1998, Monday 20 April 1998 and Tuesday
  19 May 1998 fall on the stated weekdays; "MMMBop" was the summer before; the 1990s 100-drachma coin
  shows Alexander; Boathoos is the right Delphic month for autumn; about 23,000 knucklebones came out
  of the Corycian Cave; Nero took about 500 bronzes from Delphi and died in Phaon's villa; Titus took
  Jerusalem in August–September AD 70. The herald being corrected on the emperor's title is a sharp
  touch.
- **No foreshadowing anywhere.** The year is worked out on her fingers in the present.

## Problems, in order of importance

1. **Pace, for a romance.** These five chapters are 27,400 words, about 11% of the planned book, and
   the love interest has not appeared; Valens arrives in Ch.6 at about 13%. Three of the five chapters
   (1, 3, 5) are long, slow and mostly solitary. Ch.3 is 6,800 words of one woman climbing a mountain,
   with 6% dialogue. It is beautifully done, but it asks a romance reader for a great deal of patience
   before anything happens. Ch.3 could lose 1,200–1,500 words without losing anything the plot needs:
   the long camera swap with the German, the graffiti-wall paragraph, the paragraph on the French
   excavation report, and the toilet beat are the candidates. Ch.5's monument roll-call on the Sacred
   Way is the other sag.

2. **A rhythm habit no gate counts: long "and … and … and" sentences.** Sentences with four or more
   "and"s: Ch.1 has 3, Ch.2 3, Ch.3 **23**, Ch.4 0, Ch.5 **17**. One of them is designed: the
   17-"and" sentence at the crossing is the held breath, and it works. Elsewhere this has become the
   narrator's default rhythm for any physical action, which is the same chain-sentence problem the Ch.1
   evaluator capped at 8 for that chapter. Nothing has capped it since.

3. **A voice reflex: Mara filters everything through her credentials.** References to the lab, the
   grammars, the seminar or graduate school run 3 / 7 / 7 / 2 / **10** across the chapters ("I had read
   it in Josephus in a seminar", "I had looked at polychromy through a stereo microscope since graduate
   school"). It is her character and some of it is the best material in the book. But by Ch.5 nearly
   every new sight comes with a credential attached, which slows the scene and is starting to read as a
   device.

4. **A comprehension jump.** On the mountain she cannot parse a nine-year-old (Ch.4). The next
   afternoon she follows a guide's patter and two men's banter at the fountain (Ch.5). It is defensible:
   a rehearsed tour speech and town Greek are nearer her book Greek than a shepherd boy's dialect. The
   page should say so once, in a clause.

5. **The dry paragraph button.** Short wry lines close a lot of paragraphs ("Badly.", "The Mummers had
   got at it.", "Kallia, then.", "and I was annoyed about that too."). Ch.1 alone has ten. They are
   charming. They are also the shape most likely to become the book's punchline reflex across 44
   chapters.

## Length

The plan is 260,300 words across 44 chapters; the author's floor is 220,000 and the target 245,000.
That is Outlander scale by design. On this evidence the book will be long because its chapters linger,
not because it has more story. The recommendation is to keep the total but tighten the solitary
stretches, so the length goes to Greece and Pompeii, where the romance lives.

## Suggested actions (author's call; nothing has been changed)

- Trim Ch.3 by 1,200–1,500 words and Ch.5's monument roll-call by about 400.
- Add two counts to the per-chapter gates: and-chain sentences (at most 8 per chapter, plus one
  designated held sentence) and credential asides (at most 4 per chapter).
- Add one clause in Ch.5 explaining why town Greek is easier for her than the boy's.
- Keep Ch.6–8 moving. Valens's arrival is the book's first romance beat, and the chapters before it
  have spent their patience.
