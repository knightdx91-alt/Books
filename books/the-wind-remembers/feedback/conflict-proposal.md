# Conflict proposal — The Wind Remembers

**Status: PROPOSED, awaiting author sign-off.** Nothing here is canon yet. The
architect must not build an outline against this until the author approves it.
The author's own notes (`research/source-notes.md`) remain the only binding canon.

**Settled:** this is a **standalone**.

---

## The problem with the severer as first sketched

The author's side note asks: "possibly someone that can sever the connection with
the wheel? so you have Balance?" As a straight antagonist, three things go wrong:

1. **The threat lands on a person the reader hasn't met.** Severance endangers
   Mandy's *future* lives. She doesn't know she has past ones for most of the
   book, and the reader is bonded to *this* Mandy. The threat can't hit
   emotionally until very late — exactly when the engine needs to be loudest.
2. **It's an external plot bolted onto an internal book.** The notes build a
   *remembering* engine: a woman recovering who she was, and a man who has
   watched her since her first breath and cannot tell her. That is intimacy and
   secrecy. A severer is a chase. The book would alternate between them.
3. **It makes the wheel good by default.** Put a villain against the wheel and
   the wheel becomes the order the heroes defend. But the notes' wheel
   *conscripted* Elizabeth for being exceptional, *assigned* a three-year-old to
   a stranger with no name and no face and no say, and makes souls who died
   violently re-live the moment of their death every cycle. That is a morally
   ambiguous institution, and a severing villain flattens it.

## The proposal

Keep the severer. Change what he offers. **He is not a threat — he is a mercy.**

Some old souls are tired. A soul on its fortieth life that burned to death forty
times will burn again. A man who can cut a soul free is offering rest, and he has
a real argument. The person most vulnerable to that offer is **Knight** — who
remembers everything, who has centuries of battlefield behind him, and whose
"implacable will" reads heroic but is also what a person becomes when they cannot
be released.

### The single change that makes it a standalone

**The binder and the severer are the same man. Binding and severing are one
power, run in two directions.**

He put Elizabeth on the wheel — she was exceptional, and he drafted her. Centuries
on, he has come to believe the wheel is cruel, and he spends himself cutting
people loose. He is not a hypocrite; he is a man atoning for what he did to her.
And the soul he most wants to free is the one he conscripted.

This collapses two antagonist-shaped things — whoever put Elizabeth on the wheel,
and whoever can take her off it — into **one person, one power, one crime, one
choice**. A standalone cannot afford two.

### His sin

His goal is mercy. **His sin is that he does not ask.** He severs people who never
chose it.

Which is the wheel's sin exactly. Elizabeth was not asked. Knight was not asked at
three years old. Two systems that both decide for you — and Mandy is the first
person in the story handed a real choice. **The theme is consent.** His final
offer, "I did this to you; let me undo it," is answered with "you don't get to
decide that for me either" — the theme landing as a line of dialogue rather than
a speech.

### What this buys

- **The romance and the metaphysics become one question.** Mandy's ending choice —
  stay on the wheel and keep coming back and keep finding him, or take the rest —
  *is* the love story. Same scene, same decision.
- **Knight's gift becomes the climax mechanism.** Once he commits, nothing on
  earth stops him while he still breathes. That is not a superpower, it is a
  loaded gun, and the book is about which way it points. His defining trait and
  the central choice are the same thing.
- **"Balance" means something.** Souls must be able to leave, or the wheel is a
  prison; if leaving becomes easy, whatever the wheel is for collapses. The climax
  restores *the ability to choose*, rather than winning a fight. Mandy — the newest
  soul, the one not yet hurt — argues for staying; Knight argues for leaving; they
  trade positions by the end.
- **It answers the author's other two side-note questions** without cosmology:
  souls are reborn because someone decided they should be, which points the answer
  back at Ch.1.

## Standalone disciplines (the three things that keep this one book)

1. **Never stage the wheel's administration.** No council, no clerks, no
   hierarchy, no audience with management. The wheel is weather, not a government.
   The moment an institution has officers, it has a sequel.
2. **Answer "why me," not "why the wheel."** Mandy never learns why the wheel
   exists. She learns why *she* is on it — he put her there — and she chooses.
   Characters get closure; the cosmos does not. This is what lets a standalone
   carry big metaphysics.
3. **Deliver the conscription reveal through the mechanism already in the notes.**
   Memories trickle back through dreams, faster the more she embraces her past.
   So Mandy *remembers* what was done to her. No investigation subplot, no
   archive, no research montage — the mystery resolves inside the romance instead
   of running beside it.

## Two craft calls that follow

**POV / dramatic irony.** Opening on Elizabeth's death means the reader already
knows more than Mandy does. Ch.1 is a promise that this is a dramatic-irony book,
not a whodunit. Lean in: give Knight enough POV that the reader knows what he is
early, and let the tension be *when she finds out and how she takes it* — not
*who is this man*. Trying to run a mystery after that opening wastes the opening.

**Flashback budget.** Present day is the spine. Victorian returns a handful of
times after Ch.1; Knight's medieval life gets fewer and shorter. Giving him equal
flashback real estate is writing two books inside one.

## A possible ending (author's call)

Hands are assigned only for a soul's **first** reincarnation — so Knight's job
ends. The wheel releases him and points him at the next stranger.

**And he stays anyway.** That is what implacable will is for: assignment becomes
choice. The consent theme closes on the man the wheel conscripted at three years
old, finally doing something because he picked it.
