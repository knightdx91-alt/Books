# What the notes establish (canon extracted, not invented)

Everything in this section is a restatement of the pages above — no additions.

## The two leads

**Mandy** — present-day protagonist, twenties. The reincarnation of **Elizabeth**,
a young Victorian-era Englishwoman of high society who could call the wind.
Elizabeth's life is the *first* life of the cycle, not merely an early one.
Mandy does not know she is an old soul; she only knows she is drawn to the
Victorian period — the dresses, the society — without knowing why. Her
distinguishing gift is **calling the wind**.

**Knight** — an old soul out of the **medieval** period, met in Mandy's present.
His distinguishing trait is **implacable will**: once committed to a course of
action, nothing on earth stops him while he still breathes. Long practice has
made recovering his past lives easy and early for him; his battlefield-born
instincts help. He is Mandy's assigned **"hand."**

## The wheel — rules as written

- Souls are placed on the **wheel of reincarnation**. Elizabeth was placed on it
  by something that happened in her Victorian life — the author's working
  theory is that her ability grew to the point of qualifying her as
  *exceptional*, and the wheel judged her *important*.
- The placing event: in **a moment of crisis** Elizabeth calls the wind harder
  than she ever has, and **dies doing it**. The cycle begins after that.
- Each old soul has **one thing that sets them apart** (Mandy: the wind;
  Knight: his will).
- **Unlocking memories** requires *using the gift or talent that put you on the
  wheel in the first place*. Once used, memories **trickle back through dreams**.
  The more a soul **embraces its past**, in whatever form, the faster they come.
- Memory return is **not always pleasant** — a soul who died violently
  re-experiences the moment of its death.
- **"Hands":** a soul that has reached a **point of maturity** (i.e. has been
  reincarnated many times) is *sometimes* assigned a hand — a watcher for a
  **newly** reincarnated soul, to ease the transition. **Only ever for a soul's
  first reincarnation.**
- What an assigned hand is given at the start: **not** a name, **not** a face,
  **not even a gender** — only a **vague sense of where** the person is.
- The wheel tries to keep protector and ward **close in age**. Knight was
  **three years old when Mandy was born**.
- At **the moment Mandy drew her first breath**, something shifted in the world
  for Knight. Even at three he felt **a pull toward the west**, and knew on an
  instinctual level that **he had time**.

## Structure as sketched

1. **Chapter 1 — Victorian England.** Elizabeth: high society, balls, the
   dresses. Her gift. The crisis, the greatest call of her life, her death.
2. **Chapter 2 — time skip to Mandy as a child.** Kids playing outside an old
   run-down abandoned house. Not storming, but the coming-storm feeling in the
   air. Mandy calls the wind **for the first time** — and dismisses it as the
   storm arriving, because the wind-calling game had been played before with
   nothing happening. Sun; they run laughing to Mandy's house out of the now
   sprinkling rain.
3. **Time skip to Mandy in her twenties** — present day. Workplace (hotel, or
   the author's preferred alternative, a **museum**) is where she meets Knight.
   She does not learn what he is until later.
4. **Flashbacks throughout** to both leads' *first* lives — the Victorian one for
   Mandy, Knight's adulthood for him.

## Author's open questions (unanswered in the notes — do not resolve unilaterally)

- Hotel **or** museum curator for Mandy's present-day job? (The author leans
  museum: it showcases the pull toward the period she can't explain.)
- What determines **how often** a soul is reborn?
- **Why** is a soul reborn at all?
- What is the **overarching conflict**? Working idea: **someone who can sever a
  soul's connection to the wheel** — with **Balance** as the thing at stake.
