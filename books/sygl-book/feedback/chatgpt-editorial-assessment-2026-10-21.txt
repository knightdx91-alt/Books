SYGL | FINAL MANUSCRIPT EDITORIAL
ASSESSMENT
Synthesis following 30 chapter-specific editorial reports | Supplied reading copy: 324 pages | Independent
developmental and literary-agent-style critique
Acquisition verdict: Revise and resubmit, not offer representation or acquisition in its current form. The
manuscript has a marketable core—an excluded boy with a mutilated blood sygl discovers and frees the imprisoned
originator of his forbidden magic—and an unusually appealing found-family ensemble. It also has structural, ethical,
and magic-system problems that accumulate into the climax. I would read a substantially revised version; I would not
send this draft to editors as submission-ready.
Central diagnosis: The novel repeatedly introduces meaningful moral and physical danger, then softens or
bypasses the lasting cost. That is especially visible in Balthazaar’s rapid empowerment, the comparatively
comfortable school middle, the protagonists’ repeated killing of helpless or unsuspecting enemies, and the sudden
late revelation of healing and conversion powers. The emotional ending works; the causal and moral journey toward
it needs rebuilding.

1 | The book’s promise, genre and readership
The premise promises three intertwined experiences: forbidden-magic access (the lost hand, the blood sygl,
Amelia’s release), belonging (school, dormitory, friends, romantic attachments), and political reckoning
(persecution of Blood users and the return of an originator the world has suppressed). The strongest version of Sygl
makes these strands force choices against one another. In the current version, belonging is often generously
granted while the political reckoning is deferred until the latter half.
The book reads most naturally as adult/upper-crossover magic-academy progression fantasy with a romantic
ensemble and found-family emphasis. Its approachable style and school setting have YA surface signals, but the
central multi-partner dynamic, sensual attention, executions, coercive conversions and wartime killings make a
straightforward YA pitch difficult. A serial or indie progression-fantasy readership may be more receptive to its
comfort-and-power-growth rhythm than a traditional fantasy list demanding a tightly escalating standalone plot.

2 | Structural diagnosis across the thirty chapters
Act I / discovery and admission (Chs. 1–8): The roof flicker, improvised flying rug, temple discovery and Amelia’s
binding make an inviting opening engine. Balthazaar’s missing hand ties personal harm to the world’s ideology. Yet
restoration and privileged access arrive quickly; the wound’s political meaning needs to outlast the bodily cure. Give
the protagonist a sharper objective and an enduring liability before school life settles into routine.
Act II / academy and attachment (Chs. 9–18): Familiars, practical training, the dorm, Lora and Viridia, and the
changing season supply charm. The chapter-by-chapter experience is readable, but too many sequences close with
relationships intact and danger reset. Chapters 16–17 add consequential encounters; Chapter 18 widens the war. A
leaner middle should link classes and magical experiments to problems that will matter in Londra and the duel,
rather than merely demonstrating improvement.
Act III / institutional rupture (Chs. 19–22): The discriminatory rule, Lora and Viridia’s conversion, Bal’s blood whip
and the army’s losses are the book’s strongest potential structural hinge. These events should be the point of no

3 | Character and relationship arcs
Balthazaar: His early appeal lies in curiosity, patience, practical intelligence and his experience of exclusion. His
later pattern is more alarming: a disabled bandit killed after the fight (Ch. 16), Iro executed while restrained and
cooperating (Ch. 21), a death threat against Sean (Ch. 20), satisfaction in the expected deaths of the poisoned
priests (Ch. 26), and readiness to kill obstructing villagers (Ch. 27). This can be a genuinely compelling
war-corruption arc. At present, the narrative often treats these acts as extensions of devotion or competence. Give
Bal self-recognition, resistance from those he loves, and a consequential decision in which he refuses the easy
violent solution—or knowingly pays for choosing it.
Amelia: The manuscript’s most vivid presence: irreverent, lonely, affectionate, ancient, and capable of terrifying
retaliation. Her confinement gives the political conflict emotional legitimacy, but her overwhelming power can flatten
obstacles. Her demand to imprison Raizen for three thousand years mirrors her trauma and should remain morally
uneasy. The familiar/originator power imbalance and her secrecy about blood memory deserve a reckoning with Bal,
not just a revelation that makes him stronger.
Lora: Her social intelligence, alchemy, sword competence, attention to detail and care for Viridia give her agency.
The lessons in noble conduct, the alchemist detour, and her quiet care after the poisoned-drink ordeal are strong.
Her decision to leave the Waves sygl should involve more sustained conflict with her family and with the values she
was raised to uphold. Let her expertise also lead to an error she must own.
Viridia: The crackling hair, affectionate openness, Hopper, and escape from an arranged marriage are memorable.
Her choice to drink apparently poisoned brandy is one of the book’s clearest acts of courage. Preserve the
breakdown afterward, including her suddenly still hair. She needs an independently articulated future and at least
one meaningful disagreement with Bal or Amelia after this ordeal.
Toma and the priests: Toma’s grief over Titus, Leon and Aleric finally makes the enemy morally legible. Leon’s
unfinished goat story is a valuable reminder that the targets had lives. Bring some of this humanity forward, before
the assassination, so the choice to poison them is not simply a clever problem to solve. Toma’s forced conversion is
an ethical injury even if it ends the war. Chapter 30 cannot dispose of that problem with medical care alone.
Romantic ensemble: The crowded couch, shared gestures and easy domesticity convincingly sell companionship.
The binding/conversion structure also creates asymmetries of rank, magical dependence, devotion and divine
power. Clarify explicit consent, individual boundaries and what each participant wants outside Bal’s happiness. A

4 | Magic system, continuity and fair-play plotting
Create a private rules bible before the next draft. The highest-risk rules are: sygl inheritance and suppression;
conversion and its consent requirements; the difference between familiars and life-bonded elementals;
originator intervention and war arbitration; priesthood and victory conditions; blood volume, healing and
memory transfer; runes and purification; portal/travel limits; and the cost of magical protection. Readers do
not need an appendix, but they need rules introduced before those rules decide a crisis.
Specific pressure points: a three-stone juggling memory is difficult to square with Bal’s childhood missing hand
unless the method is explained (Ch. 17); the purification rune seems to make poisoned brandy harmless at the
instant it is needed (Ch. 25); blood drinking permits extreme recovery and sleepless training immediately before the
duel (Ch. 28); the information that Toma lacks an inheritance gift conflicts with Akora’s presentation unless the
source was deceptive (Ch. 29); and converting Akora apparently converts Toma while breaking their life bond (Ch.
29). Each may be repairable, but the chain must be visible.
Injury continuity also needs an audit. Bal suffers repeated head trauma, disorientation, electrical damage, blood loss
and extensive cuts across Chapters 16–29. If magic accelerates healing, establish what it cannot repair, how quickly
it works, and what it costs. Without durable limits, the novel teaches readers not to worry when he is hurt.

5 | Political worldbuilding and thematic coherence
The amputation of children to suppress a blood sygl is a brutal founding fact. The world should behave as if such an
institution exists: who authorizes it, who performs it, what families believe, which officials benefit, how dissidents
resist, and what changes when Amelia returns. The refugee family on the road to Londra and the injured woman
absent from casualty reports are among the most effective glimpses of the human cost. Expand this method of
showing consequences rather than relying on reports about persecution.
The novel’s governing question could be: Does restoring power to the persecuted make a more just world, or
simply create a new authority capable of coercion? The story already contains the ingredients: forced infant
amputation, the school rule, Bal’s killings, involuntary conversion, and Amelia’s vengeance. Let those events speak
to one another. Avoid presenting the new Blood order as automatically ethical because its protagonists are
affectionate.

6 | Prose, pacing and scene craft
Preserve: sensory specificity (slates, reins, woodsmoke, sweet rolls, hard bread); humor that emerges from
character (Hopper and the lake); embodied emotion (Amelia straightening Bal’s collar, Viridia’s still hair, Lora
touching her wrist); and the striking images of strawberries mingling with blood, lightning vitrifying sand, and the
crowded couch. These are not generic fantasy effects; they give Sygl its own texture.
Revise: explanatory sentences that restate what a gesture already communicates; repeated classroom/travel
logistics with no altered stakes; easy agreement following major decisions; dialogue in which supporting characters
mainly validate Bal; and action passages that omit distances, injuries or the activation steps of a decisive spell. The
prose is sufficiently readable that the largest gains will come from developmental restructuring before a
sentence-level polish.

7 | A practical revision sequence
• Pass 1 — Story spine: Write a one-page account of Bal’s goal, obstacle, irreversible midpoint decision, crisis,
climactic choice and book-one resolution. Seed the Raizen conflict and its deadline earlier; compress academy
scenes that do not cause a later consequence.
• Pass 2 — Moral arc: Track every killing, threat, conversion and act of vengeance. Decide which choices the novel
endorses, condemns or deliberately leaves unsettled. Add concrete fallout for the bandit, Iro, the poisoned priests
and Toma. Give Lora/Viridia/Amelia meaningful disagreement.
• Pass 3 — Magic and law: Draft the rules bible, then revise scenes to plant the purification rune, blood healing,
memory access, inheritance gifts, elemental bonds and forced conversion well before the climax. Remove
exceptions that exist only to rescue the plot.
• Pass 4 — Character agency: Rework Ch. 19 so Lora and Viridia each face distinct losses and make autonomous
choices; give them goals and costs not dependent on Bal. Define the relationship boundaries and power
asymmetries.
• Pass 5 — War logic: Make casualty figures, infiltration, poison dosage and distribution, civilian risk, letter delivery,
extraction and originator intervention internally consistent. Clarify the victory conditions and what the ending
changes politically.
• Pass 6 — Continuity and line edit: Build a scene-by-scene chronology of travel, injuries, spell use, who knows
what, and the location of each character. Then cut redundant explanation, sharpen dialogue and polish syntax.

8 | What the ending earns—and what it still owes
The final chapter succeeds at the novel’s most intimate promise: a young man who was deprived of a hand, an
education and a place in society now belongs among people who choose him. The unfinished homework and return
to the hearth are precisely the right emotional scale after the arena. Do not replace that ending with a larger
explosion or a lecture.
But a final quiet beat can coexist with an explicit acknowledgment of the larger cost. Consider a short exchange,
decision or unresolved obligation about Toma, the newly empowered Blood faction, or Amelia’s retaliation. Readers
should leave knowing what victory means beyond the protagonists’ safety—and what the next book will demand.

Final decision | readiness and potential
Would I finish it? Yes. The warmth of the ensemble, Amelia’s voice and the late infiltration/duel material sustain
interest. Would I represent or acquire this draft? No; I would request major developmental revision. Could it find
readers? Yes, especially among magic-academy, progression-fantasy and romantic-found-family audiences. What
would most improve its prospects? Not more spectacle: stronger causality, enforceable magical limits, and the
courage to let its sympathetic protagonists be morally accountable.
