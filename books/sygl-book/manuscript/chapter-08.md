# Chapter Eight: Too Bad She's a Tempus

The wall opened, and at the top of the stone steps Amelia had her feet up on the coffee table.

She was slouched so far down on the red couch that her shoulders were nearly level with the cushions, her ankles crossed on the polished wood and the hem of her black dress fallen back around her shins. She looked as though she had been there all afternoon and meant to stay until spring. When she heard the stone grinding shut behind him, she rolled her head along the back of the couch to find him, and smiled, and waved.

Bal went over and dropped down beside her. Somebody had built the fire up while he was out. It was throwing heat across the floor and putting a shine on the leather of the empty chair.

"How's your first day?" Amelia asked.

Bal leaned back. The couch took him in up to the ribs.

"Not bad," he said. "I'm glad I've made a friend already. Viridia seems cool."

"I like her too." Amelia flexed her bare toes against the tabletop. "She'd do well in the Blood sygl."

Bal shut his eyes. The fire was warm on the left side of his face and the couch was warm everywhere else, and he had been awake since Verin's cottage.

"Yeah, I think she would," he said. "She's not really a fan of the other originators. Too bad she's a Tempus."

"Yeah," Amelia said. "Too bad."

It was nothing she said. It was how she held on to the last word, past where it should have ended.

Bal opened his eyes and looked at her.

Amelia was looking right back. She had her eyebrows up and her lips pressed together, and she was plainly waiting for something. Bal waited too. Nothing arrived. In the fire a pocket of sap hissed and spat.

Amelia sighed, took her feet off the table and straightened.

"Okay," she said. "I can't really blame you for not seeing where I was going with that. It ties into the High Priest thing anyway." She turned on the cushion to face him and tucked one foot under her. "Do you remember what I said earlier? About there being more than one way to get back at my family?"

"In the corridor," Bal said. "With the paintings."

"Well. Imagine how Raizen would feel if someone with his rarest inheritance gift came over to the Blood."

Bal tried to imagine it. He had seen Raizen's face do two things so far. It had done nothing at all while Raizen read out his names, and it had done something sharp and unguarded for half a second when Amelia clapped. He tried to fit a third expression onto it, the face of a god finding out where the girl with the storm in her hair had gone, and found he would very much like to be in the room for it.

"I wasn't really planning on anyone special," Amelia said. "Not yet. But she's too good to pass up."

Bal pushed himself up out of the cushions.

"So you want me to convert her," he said slowly, making sure he had it right. "That's the thing your family locked you away for, last time. I'd be doing exactly that." He searched her face. "Are you sure that's what you want?"

Amelia's chin came up. All the play had gone out of her mouth.

"They know I'm going to do something to get back at them," she said. "This is the last thing they'd ever expect. Plus, we need more students." She tipped her head at the room around them, at the one couch and the one chair, enough seats in all for three people if one of them didn't mind the arm. "And the fact that she's also really pretty is just another perk for you."

Bal jerked upright so fast the couch bounced. His mouth fell open. He couldn't seem to get it closed.

Amelia burst out laughing.

"I saw you looking at her," she said. "It's okay. I don't mind." She leaned back into the corner of the couch. "I was looking myself."

Bal shook his head. Nothing he could say to that would come out right, so he left it where it lay and went back to the part he could do something about.

"How exactly am I supposed to do that?"

"As a High Priest, you can," Amelia said. "You alone. Nobody else can convert someone's sygl."

She drew his hand over and turned it palm up on her knee. The sygl glowed in the middle of it, faint and red. With the tip of one fingernail she drew a line across the heel of his palm, light as a feather, without breaking the skin.

"You cut your left hand here," she said. "Then you cut theirs, and you put the two together and mingle the blood. Then you have to send your blood into them. Push it in." She pressed her thumb into the middle of his palm. "It takes a while. But once their body gets used to your blood, the sygl on their palm will change. After that they're officially one of my followers."

His hand lay in hers. It sounded simpler than writing out a pizza order.

On the edge of his bed at Saint Andre's, she had told him it could never be done to someone who didn't want it. So it would be up to Viridia. Hers to choose.

Amelia let go of his hand.

"That was another reason I made you High Priest," she admitted. "It wasn't only to keep you safe."

Bal chuckled.

"It's fine. I figured there might be other reasons." The new hand lay open on his knee where she had left it. "Look. You've given me what I've wanted my whole life. Plus more excitement than I could ever have imagined. I don't mind doing these things for you."

Amelia lit up. It went across her whole face at once, and she pulled both knees up under her dress and hugged them.

"Okay," she said, smiling. "So you're okay with Viridia joining us?"

Bal pictured Viridia over the pizza at lunch, her pale blue eyes going bright when she laughed at the familiar joke. He heard her again, saying the gods were only people, out loud, in the middle of a courtyard full of their followers.

"Definitely," he said.

---

After that, Amelia gave him his class schedule.

She had brought it back with her, a single sheet of heavy paper folded in thirds, and she watched him read it. Familiar class came first thing in the morning, then Rune working, then lunch. After lunch he had a class called Warfare, and after that one called Crafting.
The runes didn't worry him. His engraving kit was lying at the bottom of the chest in the bedroom, and he knew more about runes already than about almost anything else. The other two were only names. He read it again and it said the same thing. Warfare could mean anything from swords to maps. Crafting could mean anything at all.

He opened his mouth to ask her, and closed it again. He wanted to walk in tomorrow and find out for himself. Finding out was the best part of anything.

He folded the schedule back into thirds and left it on the table where her feet had been.

The rest of the evening went quickly.

Amelia wanted to know everything about Saint Andre's, and Bal found there was more of it to tell than he'd thought. He told her about washing dishes in the orphanage kitchen for the better part of a year to pay for a rune kit. He told her about Mrs. Brown, who could catch anybody at anything, and why he had never minded being caught. She already knew about the porridge.

When he ran out, he asked about her.

"How old were you?" he said. "When you got turned."

"Sixteen." Amelia rested her cheek on her knees and looked at him sideways. "Same as you."

"Who did it?"

"I never found out. Whoever it was bit me and left me lying in the middle of a road, and a stranger found me there." She watched the fire. "Back then everybody knew vampires existed, but there weren't many of us. Even fewer than there are now."

"The stranger," Bal said. "What happened to him?"

"He was a farmer. I killed him."

She said it plainly, the same as she had said sixteen. Then she watched him to see what he would do with it.

"I was delirious," she said. "I needed blood, and he was there, and I didn't know what I'd done until afterward."

Bal let that sit. Sixteen, alone on a road, not knowing what she was. Then he asked the question that was actually in front of him.

"Is it still like that? The hunger?"

Something in Amelia's shoulders came down.

"No," she said. "It's only that bad when you've just been turned. It ebbs away the longer you're alive." She stopped. "Or undead, I suppose."

And she laughed, short and surprised, as if the word had tripped her on its way out.

"So how do you go from a regular vampire," Bal asked, "to a Vampire Goddess?"

"Magical energy. A lot of it." Amelia spread the fingers of one hand. "It takes an enormous amount to cross the threshold between mortal and Immortal. There's no set amount. You just have to keep consuming it until it happens. I had a leg up, because I could absorb the magic from the people I fed on."

On the inside of Bal's left wrist, where she had bitten him outside Verin's cottage, the skin gave a small itch. He decided it was his imagination.

"I was the newest originator," Amelia went on. "When it finally happened, I was every bit as surprised as the others were. The moment I crossed over, I was somewhere else. A stone temple, with all of them standing in it, staring at me." Her mouth twisted. "I didn't have a clue where I was, and I asked. Aren's the oldest, so he was the one who explained it. What had happened to me, and what I was now."

"What did he say?"

"That I'd be given a sygl, and it would start appearing on mortals' palms. The universe's way of giving me followers." She leaned over and touched one fingertip to the middle of his palm, to the red mark there. "Each follower gives me some magical energy. Enough of them, and it can sustain me forever."

Her finger rested on the sygl.

For sixteen years there had been no palm there at all. Only a stump that itched whenever he looked at her roof.

He didn't say any of that. Amelia took her hand back.

"There was always a war going on," she said. "Somebody had always done something to somebody else. I stayed out of it. I'd rather spend my time with my own followers than play their games with them. They tried to pull me into one of their wars, once." She shrugged one shoulder. "So I went down there myself and wiped out half the battlefield. After that, they decided to leave me alone."

"Half," Bal said.

"About half."

"And that's why they're afraid of you."

"That's why. I don't play by their rules. They play their followers off against each other. Pawns, in their wars. They never go anywhere near the fighting themselves. So when I walked into the middle of one, they understood I wasn't going to play their game at all."

The fire sparked and crackled. Neither of them said anything for a while. A log burned through in the middle and settled. The rest of the dorm was empty and silent.

"You're the first person I've told my full story to," Amelia said.

She said it to the fire.

"To be honest," she said, "it feels good to talk about it."

"I get that," Bal said. "It's easy, talking to you."

She smiled at him.

"What will you be doing tomorrow," he asked, "while I'm in my classes?"

Amelia thought it over.

"Well, after your familiar class, I'll probably head out and see some of my other followers. On your home plane. Let them know I'm back." She smoothed a crease out of her skirt. "Most of them won't ever be able to apply to this school. But I'd like them to know I'm here, at least. I'm not like the other originators. I do try to keep in touch with my followers when I can." She left the skirt alone. "Other than that, I've got a few other errands to run."

The people she meant were somewhere back on his own side of the world. Grown-ups with red marks in their palms, keeping their left hands in their pockets at work. Some of them would have been told she was a myth their whole lives. Tomorrow somebody was going to open a door and find her standing there.

"Speaking of classes, though," Amelia said, unfolding herself from the couch, "you need to get some sleep, Master. A loud bell will wake you when it's time to get up."

"How loud?"

"Loud."

Bal got up too. Before she could step away, he caught her hand. It was cool. He held on to it and smiled at her.

"Out of all the sygls I could have had," he said, "I'm glad I ended up with yours."

Amelia's red eyes went very bright. She opened her mouth, and for once nothing came out of it. Her fingers tightened on his, hard enough that he felt her nails. Then she let go.

Bal collected the schedule on his way past and headed off to bed.

The bedroom was dark except for the line of firelight under the door. He kicked off his shoes and climbed in under the black blanket with the dark rose on it, and it took him a while to find the middle of the mattress. He left the folded schedule on the chest at the foot of it where he would see it when he woke.

He lay awake for a while. Somewhere above him, in one of the towers or down some corridor he hadn't found yet, there was a bell, and it was waiting for morning.
