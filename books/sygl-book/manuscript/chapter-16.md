On Monday Lora asked whether anyone thought it would rain on Saturday.

Nobody did, particularly. On Tuesday she asked again, in case something had changed, and Viridia told her it would only rain if she kept asking. By Wednesday she had given up on the two of them and was going out onto the front steps before breakfast to check the sky for herself. She came back in with her glasses fogged and her cheeks red from the cold, and reported that it looked fine.

She had also decided that nothing on earth was going to keep them inside the castle that weekend, least of all homework. Every evening she marched the three of them up to the Blood common room straight after dinner and sat them down at the low table until every page was done. She did her own twice as fast as either of them and then read the questions over their shoulders. When Viridia complained, Lora pointed out that a teacher could keep a student in on Saturday for unfinished work, and that she had read the rule herself. Viridia stopped complaining and finished her history.

Bal spent two of those evenings in the library first.

The library had very little on the Saegen Woods. The librarian found him a book on ley lines, and in the back of it, a single paragraph. A ley line ran straight through the woods. Some of the animals there had been absorbing the magykal energy for generations, and they had grown bigger than they ought to be, and meaner. How much bigger, the paragraph didn't say.

Bal read it twice. He wondered what a squirrel would turn into, if it lived its whole life on top of a ley line. Whether it still buried nuts. Whether it needed bigger nuts. Then he copied the paragraph into the back of his rune notes anyway, so that he would have it with him.

It was Amelia who surprised him.

On Thursday night she was on the end of the old red couch while the girls finished their runes, and when Lora started in on what time they ought to leave, Amelia said, very lightly, that maybe it would be simpler if she stayed behind this time.

Lora stopped with her pencil in the air.

"Why?" she asked.

"It's your first job," Amelia told them. "I would hate to be in the way."

Viridia put down her book. "You've got a card," she said. "With the team on it and everything. We want you to come."

"You're part of the team," Lora said, and then added, quickly, "If you want to be."

Amelia looked from one of them to the other. Then something in her face gave way, and she told them she would love to, so warmly that Lora went pink.

On Friday, after Warfare, Bal hung back while the line broke up and went to ask Zoran whether the three of them could borrow some of the school's weapons for a trip off the grounds.

Zoran said nothing for so long that Bal began composing a second, better way of asking.

"Bring them back clean," Zoran said.

Bal went to the last rack and took down the rapier he had trained with since the first day. It came with a plain leather scabbard and a belt, and the belt needed a new hole punched in it before it would stay on him. Viridia took down the long spear she always used, and carried it back to the castle on her shoulder.

---

Saturday came up cold and clear. Lora's sky had been right.

They met at the castle gate at first light. Viridia crossed the frosty courtyard with Hopper trotting at her knee.

Bal knew Hopper only from Viridia's stories. With the collar on, she was the size of a big farm dog. Her fur was silver, and across her back and shoulders it ran blood red, as though someone had laid a shawl over her. Her paws were too large for the rest of her. She came past Bal without a glance and made for Amelia, and leaned her whole shoulder against Amelia's skirt, and stayed there.

"Well," Viridia said. "I see where I rank."

Lora arrived last, at a run, and Bal saw she had a rapier at her hip that had never hung on any rack of Zoran's. The guard was dark steel, and the grip had been wrapped and rewrapped so often that it had gone smooth.

"That's yours," he said.

"My father had it made for me. After the tournament." Lora drew it a few inches to show him the blade. "It's enchanted. It never needs sharpening." She slid it home, and for once she didn't mind the attention.

Bal tried Amelia. "We could still get you something. Zoran wouldn't mind."

Amelia was scratching Hopper behind the ears. The offer seemed to surprise her.

"I am my own weapon," she said. "Besides, I don't plan on fighting unless something really bad happens. Each of you needs to feel comfortable defending yourselves."

Bal thought that over and could not find anything wrong with it.

New Spring was on the road out, and Bal had them stop. The job had come through Soren's counter, but the flowers were for the alchemist, and he wanted her to know they were on their way. Her shop was down a lane off the square, with bundles of something grey and dry hanging in the window, and the smell of it reached them before the door did.

Ms. Summers was a small, stooped woman wrapped in two shawls. She heard who they were and why they had come, and she got slowly up off her stool.

"I normally hire a team to see me through those woods," she said. "I'm not up to it this year." She looked at the four of them, and at the wolf on her step, and decided that they would do. "Mind the river. That's where they grow."

Lora asked how the flowers ought to be cut, and how long they would keep once they were. Ms. Summers' face lit up. She answered at length, and Lora wrote it all down on the back of an old receipt against the doorframe, every word, and buttoned it away in her breast pocket.

Leaving the square, they bought food for the night, and Hopper took a great interest in the jerky. It all went into Bal's pack.

---

The woods lay about twenty miles off. They would have to pass through one more town, Whitehaven, and after that cross a mountain pass before they reached the trees.

They came into Whitehaven around midday. It was smaller than New Spring and busier, with a row of food stalls down the middle of its one wide street, and the smoke from all of them blowing the same direction.

"How much farther are the woods?" Viridia asked. She was holding out her money to the man who ran the meat pie stall.

Bal checked the sun, and then his map.

"At least four more hours," he said. "Maybe five." He paid for his own pie.

He had offered, back at the first stall, to pay for all of them. Nobody would let him. Lora had said "No" before he got to the end of the sentence, and Viridia had said it was bad enough he carried everything.

The pies were hot and the crust was the flaky kind that ended up on everybody's shirt. Hopper sat at Amelia's feet and caught the flakes. They walked the length of Whitehaven while they ate, looking in the shop windows, and then set out again.

At the town limit, past the last house, Viridia put her hand on Hopper's collar.

Hopper grew. She grew as if she were unfolding from inside herself, legs lengthening, chest deepening, until her shoulders were above Viridia's waist and her head was level with Bal's chest. She shook herself all over, and her red fur lifted and lay flat.

"I'm glad you thought to buy that collar," Bal said. "I'm not sure how the people back there would have taken a full-sized bloodwolf walking down the street."

Hopper was plainly glad to be clear of the castle. She left the road at once and ranged ahead of them, out to the edge of the trees and back again, nose down and then up, checking everything for them. Lora said her raven would have liked this. It was sick, though, and she had left it at the castle rather than risk it getting hurt.

---

They were a few miles out of Whitehaven, about halfway to the pass, when they stopped to rest. There was a stretch of thick woods on the right side of the road, and a flat rock on the left that was good for sitting.

Bal was getting the water skin out when Hopper growled.

She had turned to face the woods. She was down low on her front legs, and the growl came up from somewhere deep in her chest and kept coming, and all the hair along her back stood up in stiff tufts like a porcupine's quills.

Everyone had their weapon out before they had finished standing.

Seven men stood at the edge of the forest, silent, watching them. Six were in their twenties or thirties. The seventh was much older, with grey hair cropped short and a big, heavy body. The six wore leather armor that was decent enough. The older man wore a shirt of steel mesh that had seen better days. It was pitted in places, and there was dried blood on the rings.

They had longswords. They had axes. Two carried crossbows, and both crossbows were already up and pointed.

"Your weapons," the older man said, "if you please."

He pointed his sword at the dirt by his boots. He wanted them thrown down there.

The crossbows were the problem. Two bolts would cross that ground far faster than any of them could. Charging was pointless. He let his eyes drop, as if he were working out how to toss the rapier and still keep a little pride, and the sun flashed off something silver on Viridia's left hand.

The ring.

He shifted his weight toward her and kept his voice low.

"Can you use the ring to shake the ground under them? It should give us a chance to get to them before they can shoot."

Viridia didn't answer. Her lids drooped. She was already reaching for whatever it was inside the band that she had to reach for.

Bal hoped none of the seven was Terra. This was Kenmei's domain, strictly speaking, so it was possible. There was no way to tell. All seven had weapons in their hands, and a hand holding a weapon did not show you its palm.

He moved his free hand behind his leg, where it was in her view, and opened it.

---

The ground jumped under the bandits' feet.

It bucked like a wagon bed going over a rut, and kept on bucking, and seven men who had been standing still had to start trying to stand. A crossbow bolt went wild somewhere up in the branches.

Bal was already running.

He ran at the leader, because he had done the talking, and because he was the biggest. Off to his right Hopper went past him like a thrown rock. A bolt hit her side and bounced off, and then somebody screamed, a high awful scream, and everybody but their leader jerked at the sound of it.

The leader brought his longsword around to meet Bal, and after that he was all Bal saw.

A rapier was not a hacking blade. Lora had told him that the first week, and Zoran had told him after her. You kept moving. You took the small hits when they were there to take and you never stood still long enough for somebody to land a big one. Bal had been practicing nothing else for weeks.

He kept moving. He parried and stabbed and circled. The big man was slow, but whenever his sword met Bal's, it went up Bal's arm and into his teeth, and he did not tire. Bal put the point into his shoulder and felt it skid on the mail. He put it into the meat of his upper arm and felt that one go in. The big man grunted and came on.

Somewhere to the left a great axe was whistling. Bal circled and caught it at the edge of his sight. A young bandit with an axe was swinging it in wide wild arcs at Viridia, and Viridia was darting in and out between swings and stabbing him, and each time she drew blood he roared and swung harder. Bal circled. The axe came round flat and caught her spear and tore it clean out of her hands. The spear went spinning end over end. Then grey and red hit the axeman from the side, and Hopper had him, and he did not get up.

Bal circled. Parry. Stab. Parry.

Lora was over beyond the leader's shoulder now, on Bal's next turn. Three men had her surrounded. Then two did. Then one, and then that sword was coming again.

Something turned under Bal's heel. A stone, a rut. He stumbled.

He felt the sword coming before he saw it. He threw himself sideways and twisted, and the blade, which had been meant for his spine, opened his back from one shoulder blade to the other instead. It was cold first. Then it was very hot.

He had always been able to take a lot of hurt. He took this, and got his feet back under him.

"Bal!" That was Lora, off to his right. "Should I take him?"

He looked toward her voice.

The grey-haired man's sword opened his left forearm from the wrist halfway to the elbow.

"Do not interfere," Bal said through his teeth.

He meant all three girls. There was no breath to spare for saying so.

The man showed his teeth. There were not many of them, and those he had were brown.

"Want to kill me yourself, huh?" He lunged low, trying for Bal's legs, and Bal skipped back. "Don't blame you. Not with those pretty girls watching. I'll have some fun with them, once I'm done with you."

Bal stepped back, out of reach, and dragged his good wrist across his eyes. It came away wet, sweat and something else.

The girls were all in a line on the road behind him.

Lora had her rapier lowered and her left hand pressed to her stomach. Viridia had her spear back and was up on her toes. Next to Lora stood Amelia. She had come one step off the road toward him and no farther, and her two hands were holding each other back.

Bal took in all three for the length of one breath.

Then he stepped forward with his rapier high, jabbed at the bandit's face, and slashed. The bandit parried. They traded blows, high and low, across the bucked-up dirt, for what seemed an hour and was probably a minute, and then Bal dropped straight down onto his knees and put the point of his rapier through the old man's right knee and out the back of it.

The man howled. His leg folded and he fell, still swinging, the longsword whipping round in a wild flat circle. He tried to rise and his knee would not have it. He settled for swinging, round and round, to keep Bal off.

Bal rose and stayed well clear of the circle. The fight was over. There was no sense in walking into that sword to prove it.

Hopper was standing a little way off with her head low.

"Would you like to finish it?" Bal asked her.

If a wolf could grin, Hopper did. She was across the ground in two bounds. The screaming stopped.

---

Bal stood in the road and got his breath back.

His shirt was wringing wet with sweat, and the back of it hung open. He peeled it off, slowly, and used it to wipe the blood off his arm. There was a long gaping hole in the back of it. He would have to sew that before he wore it again. He wiped his face with the front of it, which was the only part left that was any use, and went to the girls.

Lora let him come right up to her. Then she put both hands on his chest and pushed him down onto a fallen tree at the side of the road.

"Sit," she ordered. "And don't talk."

Her pack was already open. She had salve in a little clay pot, and bandages, and she went around behind him to see to his back first.

Amelia was standing off from the rest of them.

Bal moved over on the log to make room. She came and took the space, very straight-backed.

"What's wrong?" he asked her.

Lora's hands went on working at his arm. It was some time before Amelia answered.

"I think that was the hardest thing I've ever done," she said. "I wanted to hurt that man for daring to hurt you."

She had spent close to three thousand years locked away.

Bal reached over for her hand. Hers held on.

"Thank you for not jumping in," he said. "You too, Lora."

Lora glanced up from where she knelt. Then she returned to his arm.

"I have to be able to protect myself," Bal said. "It was hard for me too, knowing any of you could get hurt. And I'm proud of you. All of you. You took care of yourselves."

The salve smelled of crushed mint and something underneath it that he couldn't place. Wherever Lora had put it, the pain had eased off and gone somewhere far away, and Bal laughed, because it was gone and he had not expected it to go.

"I'd much rather I got hurt than any of you," he said. "Great job on not getting hurt."

Hopper came down from the trees to the log. She smelled of wolf and hard work. Bal rested his good hand lightly on top of her head, between her ears.

"Thanks," he told her. "You did good."

Once he was bandaged up, they went through the bandits for anything worth taking. Bal wiped the rapier clean on a dead man's sleeve and slid it back into its scabbard. Viridia kept her spear in her hand.

They found very little. A few coins. The crossbows, which none of them knew how to use.

On their leader's right hand was a ring. It was a heart, in red gold, small and well made.

"That's a heart ring," Lora said, behind Bal. "They come in pairs. Two people each wear one, and each ring beats in time with the other person's heart. If yours ever stops, it means the other one is dead." She paused. "Or that they took it off."

"It's expensive," Viridia said.

"Very."

You didn't expect to see one on a bandit. Not even the leader.

Bal left it on the dead man's hand.

They talked about it as they walked on, looking for somewhere to camp. They wanted to cross the pass and come into the woods by morning light.

---

About two miles on, a trail turned off the road into the woods.

Amelia's place beside him was empty, with no noise at all. She was back before Bal had got his pack off his shoulders, brushing a leaf off her shoulder.

"There's a fire pit," she said. "Buried, so it's been used. I think travelers stop here a lot."

Bal left his pack with his bedroll by the pit and went to look for firewood. He went bare-backed in the bandages, because his shirt was a rag, and the evening air found its way in under every strip.

Viridia saw where he was headed and came along.

They walked in silence for a way. Viridia did the picking up, branches and sticks off the forest floor, and laid them across Bal's arms, which he held out for her, stiff as a pair of shelf brackets.

"I didn't expect we'd have to fight so soon," she said finally.

"Me neither." The ring was still bothering him, and he could not have said why. "We knew it was a possibility, though. I just didn't expect bandits. I thought it'd only be things in the Saegen Woods we had to worry about."

The cut on his arm gave a burn under the bandage, as if it had heard.

"I can't believe nobody else got hurt but me." Bal chuckled. "Don't get me wrong, I'm glad none of you did. I'm only surprised it wasn't worse."

"Lora's a menace with that rapier of hers." Viridia laid a branch across his arms with some force. "I thought I was doing okay. Until Hopper had to save me anyway. Then I turn around to see how everybody else is doing, and Lora's already taken care of three of them by herself." She stopped, still not quite believing it. "And Amelia. She looked about ready to have a heart attack, watching you fight that big man. Can a vampire have a heart attack?"

Bal had no idea.

"Anyway, all she said was 'I do not like this.' And then, 'He is already hurt.' Before I'd even seen it." Viridia pulled a dead branch off a stump. "She hates that she can't protect you. Lora had it easier, I think. I heard her muttering about all the mistakes you were making." She laughed. "Nonstop."

While it was happening, the fight had felt endless, all that dodging and all those careful strikes. It had probably been over in three or four minutes. Bal was reasonably sure he could have handled one of the younger men without much trouble. But he would be damned if he let any of the girls take on the big one and get hurt doing it. He would much rather be hurt himself.

They had been lucky, too. If Hopper had not gone for the crossbows first, it could have gone a lot worse.

Soon his arms were full, and they turned back.

---

Somebody had laid out all the bedrolls around where the fire would be. They lay in a ring, an arm's length apart, except for one, which had another set close beside it.

Bal guessed which two those were. Nobody owned up to doing it, and he let it be.

Viridia stood her spear against a tree, and Bal got the fire going. Lora had a needle in her pack, and he sewed the hole in his shirt shut by firelight with stitches so big that Viridia winced at them, and pulled it back on over the bandages. Dinner was the bread, the jerky and the cheese from New Spring that morning. Hopper got the end of the jerky.

The talk around the fire was all the fight, and how to do better next time.

"Arrows," Bal said. "Those crossbows scared me." He chewed on the idea along with the jerky. "I could make us shirts. With runes worked into them that turn arrows away."

Lora sat straight up.

"I'd have to charge every rune," he said. "Each one only works once. So you'd want a lot of them. I'd just have to buy the materials."

Viridia announced that she would wear anything, as long as it wasn't brown.

Lora finished her cheese. Then she brushed off her hands and planted herself over Bal until he understood that she meant him.

"Up," she said. "You stumbled because your back foot was too far behind you. Here. Look." She put him in his stance and kicked his heel in an inch. "And you keep dropping your point after a lunge. Every time. He saw it by the third one. Again."

She went through every mistake he had made, in order. He asked her whether it was cheating to drop to the ground like that, and what you did if your opponent was taller and also left-handed, and she answered all of it patiently, however silly it sounded once it was out of his mouth.

All that while Amelia sat across the fire, hugging her shins, and said almost nothing. Bal noticed. He was sure the others had too.

When Lora was done with him, he went around the fire and held out his hand to Amelia. She took it, and he pulled her up.

"We're going for a walk," he told the others.

---

He kept hold of her hand into the forest.

At that time of night the only sounds were the insects and their own feet in the fallen autumn leaves. The fire dropped behind them to a smudge of orange between the trunks.

"What's on your mind?" Bal asked, once they were well away from camp.

Amelia took her time answering. Bal waited. He did not mind waiting. It was a good night for it, cold and very quiet.

"I feel useless." Her voice was flat and quiet. "I finally have people I think of as friends, and I can't help them. I was afraid someone would die. I can't remember ever being afraid of that before. When he cut your back," she said, and her eyes shut, and opened again, "it took every bit of will I have not to run over there and tear that man in half."

Bal walked with that a while.

"Amelia, sometimes we have to fight our own battles," he said. "I'd never fought someone who really wanted to hurt me before. And I'd do it all again, to protect any of you."

The leaves crunched. Somewhere off to the right something small ran away from them.

"I know that in the big scheme of things, there's little I can offer you besides my company. The things that can hurt the others can't hurt you. And you're still just as important to me as they are. If your family came after you, think how that would make me feel. There'd be nothing I could do to help you. I don't like that. But I trust you to do everything in your power to stay safe."

He stopped her there, among the black trunks, and turned her to face him.

"Our group means a lot to me. You mean a lot to me. I don't need you here for what you can do for me. I want you here because I like you."

She was quiet.

"Besides," he said, "you can always jump in if you see one of us about to get killed. Say a crossbow bolt had hit somebody. You've just got to believe we'll do everything we can to stay alive."

He hoped she would take all of it to heart. He left her to it, and led her back toward the orange smudge between the trees.

---

The other two had already crawled into their bedrolls when they got back to the firelight. Lora had her glasses folded on a rock beside her head. Viridia was a lump with blue hair coming out of one end.

Amelia pushed her bedroll over until it lay against Bal's, while he put more wood on the fire.

Before he lay down he went to Hopper at the edge of the light.

"You going to be our guard?" he asked.

Hopper chuffed at him, once.

That settled it. Bal went back and slid into his bedroll. Amelia's hand found his under the blankets, cool as the night was.

Beyond the trees the mountains stood up black against the stars, and in the black there was a notch where the road went through. They would be in it an hour after first light. The woods were beyond it.
