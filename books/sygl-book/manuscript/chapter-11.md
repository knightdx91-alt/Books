# Chapter Eleven: Break Their Toys

It was the same as Viridia's, with differences. Where Viridia had Raizen's mark, the blood sygl sat on the back of Amelia's tight black sweater, red on black, the same mark Bal wore on his, and below the sweater was a short black skirt. Where every other girl in the school wore plain white socks, Amelia's were long and striped black and red, and they came up past her calves.

She looked good in it. Bal could admit that much. He also could not keep from looking, and Amelia knew it. She hopped down off the stage, landed lightly, and her smile got wider with every step she took toward them.

"So how have your classes been?" she asked.

Bal glanced sideways. Viridia was having a hard time of her own. She kept fixing her eyes on the castle wall, and they kept drifting back to the striped socks. Bal found this far more entertaining than his own problem.

"Pretty good so far," he said. "We're on our way to the last one now. How are your errands going?"

Amelia's smile faded a little at a time.

"That's partly why I'm here, unfortunately," she said. She faced Viridia. "I'm sorry, but I'm going to have to steal him for a while."

Viridia flapped a hand at her. "It's fine. Do you want me to tell our teacher?"

"No. I've already taken care of it."

"Okay, then." Viridia was already backing toward the castle doors. "You two have fun!"

Bal's attention went after her. He admired the swing of her skirt as she took the steps, and her legs too, and he kept on admiring until the doorway swallowed her. Then he turned back around and found Amelia studying him with her head on one side.

"What?" he asked, and laughed, because his ears had gone warm.

Amelia smirked. "Oh, nothing. Just admiring the view." She let that hang for exactly as long as it took him to work out which view she meant. "So. Ready?"

"Ready for what? What's going on?"

She didn't answer. She took his hand instead, and the courtyard went out.

---

Wind hit him first. It was cold and smelled of cut grass, and it came straight up a long slope and flattened his sweater against his ribs. Bal had the engraving kit pinned under one arm, and he clamped down on it hard before he knew he had done it. His ears popped. The ground under his shoes was springy and uneven, nothing like flagstones, and for a second his knees could not agree with it.

They stood on top of a hill. Below them, filling the valley from one side to the other, was an army.

So many soldiers in one place was a thing Bal had no practice at. Tents ran off in rows to the far tree line, gray canvas going pale with distance, and between the rows were cookfires and wagons and horse lines and narrow lanes trodden down to dirt. Flags stood up all through the main camp on tall poles, and each carried the same jagged yellow mark, snapping in the wind. It was Raizen's. Tempus.

Somebody had sewn all those flags. Hundreds. Bal wondered if they had a person whose whole job it was.

"He doesn't already know we're trying to steal Viridia from him," he said, once Amelia let go of his hand. "Does he?"

The army below was in the middle of marching drills. Block after block of soldiers came across the open ground in step, and two blocks would swing toward each other and fold together into one, and then that one would turn and fold into the next. It was slow, and very tidy. Whenever a whole rank turned at once, the sun caught on their swords and spearheads and ran down the line.

"No, it's not that," Amelia answered. "I had a feeling one of them might try to go after my followers soon, now that I'm free. So I've been out looking for the other originators' armies. I've only found two so far. Raizen's, and Naisura's." She folded her arms against the wind. "Naisura's doesn't look like it's done anything in months. Last year he went after Nera and got beaten very badly. He's probably still licking his wounds."

"So why would Raizen come after you," Bal asked, "if he doesn't know about Viridia?"

"The games they play." Amelia started and then stopped, and frowned down at the camp. "I hate that they think of war as a game. But they do. And their fights are ranked, I suppose is the best way to put it. Almost nobody goes after someone too far above them."

"Like Naisura going after Nera."

"Exactly like that. So Raizen's only real choices right now are me or Naisura. He could probably beat Naisura. But I'm free again, and the job of dealing with me has fallen to Raizen."

Bal worked it through.

"Because you're ranked below all of them," he said. "Beneath their notice." A block of soldiers far down the slope missed its turn and had to go around again. "So I'm surprised they all came after you together like they did, last time. Knowing what they're like now. It's hard to picture them working together on anything."

"Ah. That was because they felt I was stealing from them." Amelia watched the soldiers too. "They do not share. What's theirs, they feel should stay theirs. Me winning a few of their followers over was an insult to them, personally. They couldn't let an insult like that stand."

Down in the valley a horn blew, flat and long, and every block stopped where it stood.

Bal took them all in. Men with spears. Men with swords. Somebody's sons, somebody's fathers, standing in rows on a hillside so that an originator who never thought about them could feel he'd answered an insult. Only this morning he had met this in the library, in neat print. Battle after battle, and nobody in the books had ever asked the people in the rows.

He would not step aside while innocent people were killed for the originators' amusement. He knew that already, the same as he knew his own name.

"So where does that leave us?" he asked.

Amelia smiled at him sidelong. It was a hard smile, and it showed her fangs.

"We play their game," she said, "and break their toys."

---

The horn blew again below them, two short notes, and the blocks began to come apart.

"I've moved quite a few of my followers to a hidden temple of mine," Amelia told him. "It was built about a thousand years ago, by a follower of mine, which surprised me as much as anybody. His name was Peter."

She said the name gently, as if it might break.

"He was the only blood sygl ever born into his family. All the rest of them had always gotten Terra's sygl, every generation, and they were stoneworkers, so it helped them enormously. Peter was sixteen when his sygl came, and he'd already learned the whole family business. And then his palm came up red." Amelia's arms tightened across her sweater. "They shunned him for it. So he went wandering up into the mountains. A few days later he found the perfect spot for a temple. He wasn't a Terra, and his family had thrown him out, so he built a temple to me instead. Every stone in it was laid by his own hand."

Sixteen. The red mark on Bal's palm seemed to sit up and want attention.

"How can you know all that?" he asked. "You were locked away."

The corners of Amelia's mouth turned down.

"I suppose you should hear it anyway," she said. "The normal offering in one of my temples is blood. Every temple of mine has a stone bowl and a sharpened stone knife. A follower cuts themselves and lets a little blood drip into the bowl as an offering. Then the priest seals the cut and tries to help them with whatever's wrong. And whenever someone makes an offering, I take in some of their recent memories, for a short time."

The wind pushed her hair across her face, and she let it stay.

"It took Peter twelve years to finish his temple," she said. "As a final dedication, he killed himself on my altar. He let his lifeblood run out so he could live with me forever." She was quiet for a breath. "All of his memories are still with me. From his first day to his last. What his family did to him hurt him very badly. And he was glad, at the end. Glad he'd been able to make something that would last long after he was gone."

Twelve years. Bal could not think of anything he had kept at for twelve years. He had been four, twelve years ago.

He thought of a boy alone in the mountains, sixteen, with a red palm, setting one stone on top of another for a god who would never come to see it. And then the god herself, standing on a cold hill, carrying the whole of him around inside her, birth to death, with nobody to tell.

Bal stepped forward and pulled her into a hug.

Amelia went stiff in his arms. He held on anyway. After a moment her arms came up, slowly, and went around him, and her chin settled on his shoulder. She was cool right through, sun or no sun. They stood like that on top of the hill, above the whole of Raizen's army, and neither of them was in any hurry to let go.

When they stepped apart, she would not quite meet his eyes, and she was smiling.

Her followers were afraid of her. The other originators couldn't be bothered with her. Bal liked her, and from how long she had held on he understood that it was worth something to her.

"What can I do to help?" he asked.

---

She told him.

The Blood army had to be trained, and someone had to think up tricks to help it beat the other originators' armies. Leaving out the originators themselves, a High Priest was the head of an army. Under the High Priests came the regular priests, and under them everyone else.

"You'll need to meet with them every so often," Amelia said, "so they can see you're involved. I can't interfere, not if I want to look impartial. I'll give you advice. But I can't deal directly with my own lesser followers, or with the enemy's army. Everything has to go through you."

Bal let that sink in.

"You do know I'm only sixteen, right?" he said. "Do you really think grown men are going to listen to a sixteen-year-old about war?"

Amelia's eyes went hard.

"No. I expect them to listen to their High Priest," she said. "If they do not, it will be handled."

Bal made a quiet note to himself to settle whatever he could before she killed anybody.

"Okay," he said, in his most reasonable voice. "Then that's what I'll do. I'll get some books on military strategy from the library and start reading up. Do you have more people to move to your temple?"

"Yes. It'll take me the better part of a week. I have to move them, and all the supplies they'll need while they're there."

Bal turned back to the valley. The soldiers had moved on to weapons drills, pair facing pair across the trampled grass, and the clash of swords rolled up the slope to them, late and small.

"When do I need to meet them?"

"That's up to you, really. Most of the upper command are already there."

Bal squinted at the sun. It had come a fair way down the sky while they talked.

"Do we have time before I've got to be back for dinner?"

"You just want to go hang out with Viridia," Amelia said, in a singsong.

Bal shrugged. He didn't bother denying it.

"Well, let's go, then," she said, and hooked her arm through his. Grass and wind and the whole army were gone between one breath and the next.

---

From the way Amelia had talked, Bal had expected something modest. One man had built it, after all.

The window in front of him said otherwise.

They had come down in a bedchamber, and the bedchamber had a tall arched window, and through it the temple fell away beneath him in tiers of pale cut stone. It filled the floor of a canyon. On three sides the mountains went straight up, bare and gray and streaked with snow at the top, and the only way in was a single narrow pass on the fourth side. Even a small army could hold that pass. Bal understood at once why Amelia had made this place her base.

There were windows everywhere he looked, rows of them, set into wing after wing. The rooms could have held several hundred people without filling up. The priests' quarters alone were large, and two whole sets belonged to High Priests, and he was standing in one of them.

Far down in the lowest courtyard, small figures were crossing back and forth with crates on their shoulders. Somebody was hanging washing from a line strung between two pillars. A handful of the followers Amelia had already brought, making a start on living here.

Twelve years. Looking out at it, Bal believed them all.

"I promoted two of my followers to priest yesterday," Amelia said behind him, "to help you. They're next door. I'll let you meet them, and then they'll go and talk to the commander."

Bal turned around. The room was dark red and old gold. There was a desk, a washstand, a chair with carved arms, and a great bed hung with curtains, and Amelia had already thrown herself backward onto the middle of it. She lay with her arms flung out and her striped socks crossed at the ankle, making herself at home.

"You're not coming with me?"

Amelia held up an empty hand. Then it was not empty anymore. There was a book in it.

"No," she said, and opened the book on her stomach. "This part is yours to do on your own. Oh! Before I forget again."

She clicked her fingers at him without looking up from the page. Something cold slid onto his middle finger. It was a slim white ring, and set into it was a single blood red ruby, round and dark, like an eye.

"That ring is your proof that you're a High Priest," Amelia went on. "It also enables you to do various other things." She was grinning behind the book.

Bal caught her meaning. He left his kit lying on the coverlet, waved to her, and went next door.

---

The priests' room was a little smaller than the High Priest's, and no less richly done. Two men waited in it, both somewhere in their twenties, in scarlet robes with white sashes tied around the waist. They had been sitting. Now they were up and staring at him, and then at the door he had come through, and then at him again. A stranger had just walked out of the High Priest's bedroom, and he was a kid in a school sweater.

Bal lifted his hand and showed them the ring.

He watched it land. Both of them hurried over.

"High Priest!" said the one on the left, and then the one on the right, a heartbeat behind him. "We didn't know you were coming."

The one on the left introduced himself as Thad Moren. He was a slight man, pale, with long black hair tied back in a ponytail and a sharp nose. The other was Xavier Giles. He was big in the shoulders, tan, and the backs of his hands were crossed with old pale scars that looked to Bal like a blade's work. He had clear blue eyes. They went over Bal once, quickly, and Bal had the feeling they did not miss much.

Both of them were surprised at his age. They did not hide it very well. But Thad said, and Xavier agreed, that if Lady Amelia had chosen him, that was good enough for them.

There was a table against the wall. Bal waved them over to it and they all sat.

He told them why they were here. He told them more than he would tell the commander, though not everything. He told them that the other originators had locked Amelia away over some insult they thought she'd done them, and that it had taken her all this time to break free.

"I want that story to get around," he said. "The whole army. Make sure they know she didn't abandon them by choice."

Thad's hands had gone flat on the table. Xavier said nothing. His eyes had fixed on a bare patch of wall and did not come off it.

Bal went on. The goal was to get everyone trained so they could go after Raizen, soon, because Raizen was already training his own army to do the same to them.

"The only thing helping us," Bal said, "is that this temple is hidden. We've got surprise on our side. Raizen won't expect us to have started gathering an army already."

They went on a while about smaller matters. When they all got to their feet, Thad dug in the pocket of his robe and came out with a dark stone, flat and smooth and about the size of his palm.

Two runes were engraved into one face of it in white. One was the light rune. The other, worked in close beside it, was the rune for pair. Bal liked the cut of the lines.

Thad tossed it to him. Bal caught it against his chest.

"If we have to reach you," Thad said, "we'll signal you with that. It'll glow a soft red until you tap it against the one we've got. If you want us, send your magyk into it. That sets it off."

The back of it was blank. Bal slid it into his pocket.

"Thanks," he said. "Go on and see to your duties."

They both bowed a little and went out, already talking low to each other, off in search of the commander.

---

Amelia had not moved. She lay in the middle of the bed with her book held up over her face.

"You know," she said, and turned another page, "I think she really does like you. She won't come out and say it. Not yet, anyway. But you've impressed her."

Bal's head was still full of priests and armies and rune stones. It took him a second to find where she had gone.

"Oh," he said. "You mean Viridia?"

He perched on the edge of the mattress, next to his kit. Impressed her. He went back over the day, trying to find the part that could have done it. Mr. Porter's class, maybe, and Mr. Porter running out of words. Unless it was something else entirely, something he had said at lunch without noticing, and she had carried it home and turned it over. That was a worse idea, because then it could have been anything.

"Mm-hm," said the book. "She does say some interesting things about you."

Bal frowned at her. "Says? Says where?"

Amelia turned the book around and showed him the cover. Across the front, in neat ink, was a name he knew.

"You're reading her diary?"

Amelia laughed at his face.

"What? I like knowing people's secrets. What better way than reading someone's diary?" She shut the book with a snap and rolled up onto her elbow. "Ooh. Do you keep a diary?"

"No. That's never really been my thing." Bal considered. "I think it's mostly girls who do that."

He thought a little more.

"Do you have a diary?"

Amelia went pink. She rolled onto her other side, so that he had nothing of her to look at but the back of her head.

"Maybe," she said to the bed curtains. "You'll never find it, though."

Bal grinned at the back of her head. Well, that settled it. Now he had no choice. Somewhere, in some drawer or under some loose stone in the world, there was a book, and he was going to find it.

When Amelia rolled back, Viridia's diary was no longer in her hand.

"We'd better go," she said, "if you mean to meet Viridia for dinner. I'll see you back at the dorm afterward."

She reached for him. Bal raised a palm, and she stopped.

"You know you can eat with us, right?" he said. "Viridia likes you. So do I. There's no reason for you to hide away."

Amelia lowered her eyes to the white ring on his finger and kept them there.

"Thank you for asking." Her voice had gone small. "Just give me a little time."

Bal didn't push. He gathered up the rune kit and laid his hand in hers.

"Let's get back," he said.

Somewhere ahead of him was a long table under the castle's high ceiling, and a girl with pale blue hair cutting into her dinner. He had read none of her diary. Not one word. He would have to sit down opposite her anyway, knowing it was out there with his name in it, and what his face ought to do about that, he could not begin to guess.
