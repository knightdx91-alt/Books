# Chapter Six: The Claiming

It was an hour or so after they left the cottage before Bal saw anyone else on the road.

The forest track came out onto a wider road, packed hard and rutted by years of wheels, and there ahead of them was a black carriage with a coachman in a tall hat perched up front. Behind it rattled a buggy much like Verin's. Soon more were coming in from side tracks, all heading in one direction. A boy in a school sweater hung out of one carriage window with his elbows on the sill until somebody inside hauled him back in by the belt.

Bal sat up straighter. His own sweater still smelled of brown paper.

Verin slowed Thistle to a walk and fell in behind a buggy with three passengers crowded onto its back bench. Two of them were boys around Bal's age, swapping elbows over some joke. The third was a girl a bit older than either of them. She sat with her back straight and her hands still, and she was the only one of the three not talking.

She had the Tempus sygl on the back of her sweater.

Bal twisted to check his own back. What little showed of it was still blank, exactly as Verin had promised it would be. The girl's sygl was sharp and angular, and whenever the sun broke through the branches and landed on it, the thing crackled. Tiny threads of light jumped across the wool and vanished. Bal caught it three or four times and couldn't work out whether the sweater was doing it or the girl was.

Since they were all in the same buggy, he figured the three of them were siblings.

Amelia had her chin propped on her fist and was watching the sparks too.

It wasn't long before he caught his first view of the school.

At the top of a long rise the trees fell away, and there it was across a wide green valley, the school where every Heir began. It was the oldest of all the schools the originators had made, though no one knew exactly when it was built. From a distance it could have been a castle out of a picture book, an enormous ornate pile of pale stone. Five stone towers stood around it, one for each of the originators, but the main building in the middle was so massive it made the towers look slender.

Five. Bal worked out what that meant and stole a look at Amelia.

Her eyes were on the towers too. Which one was hers, she didn't say.

As they came down into the valley, the school kept growing. A great castle wall ran right around it, high and thick, with the tops of the towers standing up behind it. The road ran straight to a gate of black iron, and the gate was up. Bal craned his neck as Verin drove them underneath. The bottom of the gate was a row of iron points, each one longer than his arm, hanging over the road.

---

The noise hit him the moment they were through.

The courtyard inside was huge, paved in pale flagstones and walled in on every side by the school itself, and it was full of students. A large crowd of them had gathered in front of a raised stone stage at the opposite end, all in the same dark sweaters, all talking at once.

On the stage stood five thrones in a row.

They were carved of marble, white and veined all through with lines of blue, so that from here they looked cool to the touch even in the sun. Each throne had a sygl engraved in gold across the front of it. The gold gleamed. Bal's eyes went along the row, one sygl after another, until they reached the last one.

The sygl on that one was the shape he had been staring at in his own hand for two days.

Verin pulled the buggy to the side, clear of the carriages still unloading, and they climbed down.

"Head right to the back and don't wander off," Verin warned them, leaning down from his seat. "It won't be long before it begins."

"Thank you," Bal said. "For the stew, and everything."

Verin gave him that easy smile again. "Good luck to the both of you."

They waved to him as he turned the buggy around. Thistle took the turn without any hurry, and the buggy went back out under the iron points and was lost among the other carriages. Verin was going home, to his cottage and his fire pit and his one chair. He had no idea who had slept on his floor.

The two of them hovered at the back of the crowd.

Up close the noise broke apart into conversations. Everyone already knew which sygl they would be claimed under, and they had started grouping up by it without being told. Near Bal a boy was complaining to nobody in particular that his trunk had come off in the wrong carriage. A girl beside him wasn't listening. She was standing on her toes to see the stage.

No one came to stand by them. There was no group for them to join.

Bal leaned toward Amelia.

"Why have this ceremony at all," he asked quietly, "if everyone already knows what sygl they are?"

"Because it keeps the thought that the originators care about you alive," Amelia said. "You know. That they took the time to choose you, personally." She looked out over the sea of dark sweaters, and spoke more gently. "Plus, for a lot of these people, this is their first step into the magykal world. Something like this stays with you."

As she finished, a gong sounded.

It was a deep, round note, and it rose out of the stones under their feet as much as from anywhere. The talking died away in stages, from the front of the crowd to the back, until even the boy with the trunk went quiet.

Bal had a thought he should have had a day ago.

"Can they sense you here?" he asked her in a rush, under his breath.

Amelia grinned and shook her head.

There was a bright flash.

---

It was white and total, and for a second Bal was blind. He blinked hard. Colors swam back into the courtyard, the stone, the sweaters, the blue-veined marble.

Every throne but the far right one was now filled.

On the far left sat Aren, the originator of the Duality sygl. Since it was daytime, he appeared as an elderly man with a long white beard, in brown wizard's robes and sandalwood sandals. Bal knew from his books that after dark Aren would be someone else entirely: Nera, a young blond woman in a shimmering white dress. He tried to picture the change happening at sunset, beard and all, and couldn't.

Next to Aren sat Kenmei, the Waves originator, a tall, stately woman in flowing blue robes with her brown hair falling over her shoulders.

Naisura was next, the Terra originator. He had the look of a man in his thirties, and he was bare-chested, with a pair of white drawstring pants and no shirt at all. He looked like he had stopped by on his way back from the gym.

Last in the row was Raizen, the originator of the Tempus sygl. He looked middle-aged, in plain orange monk's clothing, his hands resting together on his knees.

The fifth throne was empty. Nobody up there looked at it. They'd had centuries of practice.

The courtyard was entirely silent. Aren rose from his seat and walked to the edge of the stage, and when he spoke his voice carried easily to the back of the crowd without his having to raise it.

"Welcome to our school," he said. "As most of you know, this was the school we built first, together, and so we have always been particular about who is chosen to come here. Never forget that out of all the students, it was you who got the chance to come here, rather than to one of the lesser schools. As of now you are officially an Heir, with all the prestige that comes with the title."

Bal thought of Fredrick, waving from the back window of a car on his way to a lesser school, and decided Fredrick would have laughed at that.

"Now it is time to begin," Aren went on. "We will start with me. I will call out each of my chosen, and you will line up behind my throne. Then Kenmei, and so on, until all of you are gathered behind us. Once everyone has been chosen, each of us will lead you to your sleeping quarters, where your Lead Mage will give you your class schedule. Any questions you have for us must be asked at that time. Is that understood?"

All across the courtyard, heads bobbed. Aren went back to his throne and sat.

There were about fifty students in the crowd, so it would take a while.

Aren read his names first. Students broke from the crowd as their names were called and climbed the steps at the stage's edge, and a line began to grow behind his throne. After the first few names Bal stopped listening to them. He leaned in close to Amelia instead.

"You okay?" he whispered.

"Mm-hm." Amelia kept her eyes on the stage.

"You don't know how long I've waited for this," she said. Her eyes shone bright in the sunlight.

"About three thousand years, right?" Bal said.

Amelia's head turned, startled. Then she grinned at him, wide and crooked.

"Well, yeah," she said. "I guess you do know." Her gaze returned to the four on their thrones. "I promise you, this will be an initiation no one forgets."

---

The claiming went on and on.

Aren finished, and Kenmei rose and read her list in a low, clear voice, and the Waves students filed past in a steady stream. Naisura read his without standing up at all. The crowd in front of the stage shrank. Gaps opened in it, then whole stretches of bare flagstone where groups had been standing. The sun moved off one throne and onto the next. Bal's feet began to ache, and then stopped bothering to.

Raizen was the last. He stood to read, and his voice was drier than the others'. When he had been reading for a while, Bal saw a flicker of light at the edge of the thinning crowd. It was the girl from the buggy, heading for Raizen's line, and the sygl on her back gave off one small crackle as she passed out of the shade. Bal had missed her name. He hadn't been listening.

Raizen read on. The last few students went up.

Raizen stopped reading and took his seat.

Bal and Amelia were the only two people left standing in front of the stage.

A pigeon was walking on the flagstones, and Bal could hear its feet. Back where all the lines stood, a whisper started up, and then another, as the students there noticed that Raizen had finished and the two of them were still standing there.

Aren rose and came to the edge of the stage again.

"Young man," he said, and his voice carried across the courtyard. "Come closer. Bring your companion."

The two of them walked up to the stage. It was a long way with everyone watching. When they stopped at the foot of it, Bal could finally see the originators properly. Aren's beard was combed. Kenmei's eyes were a very pale sea green.

Aren turned to the others.

"Are they yours?" he asked them.

One by one, each of them said no.

Aren faced them again with a sad smile.

"I'm sorry, young ones," he said. "If yours is the blood sygl, then I'm afraid you've come so far for nothing. As you can see." He gestured toward the empty throne, and the gold sygl on its front shone back at him. "Lady Amelia has not come to claim you. And unless you have an originator as your patron, you may not attend this school."

Bal stepped forward.

"But what if she did claim us?" he asked. He pitched it to carry, loud enough that he was sure the lines at the back would hear every word. "Could we attend then?"

Aren nodded, but his smile was still a sad one. "Of course you could. But alas, Lady Amelia has not been seen in a very long time."

Bal felt Amelia step up beside him.

She looked at Aren. Then she looked past him at the others, Kenmei, Naisura, Raizen, taking her time about each of them.

She clapped her hands, once.

It wasn't loud. Half the students at the back probably never heard it. But Aren stumbled backward as if the stage had tipped under him, and caught himself on the arm of his own throne. Kenmei's hands had locked onto the arms of hers. Naisura sat bolt upright. Raizen's hands came apart.

Bal turned his head.

She looked exactly the same to him. Black hair, pale skin, the ruby earrings, the red eyes. Nothing about her had changed at all.

"I disguised myself," Amelia muttered to him out of the side of her mouth. "You could see me as I was. No one else could."

That made sense, Bal thought. He faced the stage again.

Amelia sprang lightly up onto the edge of it. The originators followed her with their eyes the whole way, warily, none of them moving. She paid them no attention. She walked along behind Aren and Kenmei and Naisura and Raizen, past each of their thrones in turn, to the end of the row, where she turned and took her seat.

She settled into it as if she had last sat there yesterday.

"Balthazaar Norheart," she said, "I formally claim you. I also name you my High Priest."

Amelia turned in her seat to face the others. Her eyes narrowed.

"Let the first one harm him at their peril."

Nobody on the stage said anything.

"There are things that need to be said," Amelia told them. "But they will wait. You will not return to your domains until they are said." She paused. "Will you?"

The other originators shook their heads, all four of them, and quickly. Bal saw them do it. Up until then, he hadn't understood how anybody could be afraid of her. Now he was starting to.

The coldness left her face. She turned to Bal and motioned with two fingers for him to go on around to the back.

---

He went up the side steps and around the back of the thrones, and there they all were.

Every first-year in the school was standing back there in four lines, with the space behind the fifth throne left bare. All of them were staring at him. Some looked confused. Some looked awed. A few, here and there, looked downright excited. A boy near the front of the Terra line had his mouth open, and nobody had told him.

Bal went and stood behind Amelia's throne by himself. It was the shortest line in the courtyard by a wide margin.

Aren clapped his hands. This one was an ordinary clap. Nobody stumbled. Each of the originators stood and descended the side steps, and their lines filed after them into the school in long dark ribbons.

Amelia stood last. She came around her throne and beckoned for Bal to follow her.

She led him in through a small door and down a narrow stone corridor, leaving the noise of the others behind, until it ended at a set of stairs going down. Their footsteps echoed in the narrow space. Bal waited until the courtyard was well behind them.

"Gotta admit," he said, "the angry vampire Goddess was a good look on you."

Amelia's laugh went bouncing down the stairwell ahead of them.

"They just know I could have wreaked havoc if I'd wanted to," she said. "And they're glad I didn't."

At the bottom of the stairs they came out into another corridor, wider than the first, and this one was lined with paintings. Old ones, mostly, in heavy frames, landscapes and portraits of strangers in clothes nobody had worn for centuries. A woman in one of them was holding a fish. He made a note to come back and find out why.

"Why didn't you?" Bal asked. "Wreak havoc, I mean."

Amelia shrugged.

"As mad and hurt as I was when it first happened," she said, "the anger has died down since then." She glanced at a portrait as they went by and then away again. "There are other ways to get revenge."

Bal waited, but she left it there. So he tried something else.

"And the High Priest thing?"

Amelia winced.

"Yeah. I know I probably should have warned you first," she said. "But honestly, I did it to keep you safe."

"Keep me safe?" Bal asked.

"Any of the originators can mess with any follower," Amelia said, "even a regular priest, up to a point. But they are not allowed to interfere with a High Priest. If they do, the offended party can challenge the originator who did it." She cut her eyes toward him. "They do not want that. I would be only too happy to go toe to toe with any one of them, and they know it. This way, if they try anything, I can back you up all the way. Not only as far as a familiar is allowed to."

Bal turned that over as they walked. He pictured Aren catching himself on the arm of his throne. Then he nodded.

"Anything special I need to know?" he asked. "About the whole High Priest thing?"

Amelia hesitated. For a few steps she didn't answer at all, and her eyes traveled on along the corridor, past the paintings to wherever it was they were going.

"We'll talk more about it tonight," she said.
