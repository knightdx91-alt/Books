# Chapter Nine: The Familiar Class

Breakfast was eggs, bacon, toast and a bowl of oatmeal, and Bal had to borrow paper to order it.

Any paper would do now, but he had come down to breakfast without any. Viridia watched him pat both his pockets, then tore a page out of the back of a notebook and slid it over.

"Write small," she said. "I want the rest of that page back."

He wrote small, tore the unused half off and handed it back, and laid his half on the plate.

"Let me see your schedule," she said.

He'd picked it up on his way out. She unfolded it, laid it beside a sheet of her own, and ran her eyes down both. Twice somebody at the Tempus table leaned out to see who Viridia was eating with, and leaned back in. The second time, Bal caught a scrap of what they were saying over there. The clap, and what Raizen's face had done at it. A boy with his collar turned up said a god didn't get made to jump in front of the whole school and then forget it, and nobody at his end of the bench told him he was wrong.

"Familiar class," Viridia read. "Same. Warfare, same. Crafting, same." She tapped the second line of his. "Rune working, different."

"So all of them but one."

"All of them but one." She handed the schedule back. "Lucky you."

The oatmeal was thick and hot, with brown sugar melted into a crust on top. The eggs were good and the bacon was better and he ate both and then he ate her last piece of toast when she pushed it at him. At Saint Andre's, breakfast had been something you got through.

When the plates were empty, they went to class together.

---

The familiar class was at the back of the school. They followed a long hallway past closed doors and tall windows, and the further they went, the fewer other students there were around them.

"So where's your familiar?" Viridia asked. "I figured she'd be waiting at the table with a slice of my breakfast."

"She's got things to do today. I'll call her when I need her." Bal tried to remember how Amelia had put it, sitting on the couch with her feet up. "She thinks it'll make the best impression if everyone sees I can call an originator to me whenever I've got a problem. She knows I wouldn't, though. I prefer handling my own problems."

"I get that," Viridia said.

She let it be, and the hallway went on.

The classroom was the last door on the far right. Inside, on the far wall, was a second door, a heavy wooden one with an iron latch, and that one led outside. Some of them, Bal supposed, were bound to bring something large out of the forest, and it made sense to call it out there instead of explaining doorways to it.

The room itself wasn't much. Twenty desks and chairs stood in four rows of five in front of a chalkboard. Down the left-hand wall were two tall windows, and through them was the forest, close and green and dark under the trees. Someone had written MR. PORTER on the chalkboard in large, careful capitals.

They were two of the first students in, and they took two desks together in the back corner, under the second window.

Mr. Porter was already at his own desk at the front. He was feeding cheese to a raven.

It was a big bird, bigger than any raven Bal had seen sitting on a gutter, and it stood on the corner of the desk with its feathers sleeked down and its head tilted, watching the teacher's fingers. Mr. Porter broke a yellow cube off a block wrapped in wax paper. The raven took it from him, neatly, and lifted its beak to the ceiling to swallow it. Then it tilted its head again and waited for the next one.

The others came in by ones and twos. Nearly all of them looked at Bal as they came past, at the blood sygl on his sweater and then at his face. A few gave his corner a wide berth so they would not have to walk by his desk.

When the last chair was filled, Mr. Porter wiped his fingers on a handkerchief, folded the wax paper over the rest of the cheese, and stood.

He was a man in his fifties, with gray hair that had gone thin across the top and a brown tweed jacket buttoned over his stomach. His glasses caught the light from the windows whenever he turned his head, and while they did, you could not see his eyes at all. He walked to the front of the chalkboard and looked out at them.

"Why do you need a familiar?" Mr. Porter asked.

Nobody answered. Twenty students turned to one another in case somebody else had brought the answer.

"A familiar has many uses," he told them, since nobody had. "Not the least of which is companionship, which should not be discounted by any means. A familiar can help to carry the magical load of a difficult spell. It can carry messages. One could even save your life." He lifted a hand toward the corner of his desk. "Take Huginn here."

The raven heard its name and fluffed itself up.

"Huginn has been with me for close to fifteen years," the teacher went on. "In that time he has become a great friend. He has saved my life twice and helped me in numerous other ways. He also gives me a different perspective when I'm confronted with a problem." Huginn made a small, dry noise in his throat, agreeing or asking for cheese. "It is for this reason that none of you will be able to leave this class without a familiar of your own."

A boy in the front row put his hand straight up. Bal had heard someone in the hallway call him Tyler.

"We can't leave the class?" Tyler asked. "Like, ever?"

Mr. Porter shook his head.

"I'm sorry. I misspoke. You will not be able to leave the forest before you have bonded a familiar."

From the noise the class made, nobody thought that was much better. Mr. Porter did not give them time to say so. He went straight on into how a bond was made.

The incantation Bal already knew, and Mr. Porter spent hardly any time on it. Most of the lecture was about the part the household enchantments book had covered in two lines: what you offered the creature, and how to be sure it was enough for the bond to be balanced.

"There must be balance," he warned, and looked along the rows to make sure they had all heard it. "I cannot stress that enough."

When he was satisfied, he waved his hand.

A soft chime came from every desk at once. On each one, a silver ring had appeared.

Bal's was lying in the groove at the top of the desk where a pencil was supposed to go. It was plain silver, a little heavy for its size, and resting in his palm it gave off a faint, slow pulse, warm as a stone after the sun has gone off it. Around the room people were turning their rings in the light and talking at once.

Mr. Porter cleared his throat. The talking stopped.

"Those are bonding rings," he said. "Every so often they release a pulse of magic, and a creature may respond to it. That does not mean it will be willing to form a bond. It will only make it curious." He let that sit. "Be as safe as you possibly can. Do not try to approach any creature you are not equipped to deal with." The glasses flashed as he turned to take in the back of the room. "Students have died in there."

Nobody talked after that.

Bal weighed the ring in his palm. Then he laid it in the pencil groove where it had come from, and raised his hand.

Mr. Porter's eyes narrowed behind the glasses.

"Yes, Mr. Norheart. You have a question?"

"What are we supposed to do if we already have a familiar?" Bal asked.

He didn't check what the teacher's face was doing. Out of the corner of his eye was Viridia, with her chin on her folded hands, waiting, enjoying herself enormously.

Mr. Porter went red. It started above his collar and climbed.

"All students were ordered not to bond a familiar before attending this class, Mr. Norheart," he said. "I take it you think the rules do not apply to you. I assure you that is not the case." He came out from behind the front row and stood in the aisle. "But since you feel you have such a grasp on things that you do not need my instruction, why don't you show us your familiar? Is it a rodent, perhaps? Or a toad?"

Bal couldn't help it. He laughed.

It was a short one, and by the time he had it back under control Mr. Porter had gone from red to purple.

"Well, come on, then!" he snapped. "We don't have all day. Show us your mastery."

The whole room had turned around in their seats. Twenty faces, and Huginn's.

Bal raised his palm to the ceiling and pushed his magyk through the sygl.

The magic left him and ran out along the thread, a long way, to wherever she was.

A second later Amelia was sitting on his desk.

She sat cross-legged in the middle of it, in the black dress, spine straight, hands loose on her knees. The brand showed clear on her forehead, red and plain as anything. The silver ring lay in its groove, an inch from her knee. Her eyes on his were wide and bright and perfectly innocent.

"You called, Master?"

Beside him Viridia made a sound like a kettle and doubled over her desk.

It wasn't Amelia who had set her off. It was Mr. Porter, and no wonder. All the purple had drained out of the man at once, and nothing had come in to replace it. He was the color of the chalk.

"Lady Amelia," he stammered.

Chairs scraped everywhere. Students were up out of their seats and backing away, and the ones in the back two rows did not stop until they had their shoulders to the wall. Tyler was crouched behind his own chair. Up front, Huginn opened his beak, shut it again, and stood perfectly still.

"What are you doing here?" Mr. Porter asked.

Amelia's head came round toward him, slowly. By the time it had finished coming round, the innocence was gone from her face. What remained was calm and cold, and every bit of it was pointed at Mr. Porter.

"Are you implying," she asked softly, "that I need permission from you to go anywhere I wish?"

Mr. Porter was sweating now. From the back row Bal could see it shining at his hairline. His hands came up, both of them, and waved in front of him.

"No, no," he said. "No. I was only curious what led you to grace us with your presence, that's all."

"This is the familiar class," Amelia said. "Is it not?"

"It is."

"Then that is why I am here." She tipped her hand toward Bal without taking her eyes off the teacher. "My Master called for me, so I came."

Bal was doing his best to keep a straight face, and losing.

"But you're," he began. "And he's a..."

He stopped. Bal watched him go looking for the end of that sentence and find nothing anywhere that would not get him killed.

Amelia slid off the desk.

She strolled toward him up the aisle between the desks, in no hurry at all. The students in her path found other places to be. She came to a stop an arm's length from Mr. Porter, close enough that he could have touched her, if he had wanted to, which he did not. He had to tip his head down to look at her. It did not help.

"Think your next words through," Amelia said, "very carefully."

Mr. Porter's mouth opened and stayed open, wordless. It was a good choice.

Bal decided he had better step in before someone got hurt. By someone, he meant Mr. Porter. By hurt, he meant killed.

"It's okay, Amelia," he said, and he left off the Lady on purpose. "I'm sure he didn't mean anything by it. Head on back to your errands, and I'll meet you in the dorm later."

It was like watching someone flip a switch. Amelia spun around to face him, and the cold was gone from her. She was beaming.

"Okay, Master!"

She gave him a little wave over the heads of the class, and then the aisle where she had been standing was empty.

Once she was gone, everyone in the room breathed out, except the two in the back corner. Bit by bit the class came back to their desks and sat, and they sent Bal the kind of looks you might send a dog that had turned out to be a bear. Viridia had her face back. Her mouth kept trying to get away from her at the corners.

This would be all over the castle by dinner.

Mr. Porter did not sit down. He stood at the front with one hand on the edge of his desk and gave them a few last instructions. He stumbled over most of them and said two of them twice. Then he went to the door at the side of the room and lifted the iron latch and pulled it open on the forest.

"Don't come back," he told them, "until you've bonded something."

They filed out past him. Viridia stopped by Bal's chair on her way.

"See you in Warfare," she whispered, and followed the others into the trees.

Mr. Porter said something under his breath to Huginn. The raven dropped off the desk, flapped twice, and skimmed low over the students' heads after the class to keep an eye on them. Mr. Porter shut the door.

With only the two of them left, the room went very quiet. Mr. Porter stayed by the door a while before he turned around.

"Ah," he said. "Um. Seeing as you already have a familiar, I could excuse you from this class, if you wish, Mr. Norheart." His hand went to the knot of his tie. "You are welcome to stay, of course," he added quickly.

"I'll stay," Bal said, "if you don't mind."

It was plain from Mr. Porter's face that he did mind, a great deal.

"After today, at least," Bal said. "Could I go to the library now, if that's okay?"

Mr. Porter assured him that was more than okay.

Bal stood. The silver ring was still in the groove of his desk, where Amelia's knee had been. Somebody in this class might need a spare. He left it lying there and took the hallway door.

---

The library was upstairs, as promised: straight up the main stairs, a right at the top, and then the second door on the left.

Partly he was curious what books a school this old kept. But he also had a plan. Last night, on the couch, Amelia had talked about still wanting revenge on the others for what they had done to her. Somewhere between that and the bell he had made up his mind to look into ways he could help. Something besides converting followers.

He could have asked her. She would have told him anything he wanted to know. He wanted to do the reading himself first, and come to her with an idea or two of his own.

The library held thousands of books. The shelves went up two stories to a narrow iron gallery, with ladders on rails to reach it, and the whole room smelled of paper and warm dust. Bal stopped inside the door and turned a slow circle.

There was a librarian at a desk near the front, a woman with a pencil behind each ear. Thankfully she was the helpful sort. He told her what he was after: the originators, and what laws governed how they dealt with their own followers, and with each other's followers. She went off into the stacks without a word and returned with an armful.

Bal read for the next hour at a table under a window.

Most of it was about wars. The longest account was of a war between Naisura and Kenmei. It went on for chapters, battle after battle, and in the end Naisura won it with one stroke. One of his high priests caught Kenmei's command tent in stone, sealed it in whole, and sank it underground. After that Kenmei's army fell apart, and the rest of it was wiped out soon after.

Bal read that part twice.

He thought of a temple behind an orphanage that nobody could get into, built by another of Naisura's high priests. He turned the page.

He looked for Amelia next. There was hardly anything. A line or two, and then nothing, page after page. It looked to Bal as though the others had tried their best to leave her alone, and he could guess why. She had never kept to their rules.

From what he could tell from the books, the rest of them did. And the rules were not kind ones. The originators looked at their followers as pieces on a game board, to be moved up and traded off and lost. Everyone's life could be spent except their own.

Bal had always looked up to the originators.

Some people were born with magyk, he knew that, and not all of them got sygls. He had grown up thinking the ones who did were chosen. That each of those marks was a piece of an originator, given on purpose, by someone who wanted the world to be better and had picked that person to help. He had wanted one so badly. He had sat on a roof and pushed magic through a stump for years on the hope of it.

Now here it was in their own histories, in plain print, and it matched what Amelia had said at the cottage about eight high priests outside a temple. The oatmeal sat heavy in his stomach.

He shut the book.

The clock on the wall said it was nearly time for his next class. Bal carried the stack back to the front desk and asked the librarian if she could set aside any other books she thought he might like.

She promised she would, and made a note on a card, and took the pencil from behind her left ear to do it.

---

Before Rune working he made a detour, down to the dorm. The wall let him in. In the bedroom he opened the chest and dug his rune engraving kit out of the bottom of his pack, still wrapped in its dish towel. He left the towel in the chest and tucked the flat wooden case under his arm.

The rune working class was taught by a short, skinny man named Mr. Winters, who wrote his name on the board and then the reason for the class underneath it. The point, he told them, was to make them familiar with all sorts of runes, what each one was for, and how to use them properly.

Bal undid the brass catch on his kit and laid the case open on the desk beside his paper.

He spent that first class copying runes off the chalkboard. The ones he knew from Fredrick's books he skipped. The rest he copied stroke by stroke, slowly, getting each angle where it was supposed to be. Among them was a narrow, hooked rune Mr. Winters called the avoidance rune.

Mr. Winters came down the rows while they worked. At Bal's desk he stopped and bent over the paper.

"That's precise," he said, and tapped the avoidance rune with one finger. "Very good. That's an essential one to know if you're ever traveling at night in unfriendly territory."

He moved on. Bal studied the rune a while longer, the hook of it and the clean line through the middle, and then he copied the next one: a squat purification rune, for turning whatever liquid it touched into plain water.

He was almost glad when the bell went for lunch.

Almost, because he had liked every minute of the runes. He closed the kit, fastened the catch, and carried it out with him, and all down the hallways to the dining hall he wondered whether Viridia would be at lunch, or whether she was still somewhere out in that forest, holding up a silver ring and waiting for something to come. He remembered Mr. Porter's warning about the forest, and walked faster.
