# Chapter Fourteen: The Card

"They put the dead in the walls," Lora was saying. "Not in a graveyard. In the walls of their own houses. So your grandmother was always right there behind the plaster while you ate your supper."

"That's horrible," Viridia said.

"It's not, though. Not to them. The book says they'd knock on the wall before a big decision. To ask."

"Did anybody ever knock back?"

Lora started to say no, and stopped, and considered it, and the sun had gone another finger lower over the trees before she had finished telling Viridia every reason the answer was complicated.

Bal kept a few steps back and let them get on with it.

She was talking from the book she was partway through back at school, not the new brown one riding in his pack. It was about a people who had been gone so long that nobody agreed on what they had called themselves. Lora knew where they had built their roads and what they had eaten and how they had counted their years, and she was telling all of it to the back of Viridia's head and the side of Viridia's face.

Bal would have bet his four new histories that Viridia couldn't have told an aqueduct from a well. She asked anyway. She asked who had dug the wells, and whether the dead in the walls had minded the damp, and once she stopped dead in the road to argue that anybody who built a city on a floodplain deserved what they got. Lora argued back. Nobody was being polite.

Bal remembered the boys in Lora's town, the tournament, the comments after. Somebody had looked at this girl and decided she was a thing to laugh at. Now she was walking backward in the road ahead of him to keep Viridia's face in view while she explained about the floods, and he decided that whoever they were, they were not getting another turn.

Lora had reached the part with the earthquake. Viridia asked which came first, the earthquake or the floods, and Lora said that was the right question, and Viridia was so busy being right that she walked into a puddle.

The last mile was uphill. Bal's feet hurt and the meat rolls were miles behind him and nobody said much on that stretch. Near the top Lora remembered one more thing about the earthquake, and they were off again, clear to the castle gate and in under it and up the stairs.

It was nearly two hours before they came down the long gallery to the gray pedestal where it ended.

"Watch this," Bal said.

He set his palm on the carving. The little prick came, and the stone drank, and his blood threaded through the grooves till every line of the carving ran red, then sank out of sight. Lora bent so close to watch it that her glasses nearly touched the stone. Viridia wrinkled her nose at the whole business, and then the wall ground aside, and she went up the steps first anyway.

The room upstairs had changed while they were out. There were two couches around the fireplace now, the old red one and a second one facing it, a darker red, with a second low table in front of it.

Amelia had the old couch to herself, a book open on her knees. It was a small book, bound in blue, with a name inked neatly on the cover, and Bal had last seen it the day before, held over her face on the High Priest's bed.

She looked up at the sound of the wall. Her gaze slid past Bal to Viridia, and her face jumped.

The book was gone. Her knees were bare of it, and her hands were empty, and she was off the couch and across the rug and had her arms around Bal's neck before he had got the strap of his pack off one shoulder. She smelled of the fire. She hugged him hard and let him go and went straight back to her place.

"Well, you've all been gone all day," she said. "Did you have fun?"

Bal decided the diary was a conversation for later, with fewer of its authors in the room.

Behind him the girls had not moved off the top step. Lora was half behind Viridia. Viridia got hold of her by the wrist and towed her in off the step.

"Amelia, this is Lora," she said. "Lora Aegis. Lora, Amelia."

Amelia raised her eyebrows at Bal, a question with no words in it. Bal shrugged at her, sheepishly, and that was enough for her to laugh.

"Well, you already know me," she said to Lora. "How are you liking it, being around Bal?"

Lora said, "Oh," and nothing after it.

All the talk from the road was gone out of her. She stood on the rug with her hands pressed flat to the sides of her trousers, plainly at a loss whether she was supposed to bow, or kneel, or what a person did with a goddess who had asked her a friendly question.

---

Bal sat down beside Amelia.

"Look," he said, to both the girls. "I'm about to tell you something. But it has to stay between us for now. Okay?"

They gave him the same puzzled look, the pair of them, and said okay.

Amelia drew a breath to speak. Bal raised two fingers off his knee, and she let the breath go. He laid his hand over hers instead and kept it there on the couch between them.

"Amelia's family betrayed her," he said. "They thought she did something she didn't do. That's why she was locked away for close to three thousand years. When I bonded her as my familiar, it was a kind of loophole. It let her get out. I've offered to release her from the bond since, and she doesn't want me to."

Lora had ended up on the edge of the new couch. Viridia stayed standing behind her.

"Her life hasn't been easy," Bal went on. "None of ours have, really. But because she's an originator, it's hard for her to make friends who aren't only there because they want something from her. So please. Treat her like you would anyone else. Let her get to know you." He turned to Amelia. "I know it's something you want and have no idea how to ask for. I'm right, aren't I?"

Amelia had closed her eyes.

One tear got out and hung on her lashes, red in the firelight, and Bal brushed it away with the side of his thumb before it could fall. His other hand stayed on hers.

"She was alone all that time," he said. "She needs friends. So do I."

Lora's mouth opened. Then it closed. Her fingers were knotted in her lap, and she was working at something, and Bal waited, and let her have all the time she needed.

"I need friends too," she said at last.

She said it to her knees, so softly it nearly went into the fire.

---

Bal eased the pack off onto the rug and opened it.

The histories came out first, the four of them, and went in a stack on the new table. He fished out Viridia's cloth bag and passed it up to her where she stood, and found Lora's brown book with the broken column on it and put it in Lora's lap. The folded job paper could stay put. That left one thing in the bottom of the bag, and he let it wait.

"And you know what friends do?" he asked Lora.

Lora waited.

Bal grinned. "They give gifts?"

He lifted out the last book, the green one with the brass corners, and offered it to her.

Lora's hands came up for it before the rest of her had caught on. It was heavy enough that it dragged her wrists down into her lap, on top of the brown one.

"But when did you..." she began, and stopped, because her own book was right there underneath it and this was not her book.

She opened the cover. She turned pages, quickly, then more slowly, then quickly again, checking it was alchemy to the last page. Her eyes had gone bright behind her glasses. Nothing spilled. Then she closed it and hugged it flat against her chest with both arms.

"Thank you," she said.

She watched him over its brass corners, in case he had anything else in there.

Bal dug in his right pocket.

"Viridia. Give me your hand," he said. "The left one. And close your eyes."

Viridia rounded the couch with a look that promised trouble if this was a trick, and dropped down by Lora, and put out her hand, and did as she was told.

The ring went onto her middle finger.

"Oooh," said Lora, very softly.

Viridia stared down at the plain bright band on her hand, speechless.

"I know it doesn't look like much," Bal said quickly. It did not. It could have been any bit of silver bought for a few coins. "But it should be plenty powerful." The card propped beside it in the window, he explained, had promised it could move the earth, briefly, and warned that it cost a lot to recharge. "So you'll want to save it. For when it counts."

"It's perfect," she said. "Thank you."

She tilted the ring to the fire, so the light ran around it, and went on admiring it, and was not listening to anybody.

---

That left Amelia.

She was sitting very straight at her own end of the cushions, her hands clasped hard on her knees. Bal had seen her nervous only once before, in the doorway at Verin's cottage, working up to a question she was afraid to ask.

"Amelia. I'm sorry I didn't buy you anything."

Something went across her face, quick and sad, and was gone. He nearly missed it.

"But I hope you like it anyway," he said.

Now she only looked confused.

From the other pocket he drew out the card. It was warm from his hand. He had worn the corner smooth on the road, without meaning to, and he gave it to her face up, so that what she saw first was the pale wood and *Sygls Best* in white across it.

"Turn it over," he said quietly.

Amelia flipped the card.

Then she went still. The fire went on popping. Lora breathed. Viridia's ring caught the light and lost it. Amelia held the card, and nothing in her stirred, not her lashes and not her breath, as if the hearth had always had a statue of a girl beside it and nobody had noticed until now. Under her thumbs the white letters lay in the grain. *Amelia Norheart.*

Then her eyes closed. She tipped sideways against him, slowly, and her shoulders started to shake.

She did not make much noise. She cried with the card held tight to her chest in both fists and her face pressed into his shirt, and Bal put his arms around her and held on.

"You belong here," he whispered into her hair, by her ear. "With us."

Across the hearth both girls were staring. Neither of them could have seen what was written on the card. Viridia's hand with the ring on it hung in the air halfway to her lap. They knew enough.

Bal rubbed her back while she cried, until the shaking eased and then stopped.

She drew back. Then she kissed him, very softly, on the mouth.

"You are a good man," she told him.

She settled in again beside him, and then scooted closer, until there was no couch left between them.

"I'm sorry about your shirt," she said.

"My shirt?"

Bal looked down.

It was the Saint Andre's shirt, the one washed so thin the elbows let daylight through. All down the front of it, where her face had been, it was soaked through dark red, a great wet patch of it over his heart. Anyone who came in now would have taken him for stabbed.

"It was worth it," Bal said.

---

They stayed up talking until late. Viridia asked whether the second couch had been there that morning, and Amelia said the dorm had made it, and Viridia said the dorm had terrible taste, and Amelia agreed with her. Lora found the chapter on poultices and read the first line of it out loud, and then the second, and then had to be stopped. Nobody brought up the card. Amelia stayed tucked against Bal's side through all of it, and now and then her hand went to the pocket of her dress, where she had put it, and rested there.

Then the curfew bell went, loud enough to be heard through every wall in the castle, and the girls had to go.

Bal walked them down the steps. At the bottom he hugged Lora, and then Viridia, and both of them thanked him again, Lora into his collar and Viridia against his ear, and he kept the red stain on his front clear of both of them. He stood and watched them go along the painted corridor together, Viridia with her ringed hand held out in front of her and Lora carrying her two books, until they turned the corner. Then the stone ground shut.

Upstairs the room was empty. The fire had burned down. The histories were stacked on the new table, and the pack lay open on the rug with the job paper still folded in the bottom of it.

He went through to the bedroom.

Amelia was already there, sitting up against the pillows with her knees drawn up. The card was on the nightstand on her side, propped against the lamp with the name facing her, and his note from that morning folded beside it.

"Come on." She patted the bed beside her. "It's time for bed."

Bal pulled the ruined shirt off over his head. For the second time that day he was down to his boxers, and now his own face went pink. He climbed in beside his beautiful Vampire Goddess, and she pulled the blanket up over them both.
