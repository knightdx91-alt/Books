# Chapter Eighteen: Six Weeks

Over the next six weeks they took a job almost every Saturday, and some weeks they found a reason to take one on Sunday too.

The money hardly mattered. They needed the road. The castle had gone sour on them by degrees, and by the second week Bal could feel it before he was through the doors at breakfast. Conversations stalled when he came near and started up again behind him. A younger student he had never spoken to stepped off the stairs to let him by, as if Bal might be catching. Twice in the corridor near the library, someone said something loud enough to carry and low enough to deny. Both times it was the same advice. He ought to stay with his own kind.

His own kind, as far as the castle went, was Bal. There was a dorm full of it.

Lora and Viridia heard worse than he did, he suspected, and told him less. Viridia came to dinner one night with her mouth in a flat line and kept silent through the whole meal. When Lora asked her what was wrong, she said "Nothing," in a voice that shut the subject, and Lora let it stay shut.

The classes, at least, went well.

In Warfare, Zoran stopped behind him one afternoon while Bal was working through his forms. Bal waited for the correction. He had learned to brace for it.

"You've come on fast, Norheart," Zoran said. "Good work."

He moved along the line. Bal was left with his rapier pointed at nothing until Lora coughed at him from across the sand.

It was Lora who did most of the work, though. Ever since the road to Whitehaven she had taken his training as her own personal job. She wanted him able to protect himself, and she wanted it even harder than he did. Three mornings a week she was waiting at the practice yard before breakfast with her own rapier and her hair already pinned up. She drilled him on footwork until he could do it along a single crack in the flagstones without stepping off. She drilled him on keeping his point up until he dreamed about it. When he finally got through a whole exchange with her without a touch landing on him, she told him it had been adequate, and was pleased with herself all through breakfast.

---

The thing he was proudest of happened in Crafting.

For weeks he had been working runes into whatever he made in there. He carved them into the handles of wooden spoons. He engraved them on hinges, on a brass box with a stiff lid, on a set of buttons that never did anything at all. Most of it failed. Some of it worked for an hour and then quit. He kept at it anyway, because he could not stop thinking about the vendor in the New Spring square, up on his crate with a longsword raised, and the fire running up the steel.

He forged the dagger himself. It took most of two classes to get the edge right and nearly all of a third to engrave it. The runes ran along the flat of the blade in a single line, small, close, and as precise as he could make them. He went over every stroke twice.

It was a Tuesday, or it might have been a Thursday.

He held the dagger above the stone bench, away from everything that could burn, and pushed his magyk into the grip.

Nothing happened. Then a thread of flame woke at the guard and crept up the blade. It was blue at the bottom and yellow at the top, no taller than a finger, and it barely reached the point before it gave out. Nobody at the next bench even raised a head from their filing.

It was a weak version of the vendor's sword. The vendor would have been embarrassed for him.

Bal kept the warm dagger across his knees until the bell rang, and he would not have traded it for that sword or for any other blade on the red cloth.

That evening he lit it for the others in the dorm, over the hearth to be safe. The flame climbed its finger's height and died. Lora asked to see the runes and read them off the blade rune by rune, under her breath, checking each against her notes. Amelia held it by the guard and turned it to the firelight, and said it was lovely work, and Bal believed her.

---

The arrow shirts took him longer. He bought the materials in New Spring and worked the runes in over several nights. Then he charged each rune himself, a few each evening until his hand ached, because a rune would only turn one arrow. After that the girls wore them under their clothes on every job. Viridia's was green.

---

The lake was near the end of the six weeks.

They found it coming home from a job, in a fold of the hills where none of them had thought to look. It was long and narrow and very still, with reeds along one shore and a slope of pale rock along the other. The water was so clear that the bottom showed, dropping away from the rocks into blue, and then into nothing.

They stopped to drink and fill the skins. Hopper went out until the water reached her chest.

The water behind her rose.

It rose in a long smooth hump with no splash at all, as if the lake were standing up, and then it broke, and there was a head under it. The head was as big as a rowboat. It was green-black and wet, with a long mouth that went nearly back to where its neck should have been. The neck kept coming. Bal had his rapier out, and he knew while he drew it that it was useless. Lora had hers out too. They were standing on a rock with a thin sword each, looking up at something that could have swallowed them both without bothering to chew.

Hopper was out of the water before Bal saw her move.

The snake turned its head after her. Its body ran away behind it under the surface for a distance Bal did not want to guess at.

Viridia stepped past him to the edge of the rock and lifted her right hand.

The lightning left her palm with a crack that hit Bal in the teeth. It struck the lake beside the snake's neck, and the whole lake lit from inside, every reed, every stone on the bottom, the snake's full length laid out in it like a road. The smell came after, sharp and hot.

The snake sank, silent. Some time later it floated up again, slowly, belly first, and stayed up.

Viridia lowered her hand.

"Well. I won't be filling my water skin here."

Amelia, who had not moved from the bank, applauded.

Until that day Bal hadn't seen Viridia use her own power.

---

The war was a different matter.

The stone Thad had given him lived in Bal's pocket, and over those weeks it glowed red again and again. Each time, Amelia took him to the hidden temple so he could tap it against Thad's and hear the news. The news was bad every time.

Raizen had found his strategy. He had released a statement through his High Priest, in his own territory. It said that Raizen and the others had shut Amelia away because she had started to go mad, and had attacked them, and they had been left with no choice. It went on. Since Raizen had been the one to suggest it, it said, now that she was free, it would be his followers who were in danger.

That one statement did a lot of work. Raizen's army had always been going to follow orders. Now it was afraid of a mad vampire coming after it, and it trained harder for being afraid. And the villages, which had never liked anyone who bore the blood sygl, had stopped at not liking. Blood sygls who would not leave their villages were being beaten. Some had been killed.

Thad told him all of it plainly, standing in the same narrow room each time with the same dark circles under his eyes, a little deeper at every visit. Then he waited. He clearly wanted orders. He wanted to hear how they could help.

Amelia could not get involved. So Bal went to the commander of the army.

---

The commander's name was Fern. He was short and stocky, with a long gray beard and intelligent gray eyes, and he carried himself in a way that made his room feel like his before you were properly through the door. A long table filled the middle of it. A desk in one corner was buried in paper, and a lamp had been left burning there in the middle of the day. On the table lay a map of Raizen's territory, weighted at the corners with stones.

"There's not much we can do," Fern said. "It's in Raizen's territory. We could send the army in, but it would be pointless."

He said it bluntly and did not dress it up.

Bal stood over the map. The wood around its edges was very dark and very old, and his fingers began to drum on it while he read. The map showed where each of Raizen's troops was posted. Amelia had found them all. It had no mark on it anywhere for a village.

"The problem is," Bal said, "even if we could evacuate them, we don't have anywhere to put them."

"No," Fern said. "We don't."

The death toll was growing every day. Bal had made his peace with the fact that people were going to die in this war. It had taken him a while to get there, but he had. That did not mean he was going to stand by.

"Could we do that to them?" Fern was thinking it through out loud. "See how they like it?"

"We will not respond in kind."

Fern met Bal's eyes and left the idea where it lay.

"Do we know where any of their priests are?" Bal asked.

"We do. Raizen has only three regular priests, and one High Priest. The High Priest hardly ever leaves his palace in Pashar." Fern set a thick finger on Pashar, then moved it. "The others travel. But the regular priests get together once a month, at an inn in Londra."

Bal was startled. "How did you find that out?"

"Spies, of course." Fern sounded faintly surprised to be asked. "We've got a team with no sygls. They pose as laborers. A man with nothing on his palm can walk into any town he likes, and nobody gives him a second look."

Fern's people had learned the priests met monthly to share news and gossip, and to drink, Bal suspected.

"Are your men still in Raizen's territory?"

"No. They've only been back a few days, though. They wanted more instructions." Fern frowned into his beard. "They hate seeing what's happening over there."

Bal's fingers had stopped. He measured the distance from Pashar to Londra with his spread hand, and then measured it again.

"Send them back," he said. "Learn how often the priests meet with the High Priest." With the priests gone, the High Priest would be easier to deal with. "Do we know if the High Priest has any special inheritance gift?"

"Wait here," Fern said.

He went to his desk, picked up a stack of papers and thumbed through it, licking his thumb every few pages. Halfway down he found the one he wanted. He left the rest on the desk and returned with it.

"His name is Toma," Fern read. "Fifty-six. No known family, but several concubines that he keeps at his palace. He's considered a moderately powerful mage. No inheritance gift."

The map had not changed while Fern was gone.

Aren's territory bordered Raizen's. Amelia had assured him Aren would not step in. That much he could put out of his mind.

Toma was another matter. Bal couldn't work out why Raizen would make someone his High Priest with no gift at all. Granted, Bal didn't have one either, as far as he knew. But his case was different.

He knew a fair amount about Raizen by now, from the books. Raizen did not like a direct fight, and avoided one whenever he could. He sent his troops out in squads to strike and then pull back, strike and pull back, over and over, wearing the enemy down. That was partly how Naisura had beaten him in the last war. Naisura's army had marched straight at him and kept coming, and never gave Raizen's squads the time to retreat or regroup.

Bal stood over the little marks that were Raizen's squads until Fern said his name.

---

He had asked Amelia once how they would even know if they had won. Short of killing Raizen's whole army, he had meant.

She had been at the foot of his bed, propped on her elbows. She explained it as though it were a game she had seen other people play for ages.

Once war was formally declared, neither originator could make another High Priest. Once an originator's priests and High Priest were dead, the war was over. Technically, anyway. The victor could go on killing the other's army if they wanted. It did not matter, at that point.

The part about the army had stayed with him longer. The war could be over and men could still be killed in it, simply because the winner felt like going on. He had known straight away that he would never be that kind of winner.

And once the war was finished, the victor could demand something from the originator who had lost.

"Have you decided what you'd demand?" Bal had asked. "If we won?"

Amelia had smiled at him. The smile came slowly.

"Oh, yes. They are bound to give me what I ask, so long as it does not physically harm them." She had paused there. "And I know exactly what I shall demand."

He had spent most of an hour at it. He had tried asking straight out, and guessing, and pretending not to care. All she would say was that if he wanted the answer, he would have to win.

---

Now, with Fern's orders given, Bal was in his dorm with a pad of paper on his knee, making notes on what he might do about Raizen's High Priest. Most of them he crossed out. Toma had no gift of his own, which ought to make him easier, and kept making him harder, because Bal still had no idea why Raizen had chosen him. The priests met in Londra once a month. Pashar was far from Londra. He wrote that down twice without meaning to, and crossed one out. The history book he had been using for ideas was open beside him on the cushion.

The girls were curled up on the plush couches with their rune homework, comfortable as cats. Now and then Lora leaned over to check one of Viridia's lines, and Viridia let her. Hopper, shrunk to fit the room, lay across both their feet and twitched in a dream.

Amelia was on the rug by the hearth with her feet folded under her skirt. She hadn't spoken since dinner. She was staring into the fire as if there were something in it she was reading, and Bal, who had been glancing up at her between notes, saw her lips part.
