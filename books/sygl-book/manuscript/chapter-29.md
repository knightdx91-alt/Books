# Chapter Twenty-Nine: Blood Elemental

The roof vanished from under his feet, and sand was there instead.

It was hot on top and cool a finger's depth down, coarse as salt, and Bal's toes sank into it. Light poured over everything. All around him, tier on tier, stone benches climbed from the sand in rows, hundreds of rows, up to a ring of high arches where the sky began. It was a coliseum, as grand as the old Roman one had ever been, and every seat in it was empty. A breeze spilled down the bowl from the rim and stirred the sand around his ankles.

Toma waited at the center of it.

Magnificent was the only word for him. His robes were blue, a deep, rich blue that fell clear to the sand, and lightning bolts were sewn all over them in silver thread, so that whenever he shifted they caught the sun and jumped. Fifty-six, Fern's file had said. He wore his years proudly.

Behind him, a wide circle had been drawn on the arena floor and ringed with runes. Inside it stood Lora and Viridia.

Bal's feet were moving on their own. Two steps. Three.

Toma held up a hand, and Bal halted.

"They are fine," Toma said. "They have not been harmed."

Bal searched the girls over, every inch of them in view. Viridia was hammering on nothing and shouting. The cords stood out in her neck with the shouting. Not a sound of it reached him. Lora was beside her, both palms on the air at the circle's edge, and her lips were moving, slow and careful, shaping each word for him. Where the circle ended, the light bent a little, with a faint blue shimmer to it.

"They cannot hear you," Toma said. "And their magyk will not affect anything outside the circle."

Amelia was at Bal's shoulder. "It's a lesser version of what they did to me," she whispered.

Bal's teeth clamped together until his jaw ached. Thirty paces of sand, that was all. He could have walked it in the time it took to breathe twice, and they might as well have still been in Pashar.

Toma smiled at whatever he saw in Bal's face.

"Yes," he said. "Plainly you care for them."

He let that stand. His voice, when it came again, had dropped, and some of the polish had worn off it.

"As I cared for my fellow priests." He stopped. "But at least yours are still alive."

Bal had braced for a speech and gotten something else. Three of Raizen's priests had taken their seats at a lunch in Londra, and a day later none of them rose again, and this man had known them. Maybe for years. Bal felt no regret over those three. But the grief in Toma's voice was real, and Bal didn't pretend to himself otherwise.

Toma drew himself up, and the priest returned to cover the man.

"I assure you," he said, "if I win, I will take great pleasure in taking their lives. Now. As mage duels go, among high priests, there can be no interference. No familiars may be called. This fight will decide the fate of the war."

"If we're having this fight to decide the war," Bal said, "then killing Lora and Viridia after I'm dead would be an act of war. With no priests left to act as intermediary, Amelia would be free to do whatever she wanted." He did not give Toma room to answer. "And even if you left one of them alive, the survivor could ask their originator to intercede on their behalf."

Toma's mouth soured.

"Agreed," he said, short and quick.

Toma hadn't expected Bal to know the rules. Bal had Amelia to thank for it. Deep in the second night on her roof, both of them sweating, she had made him sit and walked him through each rule till he had them by heart. So whatever happened now, both girls would live. For Bal, that was enough.

With the terms agreed, Toma crossed to one end of the arena, and Bal walked toward the other. Amelia came with him partway.

"Good luck," she said, with a quick smile.

She bounded to the stands in a single leap, her long skirt flaring out around her, and landed on a bench six rows up, soundlessly. She tucked her bare feet beneath her skirt, ready for the show.

Bal walked on by himself.

The sand got hotter toward his end of the arena, where the sun had been lying on it longest, and each grain worked in between his toes. Under the stands at this end there was a low dark archway with an iron gate across it, rusted shut, and it breathed out a smell of old stone and something animal, long gone.

He stopped and faced around.

From here Toma was small, a blue mark at the other end with the sun glinting off his sleeves. The circle was off to Bal's left, between him and Toma and a little off the line, and he could just make out Viridia's hair, standing out from her head in a pale blue cloud.

Amelia stayed where she was, high in the stands.

---

This was Bal's first mage duel.

Before Amelia's blood, everything he knew about them came from books he had read since he started at school. Some were histories and some were manuals. They disagreed about almost everything. On one point every single book agreed.

Always make the first move.

"Kona Niente!" Bal yelled.

The sand at his feet heaved up and flew at Toma in a great boiling cloud of dirt. Under cover of it his fingers were already at the bandolier. He worked two vials loose and flung them wide, hard and low, so they would land to the left and right of where Toma stood.

From the middle of the cloud came Toma's voice, perfectly calm.

"Czak."

The cloud fell. All of it, at once, every grain, and it pattered down in a ring around Toma's feet, where it settled. There was not a speck on his robes.

Toma raised an eyebrow.

"Is that it?" he asked. "That's all you've got?" He brushed at one sleeve, though not a grain clung to it. "Well, no matter. It's my turn."

Two days ago, he'd have seen a man standing in the sun. Now, with Amelia's blood in him, he saw it come. It came in from everywhere, from the air above the bowl and down out of the sky, thin bright threads of something he had no name for, and all of it drew in toward Toma and bunched around his hands until the light there was too thick to see through.

Toma threw back his head and cried out. It was a loud, raw, primal sound, and it seemed to tear itself out of his throat on its own.

The bolt struck the sand between them with Bal still flat-footed.

The blast picked him up and threw him. He hit the sand, and rolled, and hit again, and slid. There was sand in his mouth and sand in his ears and a ringing over everything.

He got his head up.

The storm elemental filled his whole sight.

It stood where the bolt had struck, taller than three men, a crackling mass of white and blue that was shaped something like a man and something like a thunderhead and would not hold either shape for long. Light ran over it and through it without stopping. The fine hairs along his forearms rose and prickled. He pushed himself to his feet, and once he was there he could not do anything but stare.

This was no mere elemental.

*No inheritance gift,* Fern had read out of the file. The file had been wrong.

Toma was enjoying this, and made no secret of it.

"I see you are beginning to understand the gravity of your situation," he said. "The rules clearly state no familiars. But my inheritance gift is calling a life-bonded elemental, so it is not, technically, a familiar. Which is enough to satisfy the rules." His smile went ugly at the edges. "Now. It's time for Akora to attack."

The elemental surged forward.

Bal scrambled backward and yelled a quick incantation, and a short wall of sand rose from the arena floor between them. He only needed time. A little distance.

Akora hit the wall and went straight through it.

Where the lightning touched the sand, the sand turned to glass, and the glass burst. Shards blew out in every direction, spinning and glittering. Bal twisted away and felt them go into him, a hot sting across his back and both arms, then a dozen more.

Somewhere behind the elemental, Toma was laughing.

Bal ran. Akora threw lightning at him, bolt on bolt, and he flung himself clear, dropped, rolled, came up running. The sand slid under his bare feet. Flickering whips of electric light lashed out at him from the thing's body, cracking past on his right, and he dodged left to get clear of them.

His foot came down wrong. The sand gave.

He crashed to the ground.

He was still scrabbling for his knees when the hulking storm elemental loomed over him.

Bal lay under it, and he knew. He was about to die.

He found the circle. The girls were together at the edge of it, and they had the same fear on both their faces.

He mouthed it so they could see. *I'm sorry.*

---

Akora began to turn.

It spun slowly, then faster, light whirling around a hollow at its middle, and the pull of it took hold of Bal and dragged him over the sand and off it. He dug in his heels. He grabbed at the ground. The force of it was too strong to fight, and he had to let it take him.

There was blood on his back. The glass had seen to that. He called it, and it lifted off his skin and lashed out in whips of red, and he struck at the elemental with them, lash after lash. It was like whipping a river. The blood passed clean through the light, and the light did not care.

Then he was inside.

The maelstrom closed over him. He was in the heart of the elemental's twisting body, borne around and around in a roar of light, and the glass was in there with him. All the shards from the wall had been caught up in the spin, thousands of them, and they whirled with him and through him and past him and back. Each one cut. They cut his arms and his legs and his back and his face, and they kept on cutting, shredding him. There was no taking this. Bal could only be in it.

And through it, with Amelia's blood in his eyes, he saw the core.

It hung in the center of everything, a knot of light harder and brighter than anything around it, turning very slowly while all the storm went racing around it.

His strength was going, draining out of him in red. The blood was everywhere around him, flung off him by the spin, a fine red mist thinning in the light.

He called it back. All of it.

He pulled it home to him out of the storm, every drop that had leaked out of every cut, and he wrapped it around the core, and he pressed. He pushed it in. He forced it into the creature with all the strength he had, every drop he could spare. When he could spare no more, he called on the two vials he had thrown at the start, and their blood rose from the sand and crossed the arena to him, and he forced that in too.

It lasted past all counting.

A crack split the arena, a huge sound, bouncing off every tier of stone at once. The elemental shuddered all through.

It dropped him.

He fell. He struck the sand on his side and stayed down, and somewhere far away somebody was screaming, a high, terrible, endless scream. To Bal it seemed to come from another country.

Something landed on the sand before him, softly.

Slowly, he lifted his eyes. Where Akora had been, a shape stood over him, as tall as before, still turning, though no light was left in it. No lightning anywhere. It was blood. A great slow column of swirling blood, deep red, folding endlessly on itself without a drop spilling onto the sand, and it waited before him, as if summoned.

A blood elemental. Bal had no other name for it.

There was also no such thing. They did not exist. They never had.

Bal blinked up at it. He didn't understand any of it.

The scream finally got through to him. It was Toma.

Bal rolled onto his side and tried to stand, and his arms folded under him. He tried again. After a few more tries he got as far as his knees, and then, swaying, he was up, with the arena tipping under him.

Toma was down on the sand, rocking, his head clutched in his hands.

Bal made his way over. It was slow going. The thing of blood came along close at his back, and he could hear it moving, a low wet rushing like a creek running under ice.

By then, Toma's screams had worn down into crying, little whimpering sounds of pain. The old man lifted his face. Tears were running down it, and the tears were red.

"What have you done to me?" Toma moaned.

Bal was at a loss. What *had* he done? He checked behind himself. The blood creature hung there, turning. He faced Toma again, and Toma rocked.

---

Amelia flashed into being beside him, her bare feet on the sand at his elbow, and a heartbeat behind her came Raizen, with Aren after him.

Raizen was furious. He flung out an arm, the orange sleeve of his monk's clothing swinging, and leveled a finger straight at Bal.

"Do you see?" he demanded of Aren. "This is exactly why we locked Amelia away! Her followers are too dangerous!"

Aren's long white beard stirred in the breeze, and Toma's huddled shape put a deep trouble in his old face.

Toma lowered his hands from his face.

Then Bal understood in a rush why the originators had come. On Toma's palm was a sygl, and it was the Blood.

When Bal converted Akora, he had converted the High Priest as well. Elemental and priest were life-bonded. Whatever entered the one entered the other.

The idea knocked the air out of Bal. It reached Aren and Raizen too. It was there in their faces, the same thought in both. If he could convert a High Priest, then no one was safe if Bal chose to convert them.

Amelia wasn't troubled in the least. On the contrary, she was enjoying herself enormously. And why not? Technically, the war was over. Raizen did not have a High Priest anymore.

"Raizen has a point," Aren said at last. "Your last High Priest converting people was the source of our conflict. Now this one appears able to convert the unwilling. Can you not understand why we would find that troubling?"

"I understand why it would trouble you, yes. And yet you want me to play your games, and then you get upset when they don't go your way. Bal never meant to convert Raizen's High Priest. He acted on instinct, against Toma's elemental. It was only because Toma and his elemental shared a life bond that he converted the High Priest along with it. You cannot penalize my High Priest for protecting himself."

The gods could argue without him. He was having enough trouble staying upright. The breeze off the rim found every cut on him and stung it fresh, and blood ran down the backs of his legs in slow threads and dripped off his heels into the sand, where it sank and left little dark coins. Behind him the swirling thing kept up its low wet rushing, patient as a cart horse.

A fly had found Toma. It walked across the old man's knuckles, and Toma, who had stopped rocking, made no move to brush it off.

Aren weighed her words. Then he put his question to Bal.

"Now that you are aware of your ability, what are your intentions?" Aren asked.

Bal found the circle again. Both girls were still inside it, pressed against the shimmering blue barrier, as close to him as it would let them come.

"I just want to live my life with my girls," he said. "I'll protect them with everything I have. If that means removing a piece from your armies, then I will. But it isn't something I'm setting out to do."

"So you do not plan to use your newfound ability?"

"Not if I don't have to. If somebody comes asking me to convert them, I can compromise. I'll have Amelia let you know before I do it. That's as far as I'll go."

Amelia agreed at once, and Aren accepted it.

"That's it?" Raizen burst out. "She just gets away with taking my High Priest?"

Aren sighed and faced him.

"Under the terms that were set, she has done nothing wrong," he said. "She has not interfered, and the war was fought by humans alone. I declare Amelia the winner."

Raizen shook. Bal had taken that for a figure of speech until now. The god shook all over with the force of his rage, from head to foot.

Aren's fingers cracked together, one dry click.

The girls vanished, and the circle was empty sand. Toma was gone with them.

Bal lunged forward. Amelia stopped him.

"He only sent them home to the school," she whispered. "They're okay."

He let her hold him back.

Aren frowned at him from under white brows. From his face, he was not used to mortals forgetting themselves in his presence.

"Now then." Aren smoothed his beard and turned to Amelia. "As the victor, it is your right to demand anything of the defeated, barring his life. Have you made your decision?"

Amelia's mouth curved, slowly.

"Oh, yes," she said.

Raizen heard what was in her voice. So did Bal, the cruel excitement just under the words. The rage drained from Raizen's face, and fear came in to take its place.

"You will confine yourself in the same temple I was locked away in," Amelia said. "There you will remain, for three thousand years, with no contact with the outside world. You will serve as a warning to anyone else who desires to come after me or mine."

She said the last part straight at Aren.

The two of them held each other's eyes.

"Very well," Aren said.

His fingers snapped again, and Raizen's protest went with him before it was a word.

Aren lifted his face to the sky.

"You know the others will not like this," Aren said, "but I assume that's one reason you did it. I dare say it is not something they will forget. At least they will think twice about engaging you again. I must return."

To Bal he sounded mild and old and very tired.

"Try not to start another war," he said.

He was gone, and bare sand lay where his sandals had been.

---

Bal remained in the sun, the swirling thing turning at his back, and the tension left him by degrees. His shoulders first. Then his hands.

Amelia was shining, up on her bare toes in the sand, and seeing it, Bal knew. It was over. It was really over.

She rushed him. She caught him up in a hug that crushed his bones together, and each of the thousand cuts on him should have screamed at that, and none of them bothered him. He buried his face in her hair and breathed her in.

At last she stepped back.

"I'm so proud of you! I knew you could do it."

"At least somebody did," Bal said. "I'm still not sure how I won."

He had not forgotten the thing behind him. He faced it.

"This is the part I don't understand," he said, and pointed at it.

Amelia circled the column of swirling blood, slowly, studying it. It turned for her without spilling.

"Honestly, I don't either," she said. "I've never heard of anything like this happening. Plus the fact that you broke the bond between it and the High Priest."

She snapped her fingers at it, a bright, decided little crack, and the blood elemental was gone.

Bal didn't have to ask.

"I sent it to my domain," she said. "I'll study it when I get the chance."

Then she stepped forward and caught his hand, and the coliseum went away around them, all the empty seats and the hot sand and the scuffed place where Toma had knelt.

The warmth reached his bare skin first, and the smell of woodsmoke, ahead of any sight at all. Somebody at home already had the fire roaring.
