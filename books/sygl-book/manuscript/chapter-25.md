# Chapter Twenty-Five: The Three Boars

Lora left it sealed. She crossed to the bed and sent it spinning onto the blankets over Viridia's knees.

Viridia had not moved since the knock. She was propped against the headboard with her legs under the covers and her hair squashed flat where the pillow had been. Bal climbed back in beside her. His side of the sheet was still warm.

"What is it?"

Viridia turned the envelope over, and he felt her go rigid against his arm.

"What's wrong?"

The seal cracked across Raizen's mark and came away in two pieces.

"Only one kind of person is allowed to seal a letter with Raizen's Tempus sygl," she said. "A priest of Raizen."

From his pillow he could make out a few lines in a narrow, slanting hand and a lot of empty paper under them. She went through it fast, with a frown that deepened as she went.

"They know I'm here. Not as one of Amelia's. As someone with Raizen's inheritance gift. They'd like to meet me."

The cask sat on the table opposite the bed, untouched since last night. Viridia's attention went to it and did not leave.

"Well. It's just as well I bought them a gift."

Lora had her arms wrapped around herself, though the room was already warm. "I don't like it. But it was always a possibility. They were bound to hear about you, and if they heard, they'd want to meet you. Honestly, it'd be stranger if they hadn't asked." She unwrapped her arms. "It does get us in the door. That's the one good thing. We don't have to find an excuse."

"Lunch," Viridia said. "Today. The Three Boars."

Bal climbed out of bed and went looking for his other boot.

---

Bal packed everything they owned. Lora's chest was the heaviest, the cauldron scrubbed and wrapped in a shirt and wedged in on top of the jars, and Bal carried it down the creaking stairs first, then went back for the rest. If the day went as planned, they would ride out of Londra straight from the priests' table. Nobody was coming back to the Winespring Inn.

He had the horses saddled before either girl came down. He held Viridia's while she mounted, skirts and all, then Lora's. He strapped the cask behind his own saddle, where it rode against his spine like a passenger, and swung up onto the black.

Viridia led. Bal fell in behind Lora, a servant's length back, and watched the city go by.

It was another hot morning. A woman was beating a rug over a balcony rail, and the dust drifted down onto everyone passing under it, and nobody complained to her about it. The crowds parted for Viridia's hair the same as before.

The doors of the Three Boars were shut.

That was new. Yesterday they had stood wide open, with laughter spilling into the street. Today the building offered only its plain front and white shutters, and not a sound.

The girls dismounted out front. Bal took their reins and led all three horses around to the stable built onto the side.

---

A stable boy came at a run, the same boy who had elbowed his friend and pointed. He had a cowlick standing straight up at the back of his head and a stalk of straw stuck to his shirt.

"I can take that, sir," he said, already reaching.

He walked Viridia's horse to a stall with enormous care, then hurried back for Lora's and the black. Bal let the boy stall all three before digging in his purse.

"We'll only be a short time," he said, and put the coins in the boy's palm.

The coins were silver. He gaped.

"Yes, sir!" He nearly shouted it, and Lora's horse put its ears back.

Bal laughed. The boy's face was too much. "Make sure they're watered. And give them some oats before we go."

"Yes, sir." Quieter. The coins had already vanished somewhere inside the shirt.

Bal unbuckled the straps and lifted the cask from the black's rump. It was heavier than it had been last night, or else he was tired. Then he headed around to the front.

---

Mrs. Locklan had come to meet them at a trot. The man behind the bar of the Three Boars never left it.

Long-faced and gray at the temples, he was polishing a glass that did not need it. He took in Bal's coat and then the cask in his arms, and, silent, he lifted the hand with the rag in it and pointed.

The taproom was enormous. Most of the tables stood empty, chairs pushed in, and the floor had been swept and was still damp in places. At the far corner three men in azure robes were gathered at a table with cups in front of them. Viridia was already there, in a chair that had plainly been dragged over specially. Close by, Lora had a table to herself and a small cup of water, and was taking sips.

As Bal came in, the nearest priest beckoned, two quick flicks of the fingers.

"Come now," he said. "We've been waiting for you."

He was stout, with a red neck rising out of his collar, and his fingers were drumming the table.

"Calm yourself, Titus. We have all day."

That was Aleric. Bal knew him by the scar before he knew him by the voice: a long white seam that ran from his brow down across his right eye and onto his cheek. The eye under it was clouded. The other was dark and quick and missed nothing, from what Bal could tell.

The third had to be Leon. He was the youngest of them by ten years at least, with soft hands and a wispy attempt at a beard, and he leaned in to study the cask as Bal set it on the table.

"I've never had Ventish brandy," he said. "I'd be most curious to try it. And you said the whole cask is for us?"

"Certainly." Viridia smiled at him. "It's a small token, to show how much we appreciate what the priesthood does for us. Even now, with this dreadful war going on."

Aleric inclined his head to her, and the scar showed whiter than ever in the window light.

"We do appreciate it, my lady," Aleric said. "Come. Share a drink with us. Let us celebrate Raizen's glory."

Viridia waved the offer off with the back of her hand. "Oh, I couldn't. I had the cask brought just for you. Surely you'd enjoy it more than I would. I seldom take spirits."

Aleric's good eye narrowed.

"I insist."

He drained what was in his cup. Then, to Bal at the table's end:

"Get her a cup."

---

Viridia's face did not change. Her hands did. They had been resting on the table, and now they went still and pale on the wood, the knuckles standing out. The pulse was going in her throat, as plain as the scar on Aleric's face.

A table away, Lora had heard every word. Her cup was halfway to her mouth. She had gone white to the lips.

There was no antidote. Lora had read them that part of the book too.

Viridia lifted her eyes to Bal. Whatever had been in them a moment ago was gone, and what was there now was something set and hard, and he understood it. She had already decided. If drinking with them was what it took to kill these priests, she would drink, and she would die of it a day from now along with them.

She let her lashes fall once, slowly, the signal a lady gives her servant, and turned her smile back on Aleric.

Bal went for the cup.

He kept it slow on purpose, one boot and then the other across the long damp floor, because every step bought him time to think. He could attack them. Turn around right now and go for them with everything he had. He could probably take one of them before they cut him down. One. And then Lora and Viridia would still die, here or in the street outside, and it would all have been for nothing.

No. Something. Something here.

He reached the bar. The long-faced man set down his glass.

"A small cup," Bal said. "For my lady."

The man went through a door at the back. Bal waited at the bar and looked along the shelves without taking in any of it. Rows of glass bottles, green and brown and clear. A clock somewhere ticking. His mind kept running in circles, out the door, back to the table, out again, and finding nothing at either end.

One of the bottles had a mark on it.

It was a tall clear bottle second from the top, nearly at the end of its shelf, and something had been cut into the glass on its side, fine white lines in a careful pattern. Bal leaned across the bar to see it better.

"It's a cold rune." The barman was back, with a cup in his hand. The cup clicked onto the counter between them, and now there was something like warmth in his voice. "I bought it off a fellow last week. Keeps anything you put in it cold. Any liquid at all."

He went on saying something about the price. Bal had stopped listening.

Runes.

That was it.

He took the cup. It was turned wood, small and smooth, varnished dark. He lowered it below the edge of the bar, out of the barman's sight, out of everybody's sight, and held it upside down, left-handed.

His other palm, the right, split open by itself. A thread of blood slid from the split and drew itself together and hardened into a blade no longer than his little finger, thin and sharp and dark red.

He carved fast. The purification rune was one he had copied off Mr. Winters' chalkboard that first week, stroke by stroke, and his hand had not forgotten the order of them. The blood blade cut the wood as easily as soap, and the shavings curled away and dropped to the floorboards at his feet.

There might not be a cure for nightstalker poison. But a purification rune turned any liquid into pure water. Whatever went into this cup would be water before it ever reached her mouth, if he had cut it right.

The blade sank back into his palm, and the cut sealed. He hurried back to the table.

---

The priests were talking among themselves about a road somewhere, and whose fault the state of it was. Viridia sat quietly in the middle of them, her hands resting in her lap, out of view.

Bal set the cup in front of her and hoped to catch her eye. She had her face turned to Aleric.

"That will be all," Titus said, and waved him off.

So that was all. Bal stepped back to the wall, a few paces behind her chair, where a manservant could be called if he was wanted and ignored if he was not.

As the youngest, Leon got the job of opening the cask. He worked at the stopper two-fisted until it gave with a pop that went bouncing around the quiet room. Then he put his nose right down to the hole and breathed in so long and so deep that Titus snorted at him.

"Now that," Leon said, coming up pink, "smells divine."

He poured Viridia's first. Then Aleric's, then Titus's, and his own last, filling each to the brim with great care and spilling a little anyway.

The brandy went into Viridia's cup, dark gold, right up to the rim. It stayed dark gold. If the rune worked, it would work down there where nobody could see, in the moment before she drank. If it didn't, then he had cut some stroke wrong, crouched behind a bar with a blade made of his own blood, and he would not find out until tomorrow, when finding out would do nobody any good.

Aleric lifted his cup. "To Raizen."

"To Raizen," they said, and Viridia said it too, and all four drank.

She tipped the cup back and drained it to the bottom, and her face stayed a lady's face.

Aleric's good eye had been on her all through it, above his own cup. Then, as she lowered hers, something in the priest's face let go and smoothed out. He told Leon to fill the priests' cups again.

"So," he said. "You're on your way to Pashar?"

"I am." Viridia's hands had gone back into her lap. "I've heard there are some wonderful shops in the capital. I mean to do some shopping."

"There are many good shops there," Aleric agreed. "Our esteemed High Priest will want to meet you, of course." He smiled. "I assure you, word will reach him soon after you enter the city. As it reached us, when we arrived here. To find someone with so clear a sign of Raizen's favor is rare indeed. I could send a letter on ahead of you, if you like. Let him know you're coming."

Viridia hesitated, barely, and Aleric waited on it. Bal knew what she must be weighing. A lady with nothing to hide would be flattered. A lady who said no would give the man one more thing to wonder about.

"That would be very kind of you," she said.

---

They talked for about an hour.

Bal spent the whole of it by the wall, and his feet hurt, and the priests kept drinking.

Leon kept the cups full. Titus got louder with every one, and redder, until he was pounding the table when he laughed and the cups jumped. Leon started a story about a goat and could not finish it. Later he got up to find the privy and returned by way of every chair between. Even Aleric loosened, though less than the others. His courtly sentences began to wander and come home late.

Viridia's cup stayed where she had set it, empty. No one offered to fill it again. She asked about Pashar and listened to the answers chin high, a lady being gracious to her inferiors, and only Bal, from behind, could see how straight she held her back.

Lora, off at her own table, wet her lips with her water. Her cup never got any emptier.

After their fourth cup, Viridia told them she ought to be going.

Aleric waved her on with a loose hand. "I'll send the letter tomorrow."

Viridia thanked him, gave them each the gracious farewell Lora had drilled into her on the road, and then beckoned the two of them after her.

None of the priests got up. Titus was refilling his own cup.

---

The door swung shut behind them. Viridia sagged back against it.

Her face crumpled. Tears ran straight down it, and her knees gave, and Bal caught her by the arms before she hit the step.

"I had to do it." She was shaking so hard it went through his hands. "I had to. It was worth it."

"Viridia." The words came too fast, and he could not slow them. "You're okay. You aren't poisoned."

She stared at him.

"What're you talking about?" she said. "I drank the brandy."

Bal got her moving toward the stable. Lora hurried along at their heels.

"I put a purification rune on the bottom of your cup," he said. "I doubt that's what it's meant for. But it should have done the job."

Viridia stopped walking. She was going back over it, he could tell: her runes class, the cup in front of her, the first mouthful, how watery it had tasted. Then he saw it reach her face.

"You mean all I drank was water?"

She was coming back.

"Sorry I couldn't warn you. Getting the rune on the cup at all was hard enough, before I brought it over."

---

The stable boy had the horses watered and fed and waiting in the yard. He might have waited with them the whole hour. Bal boosted Viridia into the saddle, then Lora, then mounted his own.

They rode out of the yard. Lora brought her horse up alongside Viridia's and reached across and touched her arm, lightly, just above the wrist.

"I was afraid for you," Lora said. "It was a brave thing you did."

Viridia managed a wet little laugh by way of thanks. Then she gathered her reins.

"Now we just have to get clear of this place."

She dug her heels in. Her horse jumped forward into the street, and people scattered out of her way, a man with a barrow, two women with baskets. Bal rode in silence behind her, keeping up, and watched her bounce in the saddle the length of North Street. Lora had taught her plenty about being a lady in five days. Riding like one had not been among them.

Near the gate they eased back to a walk. Bal hunched in the saddle and studied his horse's ears. The same two guards were on duty, or two just like them. They let the three of them through without stopping them. Apparently they cared much more about who came into Londra than about who left it.

Then the gate was behind them, and the road ran out ahead into the long yellow country they had ridden in through.

They kept to a walk until the walls had dropped behind a rise.

Then Viridia turned in the saddle, and Bal heeled the black, and the girls' horses surged after his into a gallop. The horses stretched out. The road went pounding away under them, mile after mile of open grass with precious little cover between here and Amelia's border, and every stride put more ground between the three of them and the soon-to-be-dead priests of the Three Boars.
