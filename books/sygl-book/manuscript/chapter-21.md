# Chapter Twenty-One: Strawberries

Hopper would not go in.

Bal tried her from the shallows first. He waded into the lake up to his knees and slapped the water and called her, and she considered him from the warm rock at the edge. Then she considered the lake, from the near rocks all down its length to the far reeds, and lay down where she was and laid her muzzle across her paws.

"It's dead," Viridia told her, wading past with her elbows held high above the cold water. "I killed it. You were there."

Hopper's ears did not move.

A week ago there had been a snake in this water as big as a barn. Today there was a dragonfly, and a breeze puckering the surface out in the middle. Bal could not blame her. He gave up and went under.

The cold drove the breath out of him and then let it back in. He came up shouting, which Viridia thought was hilarious, and for a while after that he forgot about Hopper and everything else. They raced to a rock out in the shallows and argued about who had touched it first. Lora floated on her back with her glasses left safe on her towel, and drifted into Viridia, who splashed her. He dived for a stone Amelia dropped and surfaced with the wrong one, and she told him so.

When his fingers had gone wrinkled he climbed out and joined Hopper on the rock, still at her post. The stone was hot through his back. Hopper put her nose against his wet shoulder and resumed her watch on the water.

He kept her company.

It had taken a while for the girls to treat Amelia like any other girl. They had been careful around her for weeks. That was gone now. Lora ducked her without warning. Viridia climbed onto her back to be thrown off and was thrown off, and burst up spitting and demanding a rematch. The three of them shoved and shrieked and giggled as though Amelia were as human as they were, and Amelia shrieked loudest.

Her hair was plastered flat to her head and her face was wide open, and her smile made his heart hurt.

Three thousand years, and this was what she looked like underneath them. It was a sight to see.

The sun was hot on the rock. The girls' voices carried across the lake and back, and somewhere among them he stopped hearing the words.

---

He woke to snoring.

It was soft, a small catch at the top of every breath. He opened his eyes and Lora's sleeping face was inches from his own. She had left the water while he slept and come to share his rock, and her lashes were still wet. She had a dent in her nose where the glasses usually sat.

He closed the inch and kissed her forehead, lightly. She smiled for a second in her sleep and then forgot to.

Bal got an elbow under him to see where the others had gone.

Amelia was curled up against his left side, neat and small, her back to the sun. Viridia was stretched out above his head the long way, flat on her stomach, one arm flung out. Her right hand rested palm-up on the rock exactly where his head had been, reaching for it.

He bit down on the noise that wanted out of him and got clear of them without waking anybody.

The sun had crossed most of the sky. Hours, he guessed. The last time they were here, the day of the snake, he had seen a patch of strawberries on the edge of the woods, red under the leaves, and nobody had had time to stop.

He pulled on his shirt and pants. He caught Hopper's eye and pointed at the girls, and Hopper thumped her tail against the rock. He climbed the slope to the woods still buckling on his rapier.

The woods were not thick. The trunks stood well apart, and the light came down between them in long slanting bars, but in places the branches knitted overhead and the ground went dim and cool. Birds were singing somewhere above him and kept on singing while he walked under them. He went slowly. The brambles caught at the legs of his pants as he waded through the weeds toward a flash of red he thought he had seen.

It was the patch. The berries were small and dark and warm from the day, and there were far more of them than he remembered.

Bal knelt in the briars and began to pick. The first three went straight into his mouth, which was only fair.

---

A branch snapped behind him.

That sound saved his life.

He twisted on his knees and there was someone there, right there, a shape against the light with steel in its hand already coming down. Bal threw himself sideways. He rolled to his right through the thorns, and they tore at his arms and his face, and he was grabbing for the rapier at his belt as he went, and it would not come clear. Blood magic was out of the question. He couldn't have held one thought steady enough to call it. He burst from the briars and threw himself left, and the hilt finally came free in his hand.

Whoever it was, he was barely older than Bal.

He wore woodsman's clothing, greens and browns that would vanish among the trees. His hair was short and brown. His eyes were blue and very sharp. He twirled his sword once, right-handed, easily, as if he had all afternoon.

"Almost had you there," he said, conversationally.

He came in fast. He slashed to the right and then up. Bal let the sword slide along his rapier's blade and twisted his wrist to wrench it out of the other's grip, as Lora had drilled him all those mornings. His attacker pulled back before the move was finished, and laughed.

"You'll have to do better than that," he said. "How you ever managed to kill Heath with sword work that shoddy, I can't imagine."

Heath meant nothing to Bal.

They traded blows. The stranger was quick and loose and he never stopped talking. On the fourth or fifth exchange his blade went past Bal's guard and opened a long slice down Bal's left side, and the shirt there went dark and began to cling.

"Oh, that's got to hurt." He was grinning. "Why don't I see if I can even it up for you?"

It did not hurt yet. It would come later. He planted his boots.

They fought in earnest now. The thorns were at Bal's back and the light kept going in and out of his eyes. Steel rang and rang. Bal took cuts he only knew about from the sting, and gave none, and gave none, and then the woodsman overreached on a lunge and Bal was inside it. He opened the man's forearm. On the way back out he opened his ribs.

The grin went away.

He backed off a pace and touched the blood on his sleeve. When he spoke again his voice had changed.

"I was told to just kill you. But I think I'll enjoy myself afterward. Those pretty girls of yours." He waved his sword toward the lake, toward the sound of nothing, the girls asleep in the sun. "They're nearby, aren't they?"

Something in Bal went very still.

Lora, with the dent on her nose. Viridia's hand open on the rock.

"Told by who?" he asked. He held his voice low, where it could not shake.

"Why, my High Priest, of course." He seemed surprised that he had to say so. "He sent Heath to kill you, but Heath was past his prime. Now it's my turn."

He darted in again. He feinted right and swept low, and the blade bit deep into Bal's right thigh.

Bal felt it. He let it happen. He gave one step back, only one, and swung his left fist into the man's face as hard as he had ever hit anything.

The man had not been expecting that. His head rocked back and Bal kicked the sword free and hit him again. The man went scrambling after his weapon on hands and knees through the weeds, and Bal kicked him in the side, hard, and sent him into the tree behind him. He hit it with a sound like a dropped sack and slid to its foot.

The world got very quiet.

Bal could think now.

He turned the right palm out, and it parted down its middle with no more fuss than a mouth opening. The blood left it already moving. It went low across the clearing and curled once around the fallen man's neck and drew snug there. Bal brought it in slowly, hand over hand without using his hands, and the man came with it across the ground on his heels. Then Bal lifted.

The woodsman rose up the trunk of the tree until his boots were kicking a foot off the ground.

"Tell me," Bal said. "What's your name?"

The man clawed at his throat. His nails scraped and scraped on the blood and found nothing to get under. It had set as hard as steel around him.

"It's Iro," he said. "It's Iro."

"How many of you did he send? And how'd you track us here?"

Iro had stopped fighting it. All the chatter had drained from him, and his face had gone young and scared.

"Just me and Heath. I swear. The High Priest told us we were a long shot, so it didn't matter if we found you or not. Then one of the priests came to us the next day and said we'd be sent to wherever you were. They sent Heath first, because he had more experience. After you killed him, they decided to wait. Catch you off guard." It poured from him in a rush. "They only told me an hour ago that I was coming here. No idea how they knew. I swear that's all I know."

How they had known Heath was dead, at least, Bal could guess. A red-gold heart on Heath's right hand, back on the Whitehaven road. Somebody on Raizen's side had been wearing its pair, and had felt it stop.

Sweat was running down Iro's face into his collar. He had nothing left to hide behind. Bal knew he was telling the truth.

"Okay then."

He closed the loop with a thought.

Iro's body dropped to the leaves without its head, and lay still.

"No one threatens my girls," Bal told it.

The blood returned across the clearing. It sank into his palm, and the skin closed over it, and his hand was only his hand again.

---

His shirt was finished. Iro had seen to that. It hung off him in strips, and it was wet down one whole side.

Bal dragged it off. He tied the sleeves together and knotted the tail, and knelt down again in the patch and picked the rest of the strawberries into it. There was no reason to waste them. Then he picked up his rapier and walked back down through the trees with the bundle in his other arm.

The berries bled through the cloth before he was halfway down. By the edge of the woods his forearm was sticky to the elbow, and which of the red on it was strawberry was anybody's guess.

The girls were awake and talking when he reached the slope above the lake. Lora's glasses were back on. She turned at the sound of him, lifted her hand to wave, and stopped with it in the air.

She was up and running. Her long brown hair flew out behind her.

"What happened?" She was shouting it before she reached him. Her hands were already on him, on his side, on his chest, turning him by the arm. "What *happened*?"

"Would you believe I fell on my rapier?"

Viridia and Amelia came up behind her.

Viridia took the knotted shirt from him and set it on the ground, so they could all see him properly. There was the long cut down his left side that was still oozing. There was a scatter of smaller ones over his ribs and stomach he had not even felt go in. Viridia walked around him and found the one across his back, from his right shoulder to his left hip, and muttered something under her breath that Lora did not scold her for. There was the thigh. His pant leg was black with it to the knee. That was the one that was going to hurt most, now it had started hurting. The blade had gone through into the muscle.

Amelia got him down onto the grass.

Lora was in her pack. She bandaged him fast and tight, the side and the back and the worst of the small ones, and she kept the healing potion back for the thigh. When she poured it in, he hissed through his teeth, and Lora said "Good," fiercely, to the potion.

Amelia was facing the woods, and nothing friendly was left in her.

"It's no use," Bal said. "He's already dead."

"Explain," said Amelia.

He told them. He had wanted to surprise them with strawberries, and he had nearly lost his head over it. He told them about the snapped branch, and the man in the woodsman's clothes, and Heath, and the High Priest. He told them about the whip, and what Iro had said at the end of it. The girls let him get to the end.

"I just don't get how they knew we were here," he finished. "Heck, we didn't even know we were coming here."

Amelia studied him for a full minute. Then she was gone.

Nobody said anything.

She had done this before, gone off like that, unannounced. Bal hoped that this time she was going to get to the bottom of it.

---

Lora tied off the last bandage. She had used all the healing salve in the pot, and she held the empty pot upside down to show him, accusingly.

He untied the shirt. The strawberries had held up better than he had. He split them into piles on the grass, and made one pile for Amelia, on a flat stone where the ants would have to work for it.

They ate by the lake and talked and waited for her. Hopper had her chin on his good leg. Viridia wanted the whole fight again from the beginning, and he told it to her again, more slowly. She ate three of his strawberries while he did, without seeming to notice, and when he mentioned it she called them her fee for listening. Lora had no wish to hear it and listened anyway, and twice she retied the knot on his thigh bandage, which had been fine both times.

The strawberries were warm and a little squashed and they were good.

The shadows had gone long on the lake when Amelia came back. About forty minutes, Bal thought. She sat down cross-legged next to her pile of strawberries and began to eat them, singly, looking enormously pleased.

Bal gave her a minute.

"Did you find out anything?"

"Yep."

She popped a strawberry into her mouth.

"And?"

Amelia laughed. She laughed until she was bent over her knees with it, until bloody tears ran down both her cheeks and she had to wipe them off with the backs of her hands.

"I wish you could have seen it," she said.

She had gone to Raizen's domain first, she told them, because she had been certain he was behind it. Raizen was not home. So she went on to the usual meeting place.

"They had pulled the teachers in again today," she said. "And there was your Mr. Porter in the middle of them, explaining all about you converting Lora and Viridia. Raizen was furious. Positively purple." She ate another strawberry. "The moment I arrived, they all started on me at once. My High Priest stealing their followers was the reason they locked me away before, and here it was happening again."

"And you said?" Lora asked.

"That I had no part in what happened back then. And that I have given Bal my full permission to do whatever he thinks is right." Amelia licked a thumb. "Then I walked up to Raizen. He was fuming. I told him that if he wouldn't follow the rules everyone has agreed on for our wars, I would gladly take him on, right then, in front of all of them. All he had to do was say the word."

Viridia had stopped chewing.

"The others had no idea what I was talking about. Raizen did. It was all over his face." Amelia was enjoying this. "So Aren asked me to explain. I did. Aren turned around and asked Raizen if it was true. And Raizen didn't deny it."

She paused for the space of one strawberry.

"Aren told me I could have one concession from them. I said I wanted the new school rule lifted. Naisura and Raizen both opened their mouths to argue, and Aren put up one hand, and they shut them again." Her cheeks were still streaked. "Then he asked whether you meant to convert any more followers."

Bal waited.

"I told him if you did, it would only be people you cared about. Not some great converting campaign, like the last High Priest. Aren considered it, and agreed." She brightened all over again. "Mr. Porter stepped forward to object. And Aren snapped his fingers, and sent him back to the school. Gone, in the middle of a word. Then he told Raizen to keep his teachers in check."

"And then?" Viridia said.

"Then, as I turned to go, Aren wished me good luck." Amelia sounded amazed by it. "I think that surprised me more than anything."

---

"Would you really go after Raizen?" Viridia asked.

"Yes," Amelia said. "And they all know I would. That's what will keep him in line."

"So Raizen was the one telling them where I was," Bal said.

"Yep. And then teleporting them in close to you." Amelia brushed the green tops of the strawberries off her lap. "Once war is declared, the rules say the originators can't help their followers. Thankfully, we have a loophole. I'm also your familiar, so I can help with certain things without breaking any rules. I'm fairly sure the others haven't realized that yet."

When the last strawberry was gone, Amelia popped home to the dorm and returned with fresh clothes for him. Bal changed behind Hopper, who did not care, and stood up in a clean shirt over all of Lora's bandages.

Amelia took them to her temple next.

It was abuzz before their feet had properly landed. Men were racing through the hall with armfuls of spears. Someone was calling out names from a list. Half the army was getting ready to march somewhere, and nobody had told Bal where.
