# Chapter Five: Verin's Cottage

Pierre knocked on Bal's bed frame at six o'clock.

The dormitory windows had gone from black to a thin, watery grey, and all down both rows the other boys were lumps under their blankets. Pierre stood at the foot of the bed in pajama bottoms and a sweater, his hair flattened on one side. Pierre was another of the orphans, and some mornings he helped Mrs. Brown before the rest of the building woke up.

"Get up," Pierre whispered. "Mrs. Brown wants you." He yawned hugely in the middle of it and had to start over. "She says grab your things, then stop by the kitchen for a bite to eat, then go to her office. In that order."

"In that order?"

"She said that part too." Pierre squinted at Bal's left arm, lying on top of the blanket. Word of the hand had gone through the whole building by supper. He looked at it as though it might have gone back to normal overnight, then shuffled off down the aisle. "Kitchen, then office," he said over his shoulder. "Don't make me come back up."

Bal stayed put a little longer, flexing his fingers against the blanket. They were all still there. He got up.

He dressed without a sound and shouldered the pack he had buckled yesterday.

---

Somebody had left a plate of blueberry muffins on the counter under a dish towel. Amelia was already sitting on the counter next to them, swinging her heels against the cupboard doors, eating one in small, thoughtful bites.

"These are much better than supper was," she said.

"Don't tell the cook," Bal said.

"I wouldn't dream of it."

The muffin was a little stale and he ate all of it anyway. He was stuffing the last of it into his mouth when he and Amelia pushed open Mrs. Brown's office door.

Mrs. Brown sat behind her desk. She was not alone.

Sitting in one of the chairs in front of it was an older man in well-worn mage's robes. They were red, or they had been once. The color had faded to something nearer brick, but the cloth was clean and pressed and the buttons all matched. Seeing them come in, the man rose and offered his hand.

"Hello there," he said. "You must be Balthazaar and Amelia. My name is Verin, and I'll be escorting you to the Heir's School."

Bal swallowed the muffin in a hurry and shook. Verin had an easy smile and kind blue eyes set in a nest of wrinkles. He looked less like a mage than somebody's grandfather. He didn't stare at Bal's left hand, or at Amelia, though the brand on her forehead was showing plainly under her bangs.

Verin looked across the desk. "Sarah, I'll go ahead and set the portal up outside. The rest of the children are in their rooms?"

Bal blinked. In sixteen years it had not occurred to him that Mrs. Brown had a first name.

Mrs. Brown thanked him.

"Good." Verin faced Bal again. "I'll be waiting out front. Please don't take too long saying your goodbyes." He strode out past them, closing the door softly behind him.

Bal faced Mrs. Brown.

"I didn't think to ask yesterday," he said, "but how am I supposed to explain Amelia until everyone knows who she is? A familiar brand isn't something most people would expect to see on a person, is it?"

Amelia tapped him on the shoulder.

He turned. She was standing right behind him with her face perfectly innocent, and her forehead was bare. No sygl, no glow, no mark at all.

"Where did it go?" Bal asked.

Amelia laughed at him.

"Familiars can choose whether or not to show the brand," she said, "unless they're directly commanded to keep it visible or hidden. I'll just say I'm enrolling as well, until it comes out who I am."

"Besides," said Mrs. Brown, standing up and coming around the desk, "Verin knows not to ask too many questions. He's been at this for years."

She stopped in front of Bal. For a second he thought she was going to straighten his collar. Instead she put her arms around him and gave him a quick, hard hug. She smelled of soap and starch and, very faintly, of ink. Bal stood there with his arms halfway up, too surprised to finish the job, and then she had stepped back.

"You," she told Amelia, "keep him out of trouble."

"I promise," Amelia said solemnly.

She had both hands tucked out of sight at the small of her back. As they turned to go, she tipped them toward Bal where nobody else had a view. Two of her fingers were crossed, and she looked very pleased with herself.

---

The portal took up the whole main walkway in the front courtyard.

It was twelve feet wide, if Bal had to guess, and eight feet tall, a flat upright oval of air with a faint shimmer around its rim. The gravel lane showed around its edges, quite ordinary in the early light. Through the middle of it was somewhere else entirely.

On the far side a horse and buggy stood waiting on a dirt track in a dark forest. The trees over there were huge and close together, their trunks black, and the light between them was dim and blue, late-evening light, though on this side the sun was barely up. A lantern hung from a curved metal arm on the front of the buggy, already lit, its soft yellow light falling across the horse's back.

"We'll have to go the rest of the way by cart, I'm afraid," Verin said, ushering them through. Stepping across took a single stride, and then Bal's shoes were on packed dirt and the air was all pine needles and damp earth. "Portaling doesn't work in or near the school. Well." He handed Amelia onto the buggy, then Bal. "Not properly, anyway."

"What does that mean?" Bal asked, settling onto the bench beside Amelia and wedging his pack between his feet.

Verin climbed up into the driver's seat with a small grunt and gathered the reins. Behind them the portal folded in on itself with a soft pop, and the orphanage was gone.

"When the school was built," Verin said, "the originators helped. Each of them laid an enchantment over the place to keep it safe. One of them is a diverting enchantment. If anyone tries to portal straight in, they get shifted off to some other spot on this plane instead." He clucked to the horse, and the buggy lurched forward. "There's really no telling where you might end up."

"Doesn't that work on anyone?"

"The only exceptions are the originators themselves," Verin said. "The enchantment doesn't touch them."

---

The forest was deathly quiet.

Bal listened for birds for the first mile or so and heard none. There was the dull thud of hooves on packed dirt and the creak of the buggy wheels, and that was all. Verin hummed for a while and then stopped. The lantern swung on its arm, pushing its small circle of light along the track, and everyone sank into their own thoughts.

Going to this school had been all Bal had ever dreamed about. Granted, he had never pictured going with Amelia's sygl on his palm. Even that didn't seem like such a bad thing now. He'd always been a curious kid, the kind who hunted out secrets, and blood mages were the most secretive mages of all. They had never been allowed into any of the magykal schools, since Amelia had never been there to claim them, so nobody really knew what they could do.

He turned his new hand over in his lap and looked at the faint red glow at its center. He was going to be the one who found out.

Did they have kitchens, at the school? Real ones, with a cook who didn't burn things? There must be somebody who did the potatoes.

Bal glanced over at Amelia. She was gazing out at the dark trees, but he didn't think she was seeing them. There was a small line between her eyebrows. He figured she was thinking about tomorrow, and about the other four, and what they would do when they saw her.

He reached for her hand.

Amelia started and looked at him. Bal squeezed her fingers lightly and gave her a small smile.

She smiled back. Then she turned to look out at the forest again, and she didn't take her hand back.

They rode like that for ten miles or so, her cool hand resting in his warm one on the bench between them, until the trees opened up on the left and there was a cottage.

---

It was an old cottage, low and stone-walled, with one lit window. Out front a fire was burning in a pit ringed with stones, spitting sparks at the dark, though nobody was in sight to tend it.

Verin drew the buggy up beside it and climbed down, stiffly, and motioned for them to follow.

"This is my home," he said.

He began unhooking the horse from the buggy. Freed, it shook itself from nose to tail, walked over to a wooden trough by the corner of the cottage and started to drink.

"Normally I'd try to push on to the school," Verin said, "but with how late it's gotten, we'd all better rest and head out in the morning. I've got both your school uniforms inside. You'll need to change into them before we leave tomorrow." He gestured at Bal's chest, where a sygl would have been on a student's sweater. "Once you're officially claimed, your sygl will appear on your clothes, so don't worry about it not being there before. There's stew in the pot inside. Make yourselves comfortable while I see to old Thistle here."

He went off toward the trough. Thistle lifted a dripping muzzle and blew at him affectionately.

Bal and Amelia went inside.

The cottage was one room, and well kept: a table with a single chair, a narrow bed under a patchwork quilt, a shelf of dishes. In the corner a fire burned in a stone hearth under a large black pot, and the smell hit Bal the moment he stepped inside, savory and peppery. His stomach reminded him that breakfast had been one blueberry muffin, hours ago.

On the floor near the fire lay two bedrolls, side by side. On top of each was a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. Their uniforms, Bal guessed.

He sat down by the nearer one and started working at the knot. Then he glanced up.

Amelia hadn't moved from the doorway. She hadn't taken a single step into the room. She was biting her bottom lip.

Bal frowned. He had seen her laugh and tease and turn into a cloud of bats. Nervous was new.

"Are you okay?" he asked, setting the parcel aside.

Amelia crossed the room and knelt on the bedroll in front of him.

"I am," she said. "But there's something I need to ask you."

Bal waited. In the hearth a log shifted and settled.

"Can I feed from you?" she asked softly. "I haven't had human blood in a very long time, and I need it to be at my full strength. I'm worried I won't be able to stop the others if they try something tomorrow without it."

Bal sat back on his heels.

"If it's something you need, then of course you can," he said. "You're my familiar. And hopefully my friend. Why wouldn't I help you?"

Amelia peeked up at him through her black bangs.

"It doesn't freak you out?" she asked. "I mean, I am a vampire."

Bal shrugged.

"Nah," he said, and grinned at her. "Just makes you more interesting."

Amelia grinned back at him, shyly, a little crookedly, and then she leaned in and gave him a quick hug. It was over almost before his arms caught up. He hugged her back anyway, a beat late, and then she had already let him go and was sitting back on her own bedroll pulling at the string on her parcel and saying nothing about it.

Soon she was talking. She talked while she unwrapped a dark sweater with a blank place on the front and measured it against herself to check the length. She told him how the originators had come up with the idea for the schools, and why they had wanted more than one: so that magic would have room to grow.

It was information Bal would never have gotten anywhere else, so he filed it away. Most of his attention, though, was on her.

Ten minutes ago she had been biting her lip by the door. Now her eyes were shining and the words were tumbling out of her, all because she was going to show him a school she had helped build. Bal had never had a girlfriend at the orphanage. He'd kissed a few girls, but nothing more serious than that. Watching Amelia now, he thought this was what he wanted in a girl. Well, maybe not the vampire part; he hadn't expected that. But definitely all the rest.

Amelia broke off halfway through a sentence about the library and looked at him.

"What?" she asked. "What are you looking at?"

"You," Bal said.

Amelia blushed. Bal hadn't known she could.

---

Verin came in shortly after, smelling of horse and woodsmoke, and found them both sitting on their bedrolls with bowls of stew in their laps. He ladled himself a bowl, sat down in the one chair at his table, and between spoonfuls began to explain what tomorrow would be like.

"I'll drop you off at the school gates," he said, "so you can walk in with the other students. In the inner courtyard there'll be a raised stage. All the first years assemble in front of it. Once everyone's gathered, the originators will start to arrive on the stage."

He blew on a spoonful of stew.

"As each student is claimed," Verin went on, "you'll walk around the stage and form a line behind your originator. Once everyone's been claimed, each originator leads their students to their dorms." He pointed his spoon at them in turn. "This may well be the only time you ever see them in person. So if you've any questions for them, that's when you ask."

Bal glanced sideways at Amelia and grinned. She had her lips pressed tightly together and was staring very hard into her stew, and her shoulders were shaking.

Verin didn't notice. He was chasing a piece of carrot around his bowl.

"You'll get your schedule from the Lead Mage in your dorm," he said, "but all first years take the same classes. It's only the times that are different. They mix all the different sygls together in the classes so you can get to know other mages. It's meant to help you make friends with people from the other sygls. They know that once you leave school, a lot of people tend to stick with their own." He scraped up the last of the stew and set his empty bowl on the table. "That's about it. Anything else, you'll have to figure out on your own."

He yawned, and stretched until his shoulders cracked.

"Better get some sleep," he said. "Morning comes early."

He threw a couple more logs onto the fire, pulled off his boots, and crawled into the narrow bed under the patchwork quilt. Before long he was snoring softly.

---

Bal collected Amelia's bowl along with his own, picked Verin's off the table and went outside to wash them in the horse trough. Amelia came after him.

The fire in the yard had burned down to a red glow. Thistle was grazing at the edge of the clearing, and only raised his head a little when they came out of the house before going back to his supper. Bal dipped the first bowl into the trough. The water was cold enough to make his fingers ache.

While he rinsed the bowls, Amelia stood in the middle of the yard with her head tipped all the way back, staring straight up.

Bal looked too. With the fire low, the sky over the clearing was thick with stars, more than he had ever seen from the roof at Saint Andre's, running together in places into pale smudges like spilled flour across a dark table.

"It's been so long since I was out here," Amelia said softly. "I didn't remember it being this beautiful."

Bal shook the water off the last bowl.

"How long were you in that place?" he asked.

"Almost three thousand." She was still looking up. "A long three thousand years."

There was a wooden bench against the side of the cottage, under the lit window. Bal set the bowls on the edge of the trough to dry and motioned to it, and they both sat down.

"They won't try to take you away again tomorrow," Bal said, "will they?"

Amelia shook her head.

"No," she said. "Honestly, them trapping me in the first place was mostly my own stupidity. That, and the fact that I trusted them."

"What happened?"

Amelia leaned back against the stone wall of the cottage and let her eyes fall shut.

"They found a really talented stone crafter," she said. "A high priest of Naisura's, actually. They had him build that temple for me. A peace offering, they said, because they were sorry for ever thinking I had anything to do with their missing followers." Her voice was quiet and very even. "Immortals like us can only be trapped inside one of our own places of power. A temple, for instance. I hardly ever go into one, and they knew that. So when Naisura's high priest built that temple for me, they knew I'd want to go and thank him. And I went."

An owl called once, somewhere far off in the trees. Neither of them looked toward it.

"What I didn't know," Amelia said, "was that they had their other high priests hidden outside. Every one of them gave their lives to shut me in. It took the lives of eight high priests to imprison me for all that time."

She didn't open her eyes. A single tear slid out from under her lashes and ran slowly down her pale cheek, and in the window's light Bal saw that it was blood.

He wiped it away with his thumb.

It left a faint smear on her cheek, and a smaller one on his thumb. He didn't wipe his thumb on anything.

"They chose their path," Bal said. "Now you're free. I can release you from the bond, if you want. I know being bonded to a mage who hasn't had a single day of training probably isn't what you expected."

Amelia turned her head against the wall and looked at him. She was smiling.

"Thank you for offering," she said. "That you would offer at all means a lot. More than you know." She reached up and touched the smear on her cheek with one fingertip. "But I'd like to stay. Who else is going to teach you blood magic?"

And she stuck her tongue out at him.

Bal laughed so loudly that Thistle lifted his head again to see what the fuss was about. Then he remembered what she had asked him inside, by the fire, and the laugh settled into something steadier.

"Okay," he said. "Well, if you're my familiar, then it's my job to look after you. How do you want to feed?"

At the word, Amelia's eyes brightened in the dark. She took his left hand, the one her blood had grown, in both of hers and drew it up toward her mouth. Then she stopped, his wrist an inch from her lips, and looked at him over it.

"Are you sure?" she asked.

"I'm sure."

Slowly, Amelia bit into his wrist.

Bal barely felt it. His pain tolerance had always been unusually high, and her fangs going in were a quick, sharp pressure, there and then gone. After that he felt only the cool of her hands holding his arm steady, and her hair brushing his knuckles, and the small, steady sound of her drinking.

He listened to it for a bit. Then he started talking.

He told her how excited he was to be headed for school at all. Without a left hand his whole life, he had more or less given up on school. He'd figured he would end up a regular laborer somewhere, lugging things and fixing things, until he could teach himself enough magic to be useful some other way. He kept talking, and Amelia kept drinking, her eyes half closed, until at last she lifted her head.

She licked the two small puncture marks on his wrist, once.

Bal watched, astonished, as the holes drew shut on their own. Within a breath the skin was smooth and whole again, and below it, in his palm, the sygl's faint red glow went on as before.

Seeing his face, Amelia laughed.

"It wouldn't have looked good," she said, "to show up for school with two holes in your wrist."

"No," Bal agreed. "I guess it wouldn't."

He stood up from the bench and offered her his hand. Amelia took it. Her fingers were warmer than they had been in the buggy, almost as warm as his own.

Behind them the bowls dripped on the edge of the trough, and out in the dark Thistle tore up another mouthful of grass. Tomorrow there would be gates, and a courtyard, and a stage, and four gods standing on it who expected her place to be empty again.

Bal and Amelia went inside to bed, eager for the new day.
