# Chapter Twenty-Eight: The Chalice

"Water."

He croaked it. His tongue was glued in place, and the word had to tear itself loose.

He was lying in the High Priest's bed. He knew it first by the curtains, deep red with gold worked through them, tied back on their posts, and then by the soft weight of a quilt that was too good for anybody who had come in off a battlefield. Amelia sat cross-legged on the covers beside him, near enough for her knee to touch his arm. Her face was pinched with worry. It cleared the instant he spoke.

She was up and gone from the covers before he got the word out twice. A pitcher stood on the little bedside table, and she filled a cup from it, slopping some on the wood, and was back beside him on her knees with the cup at his mouth before the spill had finished spreading. Her other palm cradled his skull to keep it off the pillow. Bal drank. The water was warm from sitting and he drank it all anyway, and some ran down his chin into the pillow.

He let his head go back down and shut his eyes.

"How long was I out?"

"Two days," Amelia said softly.

Two days.

Two more days of Lora and Viridia in the hands of people who wanted him dead. Two more days of them waking up somewhere strange to him, and going to sleep there. He lay and let that settle on him. Lying there made it no easier.

"How much do you know?" He forced his head around on the pillow to face her.

Amelia hesitated.

"Some," she said. "You've been talking in your sleep."

Whatever he'd said in his sleep, he decided to leave it there. He told her the rest, all of it, from the leaf in Viridia's hair to the bolt out of a clear sky, the craters, the empty grass, Jazen and Barda, the mage with the whip made of lightning. His voice gave out twice and she waited both times. Partway through, his hand ended up between both of hers. When he finished, she sat studying their hands on top of the quilt.

"Should I find them," she said, "and bring them home?"

It was not an idle question. Bal knew that much. If he said yes, she would go. She would go right now, this minute, and find them wherever they were, and bring them back, and she would break the rule against interfering to do it. Then the other originators would have every reason they needed. That was what this whole war was about to begin with: Amelia and the rest of them, and the rules, and who got to break them.

He refused to be the reason for more of it.

He would have to deal with this himself. Somehow.

"No," he said. "I'll figure something out."

He saw what it cost her to hear that. Her thumb stopped moving on his knuckles. She could see what it cost him to say it, too. She did not argue. She let his answer stand.

The quiet went on, and he let it.

---

Someone knocked on the door, hard.

Bal jumped, and everything from his neck to his navel objected.

"Come in!"

The door opened slowly, a crack and then wider, and Xavier put his head around it. He looked worried. He kept hold of the door, as if he might yet back out.

"What is it, Xavier?"

Bal dreaded the answer before it came. Whatever could put that face on Xavier, it would not be anything good.

"Uh. Lady Amelia. High Priest." Xavier coughed into his fist. "I'm afraid we've got news."

Bal waved them in.

They came in together and halted by the bedpost. Neither man looked to have slept in the two days Bal had been out. Their scarlet robes were filthy, mud to the knee and something darker splashed higher up, and their white sashes had gone gray. Under Xavier's clear blue eyes the skin was smudged nearly black. Thad's ponytail had come half loose and he had not bothered to fix it. In the hush after Xavier's announcement, Thad's stomach growled, long and loud. He ignored it with great dignity. Xavier ignored it too, harder.

The two of them seemed to be deciding between them who was going to say it. Xavier left it to him. Thad sighed and laced his fingers together in front of his robe.

"High Priest Toma's army is approaching from the north," he said. "They'll be here in about two hours. We've also had a messenger from Toma. Under a flag of parley."

From inside his robe he drew a roll of parchment.

"The letter wasn't sealed," he said, and handed it over.

Bal accepted it. The parchment was worn fuzzy along its edges; somebody had rolled and unrolled it more than once on its way up to him. He was afraid he knew already what it said. He unrolled it slowly anyway.

The hand was neat and upright and very sure of itself. Then he read it.

The High Priest Toma had Lora and Viridia. His army would sit on their doorstep until every last soul in the temple was dead, unless Bal agreed to a Mages Duel. One on one. If he accepted, the girls would come to no harm. To accept, he was to hang a large blue cloth from a pole and wave it once the army had arrived. Then, at the very bottom, in the same tidy hand, came a postscript. He could even have his *pet originator* teleport him, if he liked, so that they could settle the matter quickly. He was to meet the High Priest in two days' time.

Bal groaned. His course was plain. Whether he had the strength to do it was not.

"Hang the blue cloth," he told Xavier. "I accept."

Xavier went to see to it himself, and Thad went with him. They slipped out to the corridor and eased the door to, so gently that the latch hardly clicked.

Bal's head dropped back onto the pillow. The letter slid off his fingers onto the quilt and lay there, half rolled up again.

Amelia smoothed the hair back off his forehead and stayed close, and neither of them spoke.

"So I have two days," Bal said, without opening his eyes. "Two days to figure out how to beat Toma and get Lora and Viridia back."

He got himself upright, and not quietly. The burn across his front pulled tight, and the breath left him in a hiss. He swung his legs out and got his feet on the floor and stood.

The room tipped. His knees went loose.

Amelia reached him mid-sway, got an arm under his, and steered him back to the bedding.

"You can't fight him like this," Amelia said.

"What else can I do?" Even saying it hurt.

He tried again, steadier, and this time the room stayed put.

"You can use me," Amelia said, with a huff, as if it ought to have been obvious.

"Use you?" Surely he had misheard.

Amelia appealed to the ceiling for patience.

"You've been researching blood powers," she said. "Even ones nobody else has ever known about. But there's one not even you have figured out." She considered that. "To be fair, nobody else ever did either. But still."

Bal lowered himself onto the bed again, carefully, and faced her. For now, the pain in his chest could wait.

"What is it?"

"Drinking blood will heal you," Amelia said simply.

Now that she had said it, it fit. Amelia was a vampire. Vampires healed themselves by drinking blood. She had made the Blood sygl, so in a way it made perfect sense that her own would heal whoever had it. And most people were never going to drink blood if they could help it, so it also made sense that nobody had found that out. He had done it once, of course, on a stone bench in her temple, and walked out with a hand. He had never thought of it as healing since.

There was a gleam in Amelia's eye. He knew that gleam.

"And does it do anything besides heal me?"

Amelia smiled, pleased with him. That had been the right question.

"It also makes your blood magyk stronger," she said. "It might give you other blood magyk abilities too."

Bal mulled that.

"What happens if I drink the blood of somebody with a different sygl?"

"That's too unpredictable to tell." She held up a finger. "And this only works for my High Priest. That's you. Not for regular blood sygls. A regular priest gets the healing, though. Nothing else."

Bal smiled at her.

"Then would you please let me drink some of your blood?"

"Of course, Master."

She slipped from the room, and he heard the latch fall.

---

Bal waited, his feet on the cold floor.

He could hear her moving somewhere in the next rooms. A drawer. A door. Then nothing for a while. Down in the canyon, very faint, somebody was shouting orders, and somebody else was shouting back that he'd heard, thanks. Two hours. The army would be closer than that by now. Out there men were getting ready to die because of a letter on a quilt, and the only person who could stop any of it was sitting on the end of a bed, waiting for a cup.

The letter lay on the quilt where it had fallen, curled up on itself. The postscript showed at the open end. *His pet originator.* He nudged it with his knuckles until it rolled behind the pillow and was gone.

In a little while Amelia returned.

She carried a chalice before her, wide and plain, with a silver foot and a cup of gold, and it was filled to the brim. He did not have to ask with what. It was dark red, nearly black at the center, and so full that the surface stood up a little above the rim and trembled as she walked. She did not spill one drop. She rested it on the bedside table, next to the pitcher.

Then she knelt on the floor in front of him.

She did it just as she had done in the temple behind Saint Andre's, on the day of the binding. Bal remembered that afternoon in a rush, the braziers and the stone under her knees. It had been barely two months. It felt longer.

"Drinking this will bind us together even more strongly," Amelia said. Her eyes held his. "Do not speak of what you see when you drink. At least, not now."

What she meant was beyond him. He took her word for it without a question.

Amelia lifted the chalice again, two-handed, and offered it up to him.

He did as she had done and took it with both of his. It was heavier than it looked, and the gold was warm where her fingers had been. Over the rim, Amelia waited. He lifted it to his mouth and drank.

The first drop touched his tongue, and the room was gone.

Memories. They came at him in a flood, too fast, too many, each crowding the last, and it was some minutes before he understood that none of them were his. They were Amelia's. He was not seeing them through her eyes, either. He was outside them somehow, like somebody watching a movie of somebody else's life.

He watched.

They were private. That much was plain right away, and he understood then why she had warned him not to speak of them. It touched him deeply that she would let him see them at all.

Then, as they went on flashing past, he began to understand something more. He began to see why she was letting him.

He drank until the cup was empty.

When he lowered it, the bedroom was back. The curtains, the quilt, the pitcher, and Amelia on her knees in front of him, right there still.

"Did you get what you needed?" she asked.

Bal returned the chalice to the table. Then he reached down and drew her up into his arms and held her.

"Thank you," he whispered against her ear.

He felt her sigh against him, held, it seemed, since she had left the room.

He released her and stood.

He was healed. He knew it before he checked. Nothing pulled. The floor held still. He breathed to the bottom of his lungs. Across his chest, where the whip of lightning had laid him open, the skin had closed over smooth. There was no scar. Not even a mark.

Bal grinned.

Time to go get Lora and Viridia back.

---

Over the two days that followed, he trained almost without stopping.

He never had to rest. Amelia's blood kept him going, through the days and through both nights, and he did not miss sleep. They fought it out on her temple roof, high above the canyon, under the sun and later under the stars, on flat stone gone warm in the day and cold at night. Amelia held herself back as far as she could, so that he could practice against her.

He had ideas to try. New ones. Some of them came from things he had seen in her memories, and the best person in the world to try them on was Amelia herself, because nothing he did could hurt her. He tried them, and they failed, and he tried them again, and she told him exactly how badly each one had gone. She made none of it sound better than it was. He'd have hated it if she had. Sometime in the first night, one idea nearly worked. She called across the roof for him to do it again, and it fell apart on him, and they kept at it till it held. On the second evening the stone up there was scuffed in long pale streaks where his bare heels had skidded.

Then the morning of the duel came, and he was ready to face Toma.

He was not fooling himself. There was still a good chance he would die today. But there was no other road open to him, and at least now he had a fighting chance, which he'd lacked entirely in that bed.

The bandolier crossed his chest from shoulder to hip. In each of its little loops sat a small glass vial filled with his own blood, stoppered tight. He pressed the stoppers in turn with his thumb, end to end. All secure.

"Ready," he told Amelia.

He had chosen not to wear a shirt. He had on a plain pair of brown pants and nothing on his feet. The stone of the roof was cool under his soles, and gritty, and the wind rising off the canyon went straight across his bare skin.

Amelia crossed the roof to him. She was barefoot too, and wearing her black dress, the one he loved so much, its long hem stirring in the wind.

Her hand slid into his.

Her fingers fit between his, cool and sure. The bond hummed in it, stronger than it had ever been, and her blood moved somewhere in his own. The canyon, the roof, the army's noise below them: in a moment all of that would be gone. Somewhere far from here, Toma had been waiting two days for him too.

He waited for her to take him there.
