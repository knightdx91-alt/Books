# Chapter Two: The Queen in the Temple

For a few heartbeats nothing answered him.

Out of the darkness at the back of the temple, behind the last of the pillars where the firelight gave up, a pale girl stepped forward into the light.

She came unhurried, her footsteps making no sound at all on the stone, and stopped a few paces short of the nearer brazier where the glow could find her. Bal guessed she was a few years older than he was. She cocked her head and studied him with frank, open interest, much as you might study a cat that had turned up on your doorstep in the rain.

"Hello yourself," she said, with a curious look on her face. "Mind telling me how you got in?"

The girl was wearing a long black dress that fell to the floor and moved around her ankles as she walked. Teardrop rubies hung from her ears, and every time she moved her head they caught the firelight and threw it back in small red sparks. Her hair was midnight black, straight and glossy, and reached to the middle of her back. Her skin was pale as candle wax against all that black. She wasn't skinny, but she wasn't fat either; she had what Bal considered exactly the right amount of curves, and the dress made no secret of them.

He had a whole speech somewhere in his head. He was certain of it. He'd planned what he'd say if someone caught him, back on the roof that morning, and it had been a good speech, reasonable and apologetic and only a little bit untrue. None of it would come out.

At a loss for words, which was a first for Bal, he numbly pointed at the ceiling.

The girl followed his finger up to the open roof and the patch of blue sky beyond it. Then she looked back down at him, and at the rug lying in a heap on the floor behind him, and arched a perfectly trimmed eyebrow.

"You flew in?"

He managed a jerky nod.

"Huh." She sounded delighted. "No one's tried that one. Oh, they've tried the doors. Kicking, prying, chanting. One boy stood out there and asked them nicely to open. I liked him. But nobody's ever come in through the top." She clasped her hands behind her back and rocked on her heels. "What gave you the idea?"

That, at least, he could answer, once his tongue came unstuck.

"I live at Saint Andre's," he said. "The orphanage, over the wall. I was up on the roof the other day looking at the carvings, and this whole building flickered. Only for a second. I saw the pillars and the fires and the statue, and then it was plain stone again."

"Really?" She glanced upward, eyebrows raised. "How careless of it."

"Our ladder's too short to get up here. And the gap's too wide to jump." He was talking faster now and couldn't seem to stop. "So I found a spell in *Sygldry Year 2* for carrying things, and I practiced on a sock first."

"And how did the sock do?"

"It hit the dormitory ceiling. Somebody had to fetch it down."

"Poor sock," the girl said gravely. She looked at the rug in its heap on the floor. "I see you've moved up in the world."

"I honestly thought I was seeing things," Bal finished. Now that he had started talking, the questions crowded up behind his teeth. "Who are you? How did *you* get in?"

The girl laughed and waved a hand.

The air between them shivered. Stone grated softly on stone, and in a matter of seconds two stone benches stood on the floor between them, facing one another, as solid as if they had been carved there a hundred years ago. They were plain grey, smooth-topped, with lion's feet at the corners. A thin film of dust lay on them.

"Have a seat," she said, "and I'll explain."

Bal stared at the benches. He had spent a whole spring getting tin cups to float, and three nights coaxing one rug off the floorboards. She had pulled two pieces of furniture out of the air with a flick of her wrist, without a word. He'd never seen a display of magyk like it, not from the teachers who came to the orphanage, not from anyone.

He walked slowly to the nearer bench and sat down on the edge of it. The stone was cool and entirely real. The pale girl swept her skirt aside and took the other bench, settling in with the ease of someone in her own favorite chair.

"My name is Amelia," she said, "and this is my temple."

It was only polite to answer that. "I'm Balthazaar," he said. "Bal, to my friends."

"Balthazaar." She tried the name out slowly, tasting it, and looked pleased with the result. "Now that's a name. Somebody chose well."

Bal looked around. He took in the two rows of pillars, the two braziers, the shaft of sunlight falling through the roof that wasn't there, and the dark statue with its face in shadow. Then he looked back at Amelia, sitting across from him with her hands folded in her lap and the rubies glinting at her ears.

"So," Bal asked, still confused, "a temple for what?"

Amelia frowned. It was the first time he'd seen her look anything other than amused, and it put a little crease between those perfect eyebrows.

"You've never heard of me?" she said. "The Queen of the vampires?"

Bal's mouth dropped open.

He knew the words. Everybody knew the words. Vampires were a story you told younger children in the dormitory after lights out, a flashlight held under your chin. The Queen of the vampires was the one you saved for last, because she was the worst, and because she was supposed to have been real once, ages ago.

"Wait, wait," Bal stuttered. "You mean the originator of the blood sygl?" His voice came out embarrassingly high. "I thought you were a myth!"

Now it was Amelia's mouth that dropped open, and this time it was in outrage.

"A *myth*?" She came up off the bench. "They have made me a *myth*?"

Her voice rang off the pillars and came back doubled. The fires in both braziers leapt up, tall and crackling, and threw her shadow huge over the paving and halfway up the far wall. Something in the air around her went tight and cold, and every hair on Bal's arms stood on end.

Bal scrambled to his feet and began backing away slowly, one careful step at a time, keeping his eyes on her and his hand out in front of him for all the good it would do.

Amelia saw this. He watched her see it. She shut her eyes, pressed her lips together, and took a long breath through her nose, then another, and the tight cold feeling eased out of the air. The braziers sank back to their low, steady glow. When she opened her eyes again, she was a pale girl in a black dress and nothing more.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I wasn't expecting that. Although I guess I should have known." She sat back down on her bench, smoothing her skirt, and gave him a small, rueful smile. "Please. Sit. I won't bite. Not without asking."

Bal stayed where he was, halfway between his bench and the rug, unable to decide whether sitting or running was the more sensible choice.

Amelia didn't seem to mind. She was looking at his arm.

"What happened to your sygl?" she asked, pointing at the stump of his arm. "Did the thought of having a myth's sygl disgust you so much you had to cut off your hand?"

At the mention of his hand, Bal stopped.

He glanced at his stump, then back at her. The itch was there again, under the skin, sharper than he'd ever felt it.

"What do you mean?"

Amelia laughed. It was a high, clear laugh, and it echoed off the stone walls of the temple and came back to them from every side.

"My, how the fates work," she said, shaking her head in wonder. "You lose your hand for showing my sygl, and you still end up stumbling into my temple. Flying into it, no less."

Bal stumbled back to his seat. He didn't decide to; his legs simply took him there, and he sat down hard on the cool stone.

"You mean," he said quietly, "I had a blood sygl?"

Amelia inclined her head.

"A good, strong connection, too," she said. "I can feel it from here. I felt it the moment you dropped through my roof. It's still in there, you know. Cutting off the hand doesn't cut out the blood."

At her words, Balthazaar heard the truth.

He couldn't have explained how. It was a settling more than a sound, a click deep down in his chest, as something that had been crooked all his life slid into its proper place. The blockage in his wrist. The itch. Someone had bricked up a door in him while he was too small to know there was a door there at all.

He knew what blood sygls were. Everybody did. Blood sygls, while not strictly outlawed, were frowned upon. Badly.

Bal looked up. Amelia was watching him, waiting, her pale hands still in her lap.

"You don't know what they say about it?" he asked. "Out there?"

"I've been in here," Amelia said. "Tell me."

He rubbed the stump against his knee. "Well. If a baby's born showing a blood sygl, the doctors usually take the hand. Before the mark can settle in."

"Usually?" Amelia asked.

"Mostly. I don't think anybody asks the baby. People think it makes you crazy. That's what the stories say, anyway. Blood magic gets into your head, and you lose it, and you start going after people."

"Crazy," Amelia said. She spread her hands, inviting him to look. "Do I look crazy to you?"

Bal looked. Rubies, black dress, one eyebrow up.

"Not right now," he said honestly.

That got the corner of her mouth to twitch. "Go on."

"I always figured a lot of it was there to get people riled up against anyone with the mark. But that's just me. Nobody else I know ever wondered about it." He hesitated. "The other part is that nobody's seen you. Not for years. Longer than years. For a long time, people thought the blood originator was Dracula."

"Dracula," Amelia repeated. Her voice had gone flat.

"But then somebody killed him. Which kind of proved he wasn't." Bal shifted on the bench. "Everyone felt pretty stupid about that afterward."

Amelia said nothing. Behind her, the nearer brazier sank low and then flared up again, though there wasn't a breath of wind. Her hands had stopped moving in her lap.

There was one more piece, and Bal wished there weren't. He made himself say it anyway.

"Once a year," he said, "all the originators come to the Heir's School. It's the first school, the big one. They come to claim their blood, the new students born under their mark. There's a place set aside for you there, the same as for the others. You've never come. Not once, as long as anyone can remember. After enough years of an empty chair, people stopped believing anyone would ever fill it."

Once he was done, Amelia exploded.

He meant it literally. One moment she was sitting on the bench across from him, very still and very pale, and the next there was no girl on the bench at all. There was a whirling, shrieking cloud of bats, hundreds of them, their wings beating the air into a gale. They boiled up toward the open roof and wheeled around the pillars and streamed between the braziers so that the fires guttered and flared. Bal threw his arm over his head and ducked. Leathery wings brushed his hair. Tiny claws snagged his sleeve and let go. The noise filled the whole temple, a high furious chittering that set his teeth on edge.

It went on for about a minute. Then the bats poured back together over the bench, and folded in on themselves, and Amelia was sitting there again in her long black dress, not a hair out of place.

Bal lowered his arm cautiously. A single tiny black wisp drifted down past his nose and vanished before it reached the floor. He looked at the place where it had been, and then at her.

"Does that happen often?" he asked.

"Only when I'm angry," Amelia said through her teeth. "I haven't been this angry in a long while."

She was still fuming. Bal could see it in the set of her jaw and in how her fingers had knotted in the black fabric of her skirt. When she spoke, she spoke mostly to herself, in a low, furious mutter.

"They lock me up in here and then act like I don't exist." She drew a slow breath and let it out again. Then she turned back to him, and some of the anger left her. "Why haven't you gone to school? You've got the magic for it. I can feel it on you. Even without my magic, you're still powerful in your own right."

Bal held up his stump.

"No sygl," he said simply. "No school will let you in unless you have a sygl."

Amelia narrowed her eyes at him. The firelight caught them as she did, and now Bal saw clearly what color they were. Red, the same red as the rubies at her ears.

Then she grinned.

"If you had a sygl," she asked, "would you go?"

Bal answered immediately.

"Of course. I've always wanted to."

"Would you do anything to go?" Amelia asked slowly.

She rose from her bench as she said it and walked toward him, one unhurried step after another, her dress whispering on the flagstones. She never took her eyes from his. Bal found that he couldn't look away either. The rubies swung gently at her ears. She was close enough now that he could smell her, a faint scent of cold stone and something sweeter underneath it, roses maybe, or wine.

Bal swallowed nervously.

"Uhm," he said. "Within reason."

Amelia laughed and sat down beside him, and the spell of the moment broke. Her shoulder brushed his, and she swung her feet beneath the hem of her dress like a girl half her apparent age.

"Within reason," she repeated. "Good. I like a boy with sense." She turned toward him on the bench, her knee against his, and her voice went serious. "Then hear me, Balthazaar. You shall go to this school. I will bond myself to you as your familiar. Doing so will free me from this place."

Bal blinked. "Your familiar?"

"Your familiar," she said firmly. "That way you and I can show the rest of the world that the Blood Bond is alive and well." Her eyes glinted. "Let them call me a myth with me standing in the middle of their courtyard. I'd like to see them try."

Then her voice softened, and she laid one cool hand on his knee.

"I will not ask anything of you that you are unwilling to give," she said. "You have my word on that."

Bal looked at her hand, and then at her face, and then down at the stump.

It all sounded so wonderful that he didn't quite trust it. Going to a Sygldry school, as Fredrick had, climbing into a car with a trunk and waving goodbye to everyone on the steps. Learning magic from people who knew what they were doing instead of from a cracked secondhand book by flashlight. All of it was sitting there in front of him, close enough to touch, and there was still one big problem standing in the way.

Still nervous, Bal held up the stump between them.

"How am I going to get your sygl back?" he asked. "I'd need a hand to put it on."

Amelia smiled. It was a slow smile, and a warm one, and it showed the very tips of two sharp white teeth.

"Are you thirsty?" she asked.
