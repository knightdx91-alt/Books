The innkeeper hurried out from behind her counter at something close to a trot, drying her hands on her apron as she came.

She was a plump woman with a smile that started well before she reached them. Behind her the shutters stood open along the whole long wall, letting whatever breeze Londra had to offer wander between the tables. Six or seven people sat drinking in the cool. One old man had his boots off under his table and was not ashamed of it.

The stable boy's words had followed Bal in from the yard. Nobody should have known they were coming. But too many people had already seen Viridia: two guards, half a street, a man whose pipe had gone out. If they turned around now and rode back through the gate, somebody would ask why, and he had no answer ready.

He stood a step behind his lady with the bags and kept his face as empty as he could get it.

"Welcome to the Winespring Inn, my lady," the innkeeper said. "We had your letter just this morning, and your rooms are all made up. And I was told to give you this when you arrived."

She drew a plain white envelope from her apron pocket. Viridia took it as if envelopes were brought to her on every landing in the country, and passed it back to Lora without a glance.

"I'm Mrs. Locklan. Come up, come up. You'll want out of those riding things. Your room has a servants' room off it, so your people will be right there whenever you want them."

She went ahead of them up the stairs, still talking. The stairs were broad and dark with polish, and they creaked on every other step, as though the house had opinions about guests. On the landing Viridia stopped, and Mrs. Locklan stopped too, a step above her.

"Mrs. Locklan. The letter you got this morning. How were you told you'd recognize me?"

"Why, by the sparking hair, of course." Mrs. Locklan beamed down at her. "Normally I'd have taken a thing like that for somebody playing a bad joke on me. But it came with payment for the rooms and all your meals, and nobody plays a joke that expensive." She pressed her palms together under her chin. "I still can't wait to tell my husband. A high-born lady with Raizen's favor, staying in my inn. He'll say I'm making it up. Anyway." She edged past them toward the noise below. "Whatever you need, my lady, don't hesitate to ask."

Her steps went creaking away down the stairs.

---

The servants' room was a narrow slot with one bed, one window, and a door through to Viridia's room that did not quite meet its frame. Bal stowed the bags under the bed and set Lora's alchemy chest under the window, where it would not get kicked.

Back in the other room, he found Viridia with the envelope open.

She read it with the worried crease still between her eyebrows. Halfway down, the crease let go, and she started laughing, so hard and so suddenly she had to sit down on the bed. She handed the page to Lora. Lora read it, clamped her mouth shut on whatever wanted out, and tossed it to Bal.

He knew the hand at once.

*My loves,*

*Do not think I was joking about going after Raizen if something should happen to you. Surely you would not want me to have the death of a fellow originator on my hands.*

*Be safe and come home to me.*

There was no name. Just a single *A* at the bottom, with a tail on it that ran on well past where a tail should stop.

Bal read it again. Then he tore it in half, tore the halves, and kept on tearing until the letter was a little drift of white flakes in his cupped hand, too fine for anybody to piece together. He tipped them into the cold grate.

"We ought to have guessed she'd pull a stunt," he said. "That girl loves having the last word."

Neither of them argued.

---

Viridia stood and turned her back to Lora, who started on the long row of buttons down it without being asked.

"Why did you ask the guards about the Three Boars?" Lora said, now that the door was shut on the rest of the inn.

"I wanted to see if anybody would say something about the priests." Viridia worked her arms out of the sleeves. "They'll be here in two days. I thought maybe the guards would try to warn people off staying there."

The gray dress slid to the floor and she kicked free of it. Bal had meant to look at the window. He looked at Viridia. She had a fine dusting of road on her shoulders where the collar had ended, and nowhere else, and the rest of her was long and tan and entirely unbothered by him. She shook out a clean shift and caught him looking past the hem of it.

She smiled, slow.

"Later," she said, and plainly meant it.

She got herself into the shift and a plain skirt. Then she combed her fingers back through her hair, from the scalp right out to the ends, and little threads of blue went skittering off the strands ahead of her fingers and popped out in the air.

It brought back what had been nagging at him since the gate.

"Now I'm wondering if I made the right call. Bringing you both."

Viridia's hands stopped in her hair. "Where's this coming from?"

"I honestly didn't realize what a big deal your gift was," he said. "You use it so rarely, I hardly ever remember you've got it. Then we came through that gate."

"Yeah. That's probably something we should've thought of. I didn't realize either. I knew it was rare. I guess I never knew how rare. With how people were looking at me today, I'll be noticed everywhere we go."

Lora stopped folding the gray dress.

"You know, it might not be as bad as we think," she said. "Yeah, they'll notice you. But if they're looking at you, they won't be looking at us."

Bal pictured the ride in. Every face in the street had gone to the blue hair in front. Not one, that he could recall, had stayed on the manservant behind long enough to tell what color his coat was.

"Huh," he said.

"Well, it's too late to fret over it now," Viridia said, already done with it. "We need a plan for the priests when they get here."

---

A manservant out on his lady's errands was nobody, so Bal went out to learn what he could.

Xavier's people had said the Three Boars took a fresh cask of ale on Wednesdays, and a cask came from somewhere. He asked a woman selling apples where the brewers were, then a carter watering his mule, and the carter sent him east, to a big plain building with a warehouse joined to the back of it that ran right down onto the water. The river was wide there and slow, the color of tea. Only two ships were tied up along the dock, both well down from the brewer's end.

Bal knocked at the street door, waited the polite length of time, and let himself in.

A man came from an office at the back to meet him. He was somewhere in his forties and built like he still lifted his own barrels, with his sleeves rolled to the elbow. His brown hair had started going thin on top. His eyes were sharp. They took in Bal's servant's coat and dusty boots, and Bal saw him decide this might be an order.

"Hello. My name's James. How can I help you?"

Through the doorway behind him was the office desk, and everything on it sat square to the edge. Even the pens lay in a row.

"My lady's heard our revered priests will be in town soon," Bal said. "We won't be staying long ourselves, but she thought she'd buy them some spirits while we're here. She hears the Three Boars is where they always stay, and that you supply it. She hoped you might know what they'd like."

James studied him until Bal began to wonder what he had gotten wrong. Then he jerked his head at the office. He took the big desk; Bal got the chair in front of it, hard and straight-backed, chosen, he suspected, so nobody would stay in it long.

"I might know," James said. "They usually stay two days. First day they get roaring drunk. Second day they do their business, whatever it is, and then they head home."

"Would you have any idea of a suitable gift for them?"

"That depends." James put his elbows on the desk, and one corner of his mouth went up. "On how much coin you've got. And how much your lady wants to impress them."

Bal smiled back. "I'm sure my lady wants to impress them very much. Money's no object."

James laughed from the belly, one big round boom that filled the little office and bounced off its walls. "All right, then," he said, getting up. "Follow me."

---

He took Bal out the back and along a short covered passage to the warehouse. Business was good, James told him; a shipment had come in from Cartesia a few days back, and the place was nearly full. He hauled the door open on its runner.

It was cool inside, cool enough to lift the hairs on Bal's neck. The big ale casks were stacked in rows from the floor to well above a tall man's reach, and every lid had a date burned into the wood.

"What are the dates for?"

"When that one was brewed." James slapped a lid as he passed it, fondly. "The longer an ale sits, the better it gets. Some of these I'm in no hurry to sell."

The smaller casks, the brandy and the sherry, lived in the far right-hand corner, lined up neat against the back wall in a single row. They were pale maple, and small enough for Bal to wrap both arms around one and lift it.

"Your priests usually take the cheaper brandy," James said. "They don't get paid much. Mind you, it doesn't stop them spending plenty getting drunk on it." He walked along the row and stopped by the final four. "Now these. These are older. I've been sitting on them for years. Bought them off a trader vessel that came up the river, out of the Ventish region. By far the most potent brandy I've got."

Bal considered the row of them. "The most expensive, too, I'll bet."

The corners of James's eyes creased. "Of course. If you want the best, it isn't cheap."

A gift like that would surely get them close to the priests. What came after, once they were that close, was a question for later, and later was coming up fast.

"How much?" he said.

They haggled. James named a price that made Bal's stomach drop, and they went at it for a while, and in the end Bal allowed himself to be talked up further than he had to. A happy brewer forgot a customer quicker than a sore one. He arranged for the cask to go to the Winespring Inn, so his lady could present it herself, and counted the coins onto the lid of a sherry cask. It took most of the purse.

---

The girls had stayed in their room. They'd had lunch sent up instead, and there was a pot of stew on the table under a cloth and a loaf beside it still warm enough to steam when Viridia tore it.

The room had only one chair. Bal got it. Viridia handed him a bowl and half the loaf, and he ate.

The stew had real meat in it, a lot of it, and carrots and onions and some kind of bean, all in a broth thick enough to hold the spoon up. At Saint Andre's, meat in a stew had been more of a rumor than an ingredient. Bal went slowly and savored every spoonful.

"How did it go?" Lora asked.

"Pretty good. I bought us some brandy."

Lora's eyebrow went up over the rim of her glasses. "You could have bought brandy here."

"Not Ventish brandy, I couldn't." He tore off more bread. "Good thing Amelia paid for the rooms. That cask put a big dent in the purse. It'll be worth it, though. With that, we ought to get in front of them. I just have no idea what we do once we're there."

"Well, at least that part's figured out," Lora said. She was on the floor with her back to the bed. "We could always use our blood darts. Kill them right there."

"No," Bal said. "We want to walk out of there alive, remember? Kill them like that and we're fighting our way out across half the city. I know you're good with that rapier. Viridia and I aren't."

Nobody had anything after that. A cart went by under the window and someone in the yard shouted at it. Bal finished his stew, wiped the bowl out with the last of the bread, and took it down to Mrs. Locklan. He offered to wash it. She looked at him as though he had offered to marry her, and took it away from him.

---

When he got back upstairs Lora was on her knees with her alchemy chest open, standing vials on the boards around her in a widening ring.

"Looking for something?"

"Seeing if I've got everything for the nightstalker poison." She held a vial to the window, squinted at the label, added it to the ring. "It was in that alchemy book you bought me. I remember reading it and not paying much attention, because I couldn't think when I'd ever want to poison anybody. But this is perfect." Another vial came out. "It stays dormant for twenty-four hours. Nothing. Then you get lethargic and it gets hard to breathe, and your heart stops about six hours after that. They'll all be on their way home by then, and we'll be well on our way too."

Bal reached down, took her by both hands, hauled her to her feet, and kissed her.

"You're amazing," he said, when he let her go.

Lora's glasses had gone crooked. She did not fix them.

"Have you got everything you need?" he asked.

She knelt among the vials again and read the labels a second time, frowning. "Everything except asphodel leaves and ginger root, it looks like. But I can get those here. They go into a lot of ordinary potions, so nobody'll think twice if I ask." She rocked back onto her heels. "It'll take about twenty hours to brew. I need to go get them now."

She packed everything into the chest in the order she'd unpacked it, hugged Bal, hugged Viridia, and promised to hurry.

---

She was three hours.

The first hour went by without anyone mentioning it. In the second, Bal cleaned his boots, and then cleaned them again. Twenty hours of brewing, and the priests in two days, and Lora not even started.

"She's fine," Viridia said, from the bed. "She's probably found a shop with a book in it."

"Yeah."

"She's fine, Bal."

It might have worked better if Viridia had not gotten up twice to check the window herself.

Close to the end of the third hour, the door swung inward and Lora came in backward, pushing it with her back because her arms were stacked to the chin with paper packages. Bal reached her before it finished opening. He took the pile off her and laid it on the bed, carefully, in case any of it broke.

"Thanks." Lora poured herself a cup of water from the pitcher and drank it straight down. "Sorry I'm late. Sorry. The alchemist wanted help brewing a night-sight potion, and I sort of got roped in. He took the shop over when his father passed, I think, and he's had less training than I have. At least I've had Ms. Summers to help me. That guy's got no one. He's been getting by on his father's notes, but there's nothing underneath them. He didn't even know what half the herbs in his own shop were for." She emptied the cup again. "Once we were done he gave me a bunch of herbs for free, so I restocked some of the ones I'd run out of. You never know what you'll need. And I got the asphodel and the ginger root."

Viridia met Bal's eye. They might have guessed. Sword work, books, alchemy, it made no difference what it was. Once Lora got into something, she went in deep and forgot to surface.

Lora was already unwrapping, sorting each herb into its own jar or twist of paper. Bal offered to help. So did Viridia. Lora told them to enjoy the city instead, since she couldn't leave the cauldron until the brew was well under way. They hung back and offered again.

"Does either of you know a single thing about alchemy?" Lora said, exasperated. "No. You don't. You'll just be in my way."

She herded them out with both hands, told them to have fun, and closed it on them.

Out in the hall the two of them stared at it. Then they both cracked up together and had to hold it in until they got to the stairs.

"Well, my lady," Bal said on the landing, back in his manservant voice, "what now?"

Viridia held her haughty face past every table downstairs and into the yard.

"Let's go see the Three Boars," she said on the street.

---

Bal walked a pace behind her, where Lora had coached him to walk, and caught up when the crowd allowed.

"Once you've finished school, what then?"

"If the war goes our way, sure." Viridia went quiet over a stretch of cobbles. "If I'm honest, no idea. All I ever wanted was out of my village. Now I'm out, and I've got nothing after that." She asked it without turning. "Is Amelia going to challenge the other originators, you think?"

Now it was Bal's turn not to know.

"If she makes a big enough statement fighting Raizen, she probably won't have to. If we win, she can demand pretty much anything she wants from him." He thought of Amelia's face when they had talked about it, and the smile she'd had at the end. "And from that smile, she's got something good in mind."

He had missed part of the talk about the poison, between the stew and the bowl.

"I take it we're slipping it into their drinks?"

"That's the plan."

A couple had stopped dead in the middle of the walk in front of a shop, and Viridia stepped around them. The shop was a jeweler's. Rings and necklaces lay on a slope of dark cloth in the window, and Viridia's eyes went across them, quick, and returned to the street.

"Lora says it's got no taste," she went on. "Her book says so. So that part's fine."

"Why not poison the whole cask?"

"Yeah. That would probably work better." She considered it. "Less risk of getting caught slipping something into a cup."

People still stared at her hair as they passed, but less than they had at noon. Word had gotten around, maybe. They were getting used to her.

---

The Three Boars was big. Three stories of gray stone, with a neat stable built on, and a long brick chimney climbing each end, one for the kitchen and one for the taproom, Bal guessed. The smell of roasting meat rolled out of the open kitchen windows and found him across the street. Every window had white shutters.

"Ten windows on the second story," he said. "Ten on the third."

"That's a lot of rooms." Viridia had stopped on the opposite side of the street, as though she were only waiting for a cart to pass. "The inn in my village had six. And that's counting every room except the one the innkeeper and his wife slept in."

The front doors stood wide open. Laughter rolled out of them now and then, the loud afternoon kind. Two stable boys in rough clothes were lounging against the stable wall, watching the street go by because it was their job to and it was hot. One of them spotted Viridia. He drove his elbow into his friend's ribs and pointed straight at her, with no shame at all.

Viridia gave them the smallest wave, a lady's wave, two fingers raised and lowered. Then she turned for home.

"It's funny," she said, a street later. She was watching the people around them. "Looking at everyone here, you could almost believe they weren't at war. They seem happier than I'd have thought."

Bal grunted. "That's because they feel safe behind their walls. I'd bet most of these people have never laid eyes on anyone with our sygl, let alone met one. All they hear is their priests saying how crazy we are. That if our originator got so crazy she went and attacked them, it's only a matter of time before all of us go crazy too."

A pair of guards came by on their rounds, walking slow, heading up the street toward the Three Boars. Bal kept an eye on their backs until they were past.

"You have to figure the other territories are about the same," he said.

They didn't say much after that. The streets thickened toward the center of town, where vendors were calling out their wares. A stall at a corner was selling sweet rolls, glazed and still warm, and Bal bought two. They stood off to the side of the crowd and ate them without talking.

He had not only asked her about after school to learn her plans. He'd hoped hers might give him some idea of his own. He was never leaving his girls. Past that, he had nothing. His whole life had come off its track the day he enrolled. Once this war was decided, he would either be dead, or he would need to decide what the rest of his life was for, and standing on a corner in Londra with sugar on his fingers, he honestly couldn't tell which looked likelier.

He had told Viridia the truth, though. He didn't think Amelia would go after the other originators. She would make her statement whether she won or lost. If he and the girls died out here, Bal fully believed she would kill Raizen for it, and nobody alive could talk her out of it. The letter in the grate had said as much. It was a poor sort of comfort. But at least the others would leave her alone after that.

Viridia ducked behind him.

She was gone in the same breath, off through the crowd at a pace sure to get her noticed. Bal crammed the rest of his roll in and went after her. She swung into a side street. He caught her there by the arm and drew her into the mouth of an alley, then put his head out around the corner and searched the street they'd left.

Carts. Shoppers. A dog. Nobody hurrying, and nobody looking their way.

He got the roll down and faced her. Viridia was leaning on the alley wall with both hands spread on the bricks behind her.

"Sorry. We had to run." Her hair was snapping at the ends. "That was one of them. A priest. I recognized him from what Thad told us. He's got a long scar right through his right eye. He wasn't wearing his azure robes, but it was him."

Bal swore, quietly. "I thought they weren't supposed to be here yet. Why's he early?"

"No clue." Viridia took hold of his sleeve and started towing him home through the back streets. "But we'd better stay hidden until the potion's done. I'd rather not risk a fight in the middle of town. Especially without Lora."

He let himself be towed, and they walked as fast as a lady and her manservant could walk without anyone wondering why.

---

Lora had the cauldron going over a little brazier by the window when they were let back in. The room smelled sharp and green, like a cut stalk. Viridia described the scar.

Lora swore too.

"We should have counted on Aleric coming early. From the little Thad and Xavier managed to get on him, he's the most unpredictable of the three." She took a pinch of something dark and dried from a twist of paper and began to crush it between two spoons. "Titus and Leon just follow orders. Aleric's got ambitions, and he's smart. With the war on, it makes sense he'd turn up ahead of the others."

She tipped the crushed nightshade over the cauldron and let it fall in a thin dark trickle, then stir, slow and gentle.

"Everything's fine on my end," she said, still stirring. "But I agree with Viridia. We should keep to our rooms until we're ready to act."

---

For the next fifteen hours, they did.

Bal read. Viridia read too, gave it up, and talked instead, low, so as not to bother Lora. Lora would not have noticed. She stirred and measured, and held vials up to the lamp, and later to the window when it turned gray. She ate whatever was handed to her without looking at it.

Some time in the night, Bal woke in the chair with his neck bent and saw her silhouetted against the brazier, the spoon going around and around. When he woke again it was morning and she was in the same place. He would have sworn she hadn't moved.

The day went by beyond the shutters. Meals came up on trays from Mrs. Locklan and plates went back down. Tables scraped and voices rose and fell under the floorboards. Somewhere out in the city, a man with a scar through his eye was walking around a day early, and Bal stayed clear of the window.

At about five in the afternoon, Lora straightened at the brazier and stretched. Her back went off in a long row of pops, like knuckles. She breathed out from somewhere around her heels.

"I think that's the hardest thing I've done in a while," she said. She was smiling, tired down to the bone. "But it looks like it turned out perfectly."

She dipped her spoon into the cauldron and lifted it, and let what was in it run slowly back off the edge in a thin string.

It was clear as water. Bal leaned in close and could find nothing in it at all. Twenty hours of dark powders and green stalks had gone into that pot, and what was left looked like you could wash your face in it.

"See how clear it is?" Lora said. "Not a hint of anything I put in."

Viridia was half lying on the bed with her shoulders propped on the headboard. "And you're sure they won't be able to taste it?"

"As sure as I can be. My book hasn't let me down yet." Lora wiped the spoon on a rag. "Even Ms. Summers said it was an exceptionally good book. That's glowing praise, coming from her." She waved at Bal. "Bring the cask over."

The cask had arrived from the brewer's that morning on a boy's shoulder. Bal carried it to the cauldron and started on the stopper. It fought him. He got a better grip and twisted, and it came free with a pop so loud that everybody jumped.

Lora transferred the poison with her wooden spoon. It took ages. The spoon was not large, and she would not spill a drop, and Bal held the cask steady while the clear stuff went in until she was scraping the bottom of the cauldron.

Getting the stopper back in was worse. It went in partway and stuck, though Bal leaned his whole weight on it. In the end he dropped onto the floor, pulled his boot off, and used the heel as a hammer. Five hard blows drove the stopper home, and they must have carried down to every table below.

He pulled his boot back on and set the cask on the table, which Lora had cleared of her bottles and jars.

The three of them stood around it.

It was a small cask. Pale maple, a date on the lid, a fat new dent beside the stopper where the boot heel had gone in. They were pinning their lives on it.

Nothing else remained to be done. They went to bed.

---

The knock came the next morning and brought everyone bolt upright.

Bal was out of bed and through into the servants' room, where a manservant ought to be found, before he was fully awake. He pulled the door to behind him, as far as it would go. Through the crack he watched Lora pat her hair flat, push her glasses up her nose, and open the door.

It was Mrs. Locklan. A letter for her lady, she said. She hoped she wasn't too early.

Lora thanked her as a maid would and closed the door softly.

Bal came back out. Lora stood by the cold grate with an envelope in her hand. Its face was blank.

She turned it over. The flap was sealed with a round of wax, and pressed into the wax was Raizen's Tempus sygl.
