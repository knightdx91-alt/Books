# Chapter Twenty-Seven: Thunder from a Clear Sky

Viridia was laughing before the horses were even saddled.

Lora had pulled a leaf out of Viridia's hair, a big brown one that had ridden in there all night, and waved it at her. The noise Viridia made sent something flapping out of the branches overhead and away, complaining. It was a good sound to wake up to. Bal stayed on his bedroll longer than he needed to and listened.

They led the horses out to where the trees ended and stood there a while, studying the road in both directions. It lay empty. No dust hung over it anywhere, no riders, no cart.

Bal was satisfied. "Nobody's after us. No reason I can see to rush. We can take it easy today and walk them."

The black blew through its nose as if it agreed.

They walked south through the yellow grass, and the morning came up warm and then warmer, and Viridia talked. She was telling Lora about her village, story after story, about people from home. Lora asked after each of them, and Viridia answered with her hands going as much as the reins allowed.

Last night she had cried herself out in Lora's arms, and then she had slept. This morning she was herself again, or near enough that Bal saw no difference from where he rode.

He was thinking about the bookshop in New Spring. About standing in the stacks with an armful of books he had not been reading, while Viridia told Lora, past the shelf, that her whole life had been planned out the day she was born. Who she'd marry, even. He had never asked her about it. It had not been his to ask, when he'd only heard it by accident.

But she was in a talking mood.

"So what was it like?" he said. "Growing up there. I bet all the boys were chasing after you."

Something went across Viridia's face. It came and went like a cloud over a field, and her mouth went flat while it lasted. Bal wished he had the words back.

Then it cleared, and she answered him anyway.

"The day I was born, it was decided I'd marry the headman's son. Bran. He was two at the time." She gave the name a sour twist. "My whole childhood, every time I told him I didn't like him, he'd smirk at me. Every single time. And he'd tell me it didn't matter, because he would have me eventually." She let the grass go by for a moment. "Getting my sygl was what saved me from marrying him."

Lora said nothing on her other side.

Bal chewed on that for a stretch of road. He knew the rule already, that anyone with a sygl was allowed out. He had not thought much about how few must ever use it.

"Are sygls really that rare in your village?"

Viridia took a long drink from her water bottle before she answered, and the sun winked off the neck of it.

"Yeah. Not sure why, though. Since the village lets anyone with a sygl leave, it basically gave me a new life."

She laughed, the same as this morning.

"That fool Bran tried to get his father to make me stay. Another six months and we'd have been married. And his father wouldn't do it." She told it to the sky, enjoying it. "Bran was not happy."

---

Bal smiled at her, at the pleasure all over her. The Three Boars was behind her, then. Her hair was popping again, tiny blue snaps along the ends of it every time the breeze lifted them, and he had missed the sound more than he'd realized.

It was a hot day. By late morning the sweat was running into his eyes, and he mopped his forehead with a rag from his pocket.

He kept checking behind them, without ever quite deciding to. Behind them the road was always empty.

After the fourth or fifth time, Lora nudged her horse in beside the black.

"Did you hear something?"

"No. It's only, for some reason I've got this funny feeling. Like somebody's got a bow pointed at my back." He laughed at himself. "It's probably me being paranoid."

To take their minds off it, and his, he started telling them jokes. They were the ones he'd learned at Saint Andre's, mostly from the older boys, and they were mostly terrible. Lora groaned at the worst one so hard she nearly dropped her reins. Viridia made him tell another one twice because she had not believed it the first go-round, that anybody would end a joke there.

The feeling did not go anywhere.

If anything it grew. It sat between his shoulder blades through the noon heat and into the long afternoon, and the more he ignored it, the heavier it got. When the sun began to drop, he gave up on jokes. His head kept going around: behind them, the sky, the grass to the right, the grass to the left, behind them again.

Somewhere along the way the girls had stopped talking. Bal missed the moment. They did not feel what he felt, plainly. They rode with their hands tighter on the reins anyway, because the feeling was his and they trusted him.

The shadows of the three horses stretched out long and thin on the road beside them. It would be close to six. Back in Londra, three priests who had drunk Ventish brandy at lunch yesterday would be dying about now, if Lora's book was right.

The boom came from behind them.

It rolled down on them out of the north, out of Londra, and went over them and on across the grass, and all three saddles creaked at once as they twisted in their seats.

It had sounded like thunder. One single tremendous clap of it, the kind that came straight down on a house in a summer storm.

Overhead the sky was clear from one edge to the other, a hot, high, faded blue, without one cloud in it anywhere to have made that sound.

Viridia had gone pale under her tan.

"Can you feel it now?" Bal asked her.

"Yeah." She was still facing north. "Yeah, I can."

Lora said, "Feel what?"

"I can't explain it." Viridia's voice had dropped. "It feels like we're in danger."

Bal's attention was still back toward Londra, and he could not pull it loose.

"Are we in danger?" Lora asked him.

"Yes."

"Can you tell from what?"

---

The bolt hit the road between their horses.

It came from nowhere. From that blank blue sky. White light and a crack that went right through Bal's teeth, and then the black was screaming and going straight up, and Bal was in the air with no saddle under him.

He landed on his shoulder and rolled. Hooves went past his head. All three horses were bolting, reins flying, stirrups flapping. More bolts came down around them, one and then three and then too many to keep track of, and each one blew a hole in the earth and threw dirt in his face.

He got to his knees. He could not hear anything but a high thin ringing.

Viridia was up before him.

She was planted in the thick of it with her feet apart and her arms stretched straight up at the sky, and her hair had risen and spread out behind her like a blue flag in a wind. She was trying to pull them in. Every bolt that came down, she reached for it, trying to drag it off course and into herself, where it couldn't hurt anybody. Some of them bent toward her. Most of them didn't. There were too many.

Bal flung himself clear of one that came down where he had been kneeling.

He saw another strike ten feet to his left, the hardest yet. He saw the earth go up. He saw something dark and big as a loaf fly out of it, tumbling end on end, and he was still moving clear of the light when it caught him on the side of the head.

Then there wasn't anything.

---

His head woke first. It was throbbing, a slow heavy beat, far off and steady, and his mouth was full of the taste of copper.

He worked his tongue around. It hurt. He had bitten it in the fall, bitten it hard, and the side of it was thick and raw.

Bal pushed himself upright.

He tried to call for Lora and Viridia. His voice was a croak, hardly a sound at all.

The road hadn't moved. Neither had the grass. Between them, in a wide ragged ring around him, the ground was full of craters. They were blackened down in the middle and scorched at the rims, and a few of them were still smoking. Hours had gone by. The sun hung low and red in the west, and the air had cooled.

Nobody was there but him.

The wind shifted, and he smelled burnt horse.

He stood. It took two tries. He started walking, around the edges of the craters, calling their names. His voice returned slowly, cracked and then hoarse and then loud.

"Lora! *Viridia!*"

All he had left of either of them was Viridia with her arms up and her hair flying. He kept calling anyway. He circled the craters, and went on out past them, and back to the road, searching the ground for anything. A footprint. A dropped glove. Broken grass where somebody had been dragged, or had run.

There wasn't anything. The grass stood up straight wherever he looked. The road was hard and dry and kept no marks. It was as if the girls had dropped off the edge of the world.

He went on calling until the sun was down and his voice was gone again, and the dark had risen out of the ground so he could not see his own feet.

He could not search in the dark. He knew that. Stopping was harder.

The horse that had been hit was Lora's. It lay on its side at the edge of the farthest crater, and he forced himself over to it. Her bedroll was still tied behind the saddle. He worked the straps loose breathing through his mouth, and pulled the roll free, and carried it upwind until the air was clean enough to breathe. He spread it out and stretched out on top of it.

Sleep was slow coming. Every time his eyes shut, something was waiting behind them. Lora somewhere dark. Viridia with her hands tied. People who wanted them dead, with the two of them right there in reach. He turned over, and turned back, and the bedroll carried a faint smell of Lora's alchemy chest, sharp and green.

Toward morning he slept, badly.

---

The first light of the sun came over the grass and into his face.

He shaded his eyes and sat, slowly.

Lora's bedroll. Lora's horse, dark against the grass. The black craters. Nobody else.

It hit him all over again, as hard as it had at dusk. It was like being punched in the stomach by somebody he hadn't seen coming. He stayed like that with his hand still raised to the sun and waited for it to let him breathe.

He rose and searched again. He ranged out farther than yesterday, in wide loops through the grass, east of the road and west of it, hoping for something. Anything. He found nothing.

They had been taken.

How, he couldn't begin to guess. Who, or from where, or how anyone had gotten them away without leaving a single mark. But nothing else made sense. If the girls had run, or been hurt, or worse, he would have found something by now.

He stood on the road and looked north, toward Londra.

If it was Toma behind this, the girls would not be in Londra. They would be headed for Pashar. And Pashar was days and days from here. With no horse and no supplies, nothing but the clothes he stood in and Lora's bedroll, he could not possibly reach them in time to do any good.

The army was evacuating blood sygls out of the Tempus border towns. He remembered Thad saying so, on the stairs at the temple. If he found the army, he'd find a horse.

Bal rolled up the bedroll, tied it with its own strap, and turned south, toward Amelia's territory.

---

It took him two days on foot.

He walked through two long hot days. He drank from a ditch, and once from a horse trough at the edge of a field whose owner never appeared. At night he slept a few hours on Lora's bedroll beside the road, and woke before light, and walked again. By the second afternoon he was not thinking much at all. His feet went on without him.

Some of the way he talked to the girls in his head. He told Lora he was coming. He told Viridia not to give anybody any lip until he got there, which was a waste of breath, because she would anyway, and he hoped she did.

Whenever he stopped, it found him again, and he stopped as little as he could.

The village he came to at the end of the second day was small, a crossroads with a well and maybe twenty houses, and there was a party of Blood soldiers in the square.

They were waiting. Behind them a little knot of people was hurrying in and out of a handful of the houses with bundles and baskets and one with a kettle, loading whatever they meant to keep onto a cart. Some of those people had black eyes. Some had split lips, scabbed over. One man had his arm strapped up across his body in a sling, and he was trying to carry a sack with the other.

The rest of the village stood back along the fronts of the houses and cursed the soldiers. They did it loudly, and they meant it, and nobody stepped off a doorstep. The soldiers had their hands near their weapons. They looked ready to fight, and the villagers knew it.

Bal walked straight in among them.

Let anybody who liked see his palm. He wasn't hiding it. He knew, with a cold, steady calm, that if anybody in that village tried to stop him, he would kill them.

Nobody tried.

Bal knew one of the soldiers. He had seen him at the temple: a centurion, Jazen Grey.

Jazen heard boots behind him and spun. Then he was coming across the square, fast, and keeping his voice down.

"High Priest." He looked Bal over, the dust and the dried blood in his hair and Lora's bedroll clutched to his side. "What brings you out here? We all thought you were off in Londra."

"I was." He sounded tired, even to himself. That couldn't be helped. "Things happened. I need to get to the temple, fast. Do you have a horse you can spare?"

Whatever Jazen saw in his face settled it.

"Of course. Take mine." He said it without a breath between. "If you ride hard, you can be at the temple by noon tomorrow."

Over by the blood sygls and their cart, another soldier was leading horses up.

"We'll get there some time tomorrow night," Jazen said. "When you see Commander Fern, would you let him know we've had no trouble? And that we'll be with him shortly."

"I'll tell him."

Jazen called to one of his men, who jogged off and returned leading a horse.

It was the biggest horse Bal had ever stood next to. A warhorse, deep through the chest and heavy in the legs, with a head on him the size of a small barrel. He was white all over, and in the late sun his coat shone.

Jazen ran a hand down the great neck and gave it a fond, heavy pat.

"Ol' Barda's never failed me yet," he said. "He'll get you there safe. And quick."

"I owe you for this. Truly."

He got a foot into the stirrup and hauled himself up. Barda was tall, and he took Bal's weight without shifting an inch. Bal found the reins, swung the big white head around toward the south road, and raised a hand to Jazen.

Jazen returned it.

Bal gave Barda his heels, and the village fell away behind them.

---

He rode all night.

He ran Barda at a gallop until the horse's breath went ragged, then let him drop to a trot until it evened out, then ran him again, all through the dark, under a sky full of stars. Barda never balked. Some time past midnight Bal woke with a jerk, his chin down and the reins slack in his fingers, and found the big horse still pounding steadily south as if he knew the road better than his rider did. He went where he was pointed and went on, and the heat of him rose through the saddle into Bal's legs, and the beat of the big hooves made more sense than anything else in the night.

The sun came up on his left. He kept on. The hills began, low ones at first, then higher, and a few were familiar.

It was a little before noon when he heard the fighting.

---

He topped the hill and pulled Barda up hard.

Below him, in the open ground in front of the canyon where the temple hid, a small party of Tempus soldiers was fighting Amelia's. It had to be another of the scouting parties Thad had told him about. It had been going on a while. Bodies lay scattered all over the field between the lines, men from both sides, all of them still.

Lightning cracked across the field.

At the rear of the Tempus party, two mages were hurling bolts into the blood soldiers without a pause between them. Far behind Amelia's lines stood two figures in scarlet robes, and even from here he knew them. Thad, with Xavier at his shoulder. Each had a great whip of blood going, and they were working furiously, catching the bolts in midflight and slapping them aside into the dirt.

They could not catch all of them. Every time one got through, it slammed down among the soldiers and men and horses went flying.

Bal's road had brought him in behind the Tempus party. From the hilltop the two mages were plain to see, their backs to him, bolt after bolt leaving their hands, steady and unhurried.

He slid down off Barda and left him to graze. Then he took to the trees on foot.

He went as quietly as he could, crouched, from trunk to trunk. He kept waiting for a sentry. There wasn't one. With the temple in front of them, every man they had was down on the field.

The trees thinned. Bal left them still crouched, and found himself a few yards from a raised wooden platform, as high as a tall man's shoulder, with the two storm mages standing on top of it.

From up there, the pair of them were killing his soldiers one bolt at a time.

He watched the field in front of them one more breath. Then he called the blood.

His left palm opened on its own, and the blood slid out and drew itself in, short and straight and wickedly pointed, and set hard in his fist. The right split in the same breath. From that one the blood poured long and loose and lay along the ground beside him, waiting.

He jumped onto the platform.

Both men spun around. The nearer of the two already had his hand out to throw. Bal drove the dagger at his face and put it into his eye, the right one.

The man screamed. He had jerked back at the very last instant, and the blade had gone in only a finger's depth. He clutched at his face, and Bal planted a boot in his middle and kicked him clean off the platform. He landed somewhere below with a thud.

Bal released the dagger.

That left the other one.

The second storm mage was young. Not much past Bal's age, with hair so light a blond it was nearly white. He did not run. He smiled at Bal, a grim little smile with no fun in it.

He held a whip. It was made of lightning. Pure crackling light, as long as Bal's own and alive along every inch, spitting and humming to itself.

Nothing like it had ever come at Bal before. He kept his distance.

"You're too late," the mage said. "I've already sent word to my commander. Every man we've got will be here soon."

He flicked the lash.

It went past Bal's ear tearing the air, and the air behind it stank of ozone, sharp, the smell after a storm.

Bal told his own lash what he wanted, and it did as it was told. Its edge thinned until it would cut. All along its length, heavy spikes pushed up through the blood and hardened there, as red and as sharp as the dagger had been.

He swung it.

It met the lightning in the air between them with a crack and a hiss. They swung, and swung again. Blood on lightning, lightning on blood, meeting every time and sliding off and coming around for more. Neither of them gave an inch. They circled each other on the boards, feet sliding, looking for a way in.

The mage found one first.

The mage's off hand came up, and a bolt leaped out of it at Bal's face. Bal twisted aside and the bolt went by so close it lifted his hair. He was still off balance when the lightning whip came down across his chest.

It threw him. He sailed off the platform after the first mage, and hit the ground flat on his back.

---

For a while, everything was white and empty.

The smell came next. Burnt skin. His own. He could not tell yet whether it hurt. His body had gone somewhere else and not told him where.

His blood came crawling back to him over the dirt, and slid home.

Up on the platform, the storm mage laughed. Then he jumped down, and Bal heard his boots land, and then walk. Unhurried. They came around and stopped by his head.

He had to move. He had to. His arms lay where they had fallen. His legs might have belonged to somebody else.

"I think I'll get a promotion after killing you," the mage said, in an easy, friendly sort of voice, as if they were waiting together for a coach. The lightning whip swung lazily to and fro above Bal's face, humming. "It's too bad I have to end it now. I can't chance you getting away."

He raised it high. Up there against the sky it changed, the white of it going to a deep blue, with an orange core burning through, like the inside of a flame.

His wrist flicked.

Bal's eyes closed.

Lora was somewhere north of here, and Viridia. *I'm sorry,* he told them, without any voice. *Forgive me. I couldn't protect you.*

He lay there and waited for it.

Nothing came.

After a moment, he opened one eye.

The storm mage was still standing over him. An arrow stood out of his neck, feathered and quivering, and he was scrabbling at it, trying to pull it free. The whip had gone. His face found Bal's, and what was in it now was fear. He knew. He knew exactly.

A bolt from a crossbow went through the side of his head.

He dropped. He fell sideways into the grass and lay still.

Bal rolled his head on the grass. It was all of him that would move. Across the field, blood soldiers were coming, running toward him, a lot of them, shouting something he could not make out.

The dark took him.
