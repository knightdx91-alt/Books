# Chapter Four: Mrs. Brown

The front door thumped shut behind them, and the shouting from the softball game went thin and far away.

After the bright yard, the front hall of the orphanage was dim and cool, and it took Bal's eyes a second to catch up. The walls were a dull grey that somebody, years back, must have thought looked respectable, and the floor was laid with white tiles that always seemed to echo as you walked on them. Bal's shoes clacked on them now. Amelia stopped in the middle of the floor, stamped once on purpose, and listened to the sound come back to her off the walls with her head cocked.

"Good acoustics," she said.

"Good for the staff," Bal said. "You can't sneak anywhere."

Thankfully, most of the other kids were outside. That suited Bal fine. He had to go and see Mrs. Brown, and he would rather not have half the orphanage trailing behind him asking about his hand.

Mrs. Brown was one of the women who helped run the orphanage. She was in her forties, and ever since Bal was small she had worn a light grey skirt and a white blouse, with her greying hair tucked up into a bun. Some kids didn't like her. If she caught you doing something you weren't supposed to, you got a severe punishment, and she was very good at catching people. Bal had never held that against her. As he saw it, if what you were doing was worth getting caught for, why get upset when you were? He had weeded his share of cabbages on the strength of that idea, and he still thought it was sound.

But Balthazaar had always known something else about Mrs. Brown, too. If he ever truly needed someone to talk to, or if he had really messed up, he could go to her.

This was one of those times he really needed to talk to her. He hadn't decided yet whether it was also the other kind.

Her office was at the end of the downstairs hallway, past the bulletin board with its curling chore lists. Five wooden chairs stood in a row beside her door. He knocked twice on the door, propped the rolled rug against the end chair, took the one nearest the door, and motioned for Amelia to do the same.

"What are we doing?" Amelia asked, plopping into the next chair.

She looked up and down the hallway with open interest. Even the chore lists got a look.

"I need to talk to someone about my hand," he said. He held it up for her to see. "Well. That, and me going to school. There's a new orientation class in two weeks. We had three boys and two girls leave a few days ago."

"And you think there's room for one more?"

"I think," Bal said, "that's what we're here to ask."

Amelia swung her feet under the chair. She did not seem worried in the slightest. Bal was worried enough for both of them.

"Come in," a voice yelled from behind the door.

Bal stood, wiped his palm on his pants, and motioned for Amelia to follow him inside.

---

The inside of Mrs. Brown's office showed little in the way of personality. It never had. There were two grey filing cabinets against one wall and a heavy wooden desk in front of the window, and that was nearly all. The only thing in the room that belonged to Mrs. Brown herself was a single picture frame on the corner of her desk, turned toward her chair, showing her husband. He was dead. Bal had known that for years without knowing anything else about it. Otherwise the office was as spartan and bare as a waiting room.

Mrs. Brown was writing something. She did not look up when they came in, and Bal knew better than to interrupt, so they waited just inside the closed door. Her pen scratched steadily across the page. Through the window behind her came a faint crack of the bat, then a cheer.

At last Mrs. Brown finished her line, put down her pen, and raised her head.

She froze.

Her eyes went to his left arm first. Everyone's did, and all his life they had always found the same thing there. Today they found a hand. Then they went to Amelia, to the red eyes and the small blood-red sygl glowing on her forehead, and back to the hand.

Hoping to forestall a lot of questions he wouldn't have any answers to, Bal quickly summarized what had happened.

"I saw the roof of the mausoleum flicker the other day," he said, "and I could see inside, and it isn't a mausoleum, it's a temple. So I enchanted my rug and flew over. And the roof isn't really there, so I went right through it, and she was inside." He tipped his head at Amelia. "This is Amelia. She's where blood sygls come from. Their originator. The rest of them locked her in there. It turns out I was born with her sygl, that's why my hand was taken off, and she gave me some of her blood and it grew back. And then we bonded. She's my familiar now. So she could get out."

He shut his mouth.

Bal couldn't tell what shocked Mrs. Brown more, the fact that he had a new hand or the fact that he now had the Vampire Goddess for a familiar. For a good while she kept opening and closing her mouth without any sound coming out, which made Bal think of a goldfish. He very carefully did not say so.

Finally she said, "Well then."

Then she sat back in her chair.

They stared at each other. Outside, someone shouted that it was a foul, and several people disagreed.

Then Mrs. Brown laughed.

It was a real laugh, sudden and helpless. Bal had heard her laugh before, at Christmas, at the younger ones' plays. He had not heard her laugh in this office before.

"You just don't do anything by half measures, do you?" she said.

Bal grinned.

"I can honestly say this was not how I pictured my day going." Then the grin slipped a little. He held out his new hand and showed her the palm, and the sygl glowing red in the center of it. "Is this going to be a problem, though? You know how the schools are about blood sygls."

Mrs. Brown studied the sygl. Then she picked up her pen again and tapped it on the desk, thinking.

"I know what you mean," she said slowly, "but they really don't have a choice, do they? I mean, not if she shows up." She pointed the pen at Amelia. "Once they call roll for any blood sygls, you'll stand. Then they'll ask for your originator to acknowledge you. Which is Amelia here." The pen tapped the desk once more. "If she did not, you would not be able to enroll in the school. But she will. So you can."

She dropped the pen on the desk and leaned toward them, her wooden chair creaking with the motion.

"They are not going to be happy you're there," she warned them, looking from one to the other. "Either of you. The only reason they have not outlawed blood sygls from attending is that they want to be seen as fair." Her mouth went thin. "That's probably why they locked her away, now that I think about it. It made it seem as though she had simply abandoned her followers."

Amelia nodded at that.

"It was that," she said, "and the fact they knew they could not win in a straight-up fight with me."

She said it without any heat at all, the same as she might have mentioned the weather. Then she tucked her hair behind an ear and looked over at Balthazaar, and her face changed.

"But I think it's time they see what a true blood mage can do," she said, smiling.

Bal nodded.

"Can you get me enrolled, then?" he asked Mrs. Brown. "I know I'll have to leave soon to get there in time. Plus I'll need the school supplies."

Mrs. Brown waved that off.

"Supplies are provided by the school for first years," she said. "You'll only need to take whatever personal belongings you want. They have their own uniforms and everything."

One problem fewer, then.

"Can I ask a question?" Amelia said.

Mrs. Brown sat back and gestured for her to go on.

"Why are you willing to help a blood mage," Amelia asked, "when most want to kill them, or at the least don't want to be involved with them?"

Mrs. Brown sighed. Her eyes drifted to the picture on her desk.

They stayed there. Bal had stood across this desk more times than he could remember, for broken windows and missed chores, and he had never seen her look at that frame before.

"Weak affinities sometimes don't show up until later in life," she said. "If at all. My husband's didn't show up until he was in his thirties. It was blood." She did not soften the word. "Once it did, the factory he was working for fired him. They said they didn't want to be seen as a blood mage supporter. After that he did what work he could find, but it was never enough. He ended up getting shot coming home from work one day."

She stopped there. Outside the window, the game went on.

"I've never been able to find out why," Mrs. Brown said, "but I've got my suspicions."

That was the whole of it. It had taken her less than a minute to tell. Bal stood there and looked at the man in the frame, a stranger with a narrow face and his collar done up to the top button, smiling at whoever had held the camera, and thought that it must have taken her a great deal longer than a minute to learn to tell it that short.

Then she looked away from the picture and back at them, and she was Mrs. Brown again, grey skirt and white blouse and the bun, the woman whose office this was.

"So I want to help Bal overcome this hatred, if I can," she said to Amelia. "With his sygl being visible the day he was born, I know his affinity must be strong. Otherwise they wouldn't have removed his hand that soon."

Bal's new hand closed into a fist at his side. He didn't tell it to. The fingers simply curled in on their own, hard enough that the nails bit into the sygl, and stayed there.

Amelia saw. She reached over and covered his fist with her own hand. Her palm was cool, and she held on.

"Don't worry," she told him softly. "They can't do it again."

Bal looked into her eyes. They were very red and very steady, and none of the teasing was in them now. He believed her. Under her hand his fingers came open one at a time, and she didn't let go.

"So what do I need to do?" he asked Mrs. Brown. The words came out rougher than he meant them to, and he cleared his throat.

"Nothing, except pack the few belongings you wish to take with you," Mrs. Brown said. "I need to reach out to the school, so you'll probably leave here in the morning." She looked at them both, and at their joined hands, and her eyebrows went up a fraction. "The fact that you have a familiar, not to mention that it's your own originator, will cause a stir as well. Most students don't get a familiar until it's introduced in their classes. I'll let the school know you have one, but I'll leave the details out." She glanced down at her letter, which in that office meant you were finished. "For now, it's best you run along and pack. I will send Pierre for you in the morning."

---

With that dismissal, Bal collected the rug off the chair in the hall and headed up to his room, and Amelia followed him.

The dormitory was on the second floor, a long room with the beds in two rows and a narrow locker at the foot of each. With everyone outside it was empty, and it smelled of clean sheets and somebody's forgotten oranges. Bal's bed was the third from the window on the left.

The first thing he did was unroll his rug and lay it back on the floorboards beside the bed, where it had always been. He straightened the corner with his foot. It looked like it had never gone anywhere.

Amelia came and stood beside it.

"You're leaving it?"

"It belongs to the orphanage," Bal said. "Somebody else can have it next."

As the majority of his belongings were not things of sentimental value, it did not take Bal long to pack. He slid his backpack off and emptied it onto the blanket. Out came the flashlight, the water bottle, *Sygldry Year 2*, and both cheese sandwiches squashed flat in their napkin, which he had forgotten all about. Then he knelt in front of his locker. Most of it he left where it was: shirts with the cuffs gone, comics everyone had read, a jar of marbles. A school would not need his marbles.

What he really wanted to take didn't amount to much. A few books. The compass he'd been given for his birthday, brass and dented, with a needle that still swung true. A pocketknife. It was a good pocketknife and he liked it. And, wrapped in a dish towel at the very back of the locker, his rune engraving kit.

It was a flat wooden case with a brass catch and his first scratchy attempts still inside. He'd bought it with his earnings from working in the kitchen. It had cost him most of a year of peeling potatoes and scrubbing pans after supper, and he did not regret a single pan of it. He wrapped it back up and fitted it into the bottom of the pack where nothing could knock against it.

Behind him the bedsprings creaked. When he turned around, Amelia had swiped one of his spell books off the top of the pile and was lying back on his bed with her ankles crossed and her hair spread across his pillow, reading.

The books themselves were old. Their covers were soft at the corners, and one had a ring on the front from a mug of cocoa. The school was going to provide him with new ones. Bal packed these anyway. Fredrick, the boy who had given them to him, had been a friend, and he didn't want to leave them behind.

"You know," Amelia commented, turning a page, "most of the stuff they teach first years doesn't seem that difficult."

Bal rolled his eyes.

"That's because it's for first years." He took *Sygldry Year 2* from the pile and held it up so she could see the cover. "The spell I used on the carpet isn't taught until the second year."

Amelia peered at him over the top of the book. Only her eyes showed above it, red and laughing.

"Oh, so you're an overachiever, huh?"

It felt a little odd, being teased like this by the originator of an entire sygl. But she was lying on his bed with her feet on his blanket, making fun of his reading, and Bal had to admit she seemed more like a regular girl than anything out of the stories. A regular girl who could make benches out of thin air. But still.

"I like to read," Bal said, and shrugged. He packed *Sygldry Year 2* with the others. "Just think, if I hadn't seen that flicker, or found that spell, I would never have found you."

Amelia lowered the book. For a little while she didn't say anything. Then she closed it, suddenly serious, set it aside on the blanket, and sat up.

"I should warn you," she said. "The others are not going to like you being at the school. It's not just the students and the teachers. I'm talking about the other originators." She drew her knees up under the black dress. "They cannot interfere with you directly. But others can."

Bal buckled the pack shut and came to sit beside her on the edge of the bed. The springs dipped and tipped them a little closer together.

"Why did they lock you away in the first place?" he asked.

Amelia didn't answer straight away.

"Do you know the reason blood mages are so dangerous?" she countered.

Bal thought about all the stories told in the dark in this very room, and how little was in any of them once you took out the screaming.

"Not really," he admitted. "I know that some of their magic has to do with their own blood. But other than that, no."

"I'm not really surprised that's all you know." Amelia studied her hands in her lap. "You see, blood mages are so dangerous because we can alter people's sygls."

Bal opened his mouth.

"Not without them wanting it," she went on quickly. "Never that. But it can be done. The other originators were shocked when this came out. Honestly, it was a closely guarded secret at one point. But Raizen, the storm originator, got one of his followers to kidnap a high priest of mine." Her voice stayed even, but her fingers had tightened on each other. "The other originators had grown concerned that some of their followers were disappearing from their sight. So Raizen's high priest captured one of mine, and tortured him, to find out what was going on."

"Truthfully, I was more concerned with other things at the time," Amelia said. "I didn't realize that one of my high priests had gone on a converting crusade. The others didn't believe that I'd had nothing to do with it." She lifted one shoulder. "To be fair, if I had known, I would have been okay with it. The only reason converting bothers the others is that when someone converts, they don't lose the inherited gifts from their old sygl."

A dozen questions jostled up at once, and he went with the one that had snagged.

"Inherited gifts?" he asked, curious.

Amelia grinned at him. The seriousness went out of her face as fast as it had come.

"Don't worry," she said. "You'll find out at the ceremony."

"That's not fair. You can't just..."

"Now," Amelia said over him, "I'm hungry. Let's go get something to eat."

Bal's eyes widened. He was suddenly very aware of the side of his neck.

Amelia rolled hers.

"*Normal* food. I can survive off it, but I do need blood to really thrive." She swung her legs off the bed and stood, caught both his hands, and hauled. "Come on, Master. I've heard a great deal about this cook of yours. It's time I tasted the evidence."

Bal let himself be pulled up off the bed. "You'll be sorry."

"I've been locked in a temple for three thousand years," Amelia said. "I doubt it."

She towed him toward the door in search of sustenance. Bal looked back once from the doorway. His rug lay straight on the floorboards beside the bed, and at the foot of it sat his backpack, buckled and full and leaning against the locker, waiting for the morning.
